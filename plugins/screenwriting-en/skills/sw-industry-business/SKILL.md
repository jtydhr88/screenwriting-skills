# Industry & Business (行业与生意经)

The operational logic and survival strategies of the American screenwriting industry. Core consensus: **"It is a business, not a fairy tale"** (David Diamond and David Weissman); "The writing of screenplays is not about the movie business, but the movie business is about the writing of screenplays" (Richard Walter, Principle 56); "The cleverest marketing strategy is a good script" (Principle 62). This skill covers: vetting ideas, loglines and pitches, submissions and representation, contracts and credits, notes and rewrites, film vs. television, and professional mindset. For writing workflow and formatting, see `sw-format-adaptation`. For the full self-check checklist, sample query letters, and contract terminology, see [reference.md](reference.md).

## 1. Writing from the Buyer's Perspective (Diamond & Weissman)

- Submitting a script must offer value to every person along the chain—every link in the pipeline must be able to say, "This is good for me" (agent -> producer -> director -> executive); "It is the screenwriter who shoulders the burden of helping everyone else achieve their dreams."
- **Movie Idea != Screenplay Idea**: A real-life event in itself is not a movie idea. **The Three Cs**: Character, Concept, Context ("This person, in this situation, within a specific environment... until they do the right thing"); the concept of *Groundhog Day* could be made as sci-fi, horror, or a thriller, but adding a "disgruntled weatherman" -> comedy, adding "Groundhog Day" -> specific tone.
- **Testing the Idea**: Do not write an outline first; pitch it verbally to trusted listeners and gauge their reaction: is it "**I have to have this**" (launches a career), "Okay, I get it" (promising but common, will not pay the bills), or "I do not understand" (back to the drawing board). The food court epiphany: "What would our movie look like if Disney were making it?"—examine it from the top down through the studio's lens.
- Blockbuster (larger than life) vs. Indie (execution-dependent); "Is this idea right for me?"—write the genre you love watching most; list multiple concepts first (pick 1 out of 16).
- **Comparable Films List**: Same genre / similar protagonist / similar engine, listing both great and terrible films; "originality for the sake of originality earns no points"—what earns points is respecting genre conventions while delivering fresh originality on top of what came before.
- **The One-Page Nine-Step Treatment** (does the reader's job for them, "making the reader look like a hero"): Setup -> Opportunity/Challenge -> Act One Turn -> Page 45 Twist -> Midpoint Reversal -> Page 75 Escalation -> Low Point at End of Act Two -> Even Worse Final Test -> Climax/Set Piece. Clarify the film's **central argument** (theme = the belief system that triumphs in the debate).
- **Character Action Tracker**: Recount the nine steps entirely from each character's point of view (Han Solo appears at minute 45 purely for the cash -> "How much money?" -> takes the cash and leaves -> swoops back in at the critical moment); "Can you tell the entire story from every character's individual point of view?"
- **Set Pieces** = "Trailer moments," one in Act One, one in Act Three, and multiple throughout Act Two; "What happens in the second act? Where are the set pieces?!"—a draft without set pieces might sell, but one with set pieces lets executives "see the movie right before their eyes."
- **Submissions**: "Never lose the reader's interest" is the cardinal rule; open with something distinctive; register with the WGA; rely on personal relationships (entry-level Hollywood jobs, peer collectives of aspiring writers, start with friends, then one producer, one executive, then agents); contests and The Black List are supplementary; never pay someone to read your script; "Representatives appear on their own the moment you have a marketable script."
- **Notes and Feedback**: If multiple readers agree, make the change; distinguish the diagnosis from the prescription ("It is an antique car, gentlemen"); use the one-page outline and character tracker as your yardstick; "Following bad notes is one of the most common mistakes screenwriters make," but ignoring them altogether is just as disastrous; "Getting fired means you already got the job in the first place."
- **Resilience**: 15 movies produced out of 47 projects (a .319 batting average); "As writers, the only things within our control are what we write and how we write it"; learn at least one craft lesson from every project.

## 2. Idea Vetting: The PROBLEM Seven Elements (Bork)

- The three common flaws of rejected scripts: lack of a compelling core idea, lack of emotional resonance, or lack of an entertaining experience. **60/30/10 Rule**: 60% of a script's success depends on the core idea (conveyable in a one-page summary), 30% on structure, and 10% on scene-level execution. Before typing FADE IN, draft the concept into a one- or two-sentence logline plus a one-page summary to gather objective feedback.
- **Three Logline Essentials**: Protagonist identity and relatability / Inciting catalyst / The nature and grueling difficulty of the mission (the most critical); never describe internal character arcs.
- **P - Punishing**: Story = protagonist + tree + rocks; out of 300 purchased scripts, only 8 received a recommend, with frequent critiques like "the story starts too late / scenes lack meaningful conflict / the stakes are inconsequential"; seven hallmarks of the "perfect arena"; when adapting true events, isolate "the big problem" and the "perilous process."
- **R - Relatability**: A single protagonist present in nearly every scene, never passive; "how they treat other people" drives audience affection; their flaw = "not living their best life" rather than "being maliciously mean"; spend the first ten pages establishing the status quo before unleashing the catalyst; eight core categories of dramatic problems; an antihero must confront an overwhelmingly massive mess (Walter White starts out as the most sympathetic man imaginable).
- **O - Originality**: A familiar genre + one key subverted element (James Bond -> Jason Bourne -> *Mr. & Mrs. Smith* -> *Spy Kids*) + the writer's voice; study competing projects directly; "buyers value novelty far less than writers and critics do."
- **B - Believability**: Clarify the rules of the world within the first 10-15%, then strictly obey "what a real human being would actually do"; simple concepts beat convoluted mechanics; the antagonist's motivation must remain recognizably human and logical.
- **L - Life-Altering**: External stakes come first; the eight-tier stakes hierarchy (Survival -> Securing Justice -> Freedom -> Returning Home / Preserving the Family -> The Ultimate Life Partner -> Career Viability -> A Life-Changing Prize -> A Shot at Genuine Happiness); once life-or-death stakes are introduced, they must persist to the very end; seven invalid pseudo-stakes (career success, purely internal epiphanies, vague military skirmishes, fuzzy happiness, isolated decisions, an unlikable jerk turning slightly nicer).
- **E - Entertaining**: Ten primary emotional payoffs; any "drama" without life-or-death stakes must layer in at least two distinct entertainment elements (humor, wealth and prestige, high-end environments, betrayal and manipulation, music, spectacle events, sex and rivalry, outrageous characters, exotic worlds, uplifting love and family); "sheer entertainment value is the ultimate trump card."
- **M - Meaningful**: Theme represents competing values with no easy answers, carried by the impact character / B-story; let the audience feel it rather than be lectured on it.
- Television procedurals are restricted to heroic, high-stakes, highly entertaining professions (police, doctors, lawyers, and their variants); Levitan's "two characters on a line" method builds conflict webs; characters require a credible mechanism that forces them into ongoing proximity; episodic sub-problems can resolve, but the core overarching problem and "unmet need" must never be resolved.
- Sourcing ideas: brainstorm parallel lists and cross-pollinate them ("Aliens + Little League baseball?"), 15 minutes daily, focusing on raw output before filtering; "talent is almost entirely a matter of attitude and practice."

## 3. Loglines, Genres, and Pitching (Snyder / Hicks / Hoxter / Masterclass)

- Snyder's four elements (ironic hook + mental picture + audience and cost + strong title) plus protagonist adjective, antagonist adjective, and primal goal; "Anyone standing still is someone I can pitch to"; high concept never dies (the international market accounts for 60%); never say "it is X meets Y" during a formal pitch; position within the ten signature story genres.
- Hicks on pitching: distill the entire screenplay down to a few lines ("A lone cop trapped inside a skyscraper seized by terrorists"); three justifications (executives hear dozens or hundreds of pitches every week; cinema spreads by word of mouth; producers are probing the writer's temperament); "this industry is built on the word 'no'"; preparation: evoke the atmosphere and mood, clarify the protagonist, capture the root conflict, craft the logline ("A supply wagon train in outer space"); speak conversationally in the meeting instead of reading from paper, do not mechanically memorize, read the room's eye contact, keep it brief.
- Hoxter terminology: logline != tagline ("They are young, they are in love, they kill people" is post-release marketing); treatment is a prose sales tool; elevator pitch <= 30 seconds; coverage is the initial reader's report archived on record that impacts your next script; bake-off is a competitive pitch contest between writers; gross vs. net (net profit is "something you will never see"); the Minimum Basic Agreement (MBA) is renegotiated every three years; "&" denotes a writing team, while "and" indicates separate sequential writers.
- Masterclass (Richards): "You caught a tiger, but you are petting it like a kitten"; include a one-sentence logline plus two or three comp titles inside the script deck ("*The Godfather* meets *Furious 7*"); has written over 150 treatments; introduce a two-hour film in one minute; Banks: answer the twelve beat questions within five minutes, pitch archetypes rather than granular mechanics, "strive for clarity rather than exhaustive completeness"; if you are four and a half minutes in and still explaining the backstory, "the investor has already answered their phone."
- Walter: **Never send treatments to producers** (if a script and a treatment are submitted together, the producer reads only the treatment; treatments for unwritten screenplays are dismissed); if a query letter prompts a request for a synopsis, keep it under one double-spaced page and write it like a movie trailer.

## 4. Market Entities and the Six Criteria (Hicks)

- **The Four Entities**: Producers (making wagers while hedging against risk; a single project can pass through 12 or more screenwriters); Directors (the first hired after the script is locked, always finding reasons for further rewrites); Stars ("you are writing for movie stars and prospective movie stars"); The General Public ("they are not like you; they are like you"). All four desire the exact same thing: a compelling story.
- **Six Fundamental Criteria**: An unquestioned, single protagonist; a clear problem and external goal; a vivid, focused antagonist; a protagonist forced into decisive action under a ticking clock; core values entangled in the crisis, fundamentally transformed by the end; emotional propulsion prioritized over intellectual cleverness.
- The portrait of Sparky the dog fable: only the original painter's signature carries supreme value, "an absurdity in portrait painting, yet completely standard and rational in Hollywood."
- **Once a screenwriter sells a script to a producer, it becomes the producer's property**; average studio budget sits at $60 million; "what gets made is not the movie producers wish to make, but the movie they are capable of finishing"; the true customer is "the person wearing a suit to work," whose sole inquiry is "How much will it cost?"
- Script coverage: readers and story analysts produce a 1-2 page summary evaluating concept, characters, craft, and budget; executives read only the coverage summary; most readers are sharp, dedicated professionals.
- The WGA registers more than 40,000 scripts every year; Hollywood releases only around 300 films annually.

## 5. Representation and Submissions (Walter / Hicks / Diamond / Henson)

- **What an Agent Is Not** (Hicks): Not an employment bureau ("you yourself are the primary salesperson"), not a babysitter, not a banker; **What an Agent Is**: A business professional (taking a 10% commission), a team member, a corporate partner ("Take it up with my agent!"), a stamp of legitimacy (most companies reject unsolicited materials).
- **The Query Letter** is the single viable pathway: Paragraph one hooks curiosity with a minimal premise logline; paragraph two details personal background; asks if they are open to reading; includes a stamped return postcard; target two or three agencies initially; **no legitimate literary agent ever charges a 'reading fee.'** Walter's experiment: query letters from four student classes sent to WGA-signatory agencies yielded a **96% agreement-to-read rate**; template: "I am an MFA graduate student in screenwriting at UCLA. I have written a feature screenplay titled *Ghost Family*, an action/adventure set in contemporary New York and 9th-century Scotland. May I send you the script for your consideration?"—**do not attach the script, do not attach an outline**, say as little as possible; address it to a specific agent by name (call the front desk switchboard to get names); do not call, do not request delivery receipts, do not use gimmicks (fortune cookies, carrier pigeons); a traditional paper letter stands out on a desk (Principle 65).
- **Do Not Use the Phone**: Agents ignore most phone calls yet reply to all mail; never call to ask if they received the script; never call to say "do not read that draft I just sent, I revised it"; on voicemail, state your name and phone number first.
- **Breaking into Television** (Walter): Send an appreciative fan letter directly to a staff writer on the series (routed care of the WGA membership office), praising specific creative merits, inquiring about their writing routine, **without claiming to be a writer yourself and without asking for representation referrals**—Principle 63 (every successful writer began completely unknown), Principle 64 (every writer is hunting for excuses to evade the blank page in front of them); establish genuine rapport before ever floating your own script.
- Agency commissions are statutory at 10% (deducted strictly from money earned, never prepaid upfront); standard 90-day clauses; verbal agreements suffice; **sign release forms without hesitation** (they carry little practical downside); new writers should target hungry boutique agencies or "the newest young agent at an established agency"; beware the "revolving agency roster" trap; "treat kindly those who lent a hand while your career was on the rise."
- Henson: Complete at least three or four polished feature screenplays before seeking representation ("a store needs a full inventory of goods"); professional rigor is the sole metric: "I may be an amateur actor, but my performance cannot be amateurish"; master your screenplay completely and rehearse a tight one-minute phone pitch; the path from page to screen routinely spans over 3 years (*E.T.* took 8 years, *Forrest Gump* took 10).
- Snyder: Contact hierarchy in descending order: face-to-face > phone call > personal query letter > email; after every rejection, ask "Is there anyone else you would recommend I send this to?"; the first buyer is the most crucial, accept modest money to get in the game; steer clear of contests without verified industry judges; high-profile novelty pitch stunts (synchronized alarm clocks, hazardous radioactive script canisters) yielded $1 million sales.
- Gatekeeper theory: Receptionists, assistants, and coverage readers wield authority far exceeding their job titles; "turn them into your greatest allies."

## 6. Contracts, Credits, and Money (Hicks / Walter / Masterclass)

- **The Option**: An independent producer secures exclusive development rights for roughly 10% of the projected purchase price, using that window to raise funding and attach talent and directors; upon expiration, rights revert to the writer while the option money is retained; **never sign an option on your own**: always run it past an agent or entertainment attorney; insist on including the clause: "**The writer must be recognized as a professional screenwriter subject to the WGA Minimum Basic Agreement (MBA).**" Walter: option deposits provide massive advantages (promotes your profile, generates recurring option renewals, preserves underlying copyright); a script that gets passed from hand to hand becomes "discount merchandise"; insist on automatic reversion clauses (rights revert if unproduced after seven years, subject to repayment).
- **Development**: The most common outcome is hiring an experienced veteran to rewrite your draft, where you collect your full fee but step aside; or milestone disbursements paid out upon the start of principal photography.
- **Credit Arbitration**: Determined entirely by the WGA, independent of company influence; a second writer must contribute over 50% of the material to secure a screenplay credit; four primary designations: Written by / Story by / Screen Story by / Screenplay by; "George & Martha" denotes a collaborative writing team, while "George and Martha" denotes separate sequential contributions; writing the initial original spec guarantees at least a Story by credit; **arbitration demands comprehensive supporting materials** (Walter's *American Graffiti* lesson: submitting only drafts without comparative analysis memos cost him credit); ten creative rights guarantees (the original writer retains the first rewrite right, must be consulted on subsequent revisions, must be granted a meeting with the director, must be invited to the first cast-and-crew screening).
- **Money**: Studio contracts routinely award 5% of net profits—a hallmark of creative accounting; **you will never see net profit, earning only 5% of absolute zero**; if offered gross points, accept instantly; sign cash-on-delivery contracts and sell to the highest bidder; track MBA scale rates; separate underlying rights (novel, stage play, sequels) and negotiate character, merchandising, and ancillary rights independently; work-for-hire assignments forfeit underlying copyright ownership; **step deals** feature guaranteed minimum payouts at every stage; production bonuses; deferred compensation for beginners equates to "working entirely for free." "Do you need money to pay rent, or do you need your name in the end credits?"
- Walter: Fight for the highest possible fee ("money is a metaphor with numbers on it, signifying respect"); **never write for free, never work on projects where you do not own the rights** (pencils do not move themselves; "a writer should never suffer the indignity of begging a producer for compensation"; the more a producer pays, the more they value the script); leave financial disputes to your representative; while actively drafting, purge thoughts of contracts, copyright, and backend points from your mind.
- Copyright: Protection attaches automatically upon completion; formal registration is required to pursue statutory damages; WGA registry costs $20 and is open to non-members; raw ideas cannot be copyrighted (the Darwin/Wallace dynamic); "never succumb to paranoia"; "worry about character, dialogue, and storytelling; do not obsess over plagiarism."
- True-life stories: Consult an attorney first and secure a signed Life Story Rights Option Agreement, including from close family members.
- The Guild: Join after selling your first script or landing your first guild-covered writing contract; initiation fee is around $2,500; "writing an undeniable script is the only path into the guild."
- **Packaging**: The screenplay serves as the foundational vehicle; bankable elements (stars, bankable directors) exist not simply to attract audiences, but to persuade financiers to unlock capital and give studio executives cover; **Principle 66: The single defining factor in box-office success is word of mouth**.
- Masterclass (Stentz): Writing credit designations: "with" (collaboration) vs. "and" (sequential rewrite); three anonymous WGA arbitration judges; "I am not the boss and you are not the boss: the story is the boss"; screenwriters do not retain film copyright, whereas novelists keep underlying rights; screenwriters receive WGA pension and health benefits.

## 7. Notes and Rewrites (Multiple Sources)

- Walter's "Yes, but..." honest negotiation strategy ("Yes, we could relocate the setting to Latvia, but here is everything we sacrifice"); the fire hydrant effect (every junior executive assistant feels compelled to leave their mark); "Producers subconsciously expect writers to defend their creative choices... are you an artist or a stenographer?"; nod along when executives say "strengthen the protagonist"; the red dress / green dress syndrome and the 5% illusion are self-destructive traps; "leave your rage inside the screenplay"; respond to rejections with a simple "Thank you for your time."
- Masterclass (Richards): Assemble a trusted 4-5 person "brutal feedback circle" where you take notes without being defensive, asking clarifying questions; "Your plane never took off" = there is no real narrative break between Act One and Act Two; "Something bizarre needs to happen in Act Three" = the protagonist's personality is too flat; if multiple readers give wildly divergent, contradictory notes, you have a structural core problem; "When Act Three fails, the defect always lives in Act One"; yield to the director once production begins.
- Banks: "Preserve every sharp edge from your original spark; trim the eccentricities so they fit inside the client's corporate box, but never flatten the thematic layers of the story"; "Bad notes can spark brilliant solutions" (adding a pet dog to a ghost-baseball script -> only the kid and the dog can see the ghosts); **the screenwriters who refused notes went broke** (a dingy apartment on Hollywood Boulevard vs. a home in Beverly Hills); it takes capital to say "no" (at least 5 hit movies).
- Hicks: Critique yourself thoroughly before critiquing your collaborators; show your draft to two friends guaranteed to tell the truth; the first industry professional to evaluate your script should be a paid consultant, not an agent.
- Henson: Distinguish nitpicking collaborators (take it as casual feedback) from consistent structural critiques (face them and rewrite); if five out of six readers love it, you are on solid ground.
- Mei Feng on *Mildred Pierce*: Producers dictate the script's trajectory (adding flashbacks, inserting a murder, excising the singing career) by commissioning detailed outlines before assigning top screenwriters, hiring script doctors to write 62-page notes, and replacing writers frequently.

## 8. Film vs. Television (Bork / Stentz / Walter / Diamond)

- Television is a "series," not a self-contained narrative with a definitive ending: each episode resolves minor tactical problems, but the overarching core conflict and "unmet need" remain unresolved; never mistake internal character growth for a series engine; "Film is a director's medium, television is a writer's medium" ("he who controls the budget calls the shots").
- Stentz: Four serialized storytelling models (anthology / procedural episodic / season-contained arcs / continuous serialized drama); end every episode on a cliffhanger; bottle episodes save production cash for explosive season finales ("conflict is cheap, suspense is cheap, yet audiences love both"); show bible and style guides maintain consistency across rotating directors; TV episodes shoot in 8-14 days at $3-$8 million each; television is character-driven while film is concept-driven (*Ex Machina* vs. *Westworld*); "If you need a ten-page rewrite overnight, never hire a feature screenwriter"; development shifts: platforms now order full seasons directly or commission three to four episodes up front.
- Walter: Television pays most handsomely (residuals "keep flowing for the rest of your life"); television treats writers with greater authority (a second story conference requires an extra $10,000 fee); premium cable shows far more creative daring than mainstream studio features; however, freelance staff writers have vanished (replaced by full-time staff employees); writing for television requires living in Los Angeles.
- Bork: One-hour TV dramas without life-or-death stakes or procedural machinery must be relentlessly "wealthy, sexy, and glamorous"; the three golden professions of drama series (cops, doctors, lawyers).
- Diamond: The spec script market has contracted dramatically (173 specs sold in 1995 vs. 55 in 2010), yet it remains the ultimate calling card for emerging talent; the intellectual property (IP) paradox (studios crave pre-existing IP, yet scour unknown screenwriters for fresh original voices to hire for franchise sequels).

## 9. Oscar and Market Realities (Mei Feng)

- Screenplay categories are more progressive than Best Picture, forecasting future industry trends; the Academy favors social realism, projects balancing historical weight with humor, low-budget high-yield productions, and innovative storytelling within recognizable classic frameworks; approach horror, broad comedy, and ensemble experimental structures with extreme care; campaign expenditures ($774,000 spent by DreamWorks) directly influence screenplay voting.
- The Indie Breakthrough year (1996 marked the first year without a major studio nominee in Best Picture); "A studio deciding which script is exceptional is entirely different from greenlighting that script for production"; Hollywood remains far more conservative regarding screenplays than visual aesthetics; for idiosyncratic Charlie Kaufman-style material, "Hollywood has yet to establish a systematic framework to package and sell it."
- A three-way power struggle (writer, director, studio) shapes the final cut (*Good Will Hunting* stripped away gritty realistic subplots to satisfy mainstream expectations).
- Hitchcock's copyright economics: treat every intellectual property acquisition as a financial puzzle to minimize costs and turn a profit; use blind intermediaries to prevent celebrity status from inflating rights prices.

## 10. Professional Mindset and Habits

- Walter: Chasing industry trends guarantees you arrive too late (Principle 60); the most perilous strategy is playing it safe (Principle 61); writing a screenplay solely hoping it sells leads to heartbreak: "A writer must write for the pure sake of writing"; refuse to adopt a victim mentality, write continuously through dry spells and build financial reserves; "Be grateful even for being mistreated by this industry, because being completely ignored is vastly worse"; allow yourself a few days of despair after a rejection, but remember: "It only takes one yes" (*Platoon* was shopped for ten years); having your work rewritten by others is an occupational privilege; "Art is pain"; nurture two distinct identities (the artist and the working professional); learn to see the world through the minds of your agent and producer.
- Diamond: "Haste makes waste" (a screenwriting factory generated eight scripts, none of which sold); glean at least one lasting craft takeaway from every single script.
- Bork: "Stop agonizing over whether you possess talent; eliminate the question from your mind entirely."
- Henson: "Writing a script is easy; selling it is hard"; cultivate adaptability and passion; treat screenwriting as your calling, and leave financial worries for later.
- Masterclass: Richards: "You must have skin thick enough to endure brutal feedback, yet remain sensitive enough to create genuine art"; "Making movies is a war waged against people who do not care about art"; Banks: "No matter how many times they break your heart, treat them like your first love"; Stentz: "It took me twenty years to become an overnight success"; "The hallmark of a professional writer is the ability to convince others to spend money on your vision" (avoid financing underlying rights out of pocket).
- Field: "An overnight success takes fifteen years"; a director can take a brilliant script and direct a terrible movie, but no director can take a terrible script and direct a masterpiece.

## 11. Checklists

**Pre-Draft Idea Vetting**
1. Are the Three Cs fully established? Did a verbal pitch to three people elicit an enthusiastic "I must have this"?
2. Has the concept cleared all seven PROBLEM checkpoints? Are high stakes matched with genuine entertainment value?
3. Are the three logline essentials (Protagonist / Catalyst / Mission) locked down? Are they paired with two or three reference comps?
4. Is this a high-concept blockbuster or an execution-dependent indie? Is it tailored to my strengths? Is the comparable films list compiled?

**Pre-Submission**
5. Do you have at least three or four polished feature screenplays ready? Is the script registered with the WGA? Is the cover page stripped down to clean PDF essentials?
6. Is the query letter concise, free of attachments or treatments, and addressed to a specific agent by name? Are you querying only two or three targets at first?
7. Have you practiced your one-minute verbal pitch until it flows naturally?
8. If targeting television: have you reached out with an authentic appreciation letter to a staff writer on the show?

**Pre-Contract**
9. Has an entertainment attorney vetted the option agreement? Is the MBA signatory clause included?
10. Are you declining net profit points in favor of gross proceeds, guaranteed cash, and step deals? Are ancillary rights negotiated independently?
11. Are you writing on spec for free or doing unpaid work on material you do not own?
12. Have you assembled all supporting documentation and revision memos for credit arbitration?

**Receiving Notes**
13. What notes are shared across multiple readers? Does the note highlight a genuine diagnosis or just a clunky fix? Have you evaluated the note against the one-page outline and character tracker?
14. Is your "Yes, but..." creative defense ready? Have you defended the story's essential voice and distinctive edges?
15. If rewritten or replaced: is your original sample preserved, and have you already begun developing the next concept?

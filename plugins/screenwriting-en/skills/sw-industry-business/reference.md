## I. Bork's Complete PROBLEM Seven-Element Self-Checklist

**Punishing**: (1) A major problem requiring the entire story to resolve (one that television could never solve); (2) The goal is difficult but not impossible; (3) In the course of actively solving it, complications push abilities to the absolute limit; (4) Actions lead to unintended consequences, complexities, and conflicts requiring further action; (5) The problem escalates through the middle until it seems hopeless, solvable only through a climactic final showdown.
Seven qualities of the "perfect contest": an exceptionally difficult opponent evenly matched; every player has a backstory; the stakes could not be higher; the home team takes no early lead and instead suffers unexpected setbacks; rallying from crisis through passion and grit, where most efforts fail or worsen the situation; falling noticeably behind and seemingly without hope near the end, whereupon a new plan emerges though the goal remains daunting; rising from behind to stand triumphant at the final moment.

**Relatable**: (1) The protagonist easily wins favor, ensnared in a problem that invites sympathy while remaining entertaining throughout; (2) External life problems resonate on the most fundamental, universal human level; (3) Thoughts, feelings, and desires are sufficiently clear to be shared; (4) Every plotline has a specific outcome the audience roots for and feels invested in; (5) Involves growth, change, and complete transformation, rather than a simplistic shift from selfishness to selflessness.
Protagonist flaw checklist: Luke lacks faith in himself and the Force; Josh cannot accept living like an ordinary kid; George dreams of leaving Bedford Falls while blind to the happiness he already holds; Tess lacks self-confidence; Rick believes in nothing; Riggs has a death wish; Elle sequesters herself as a sorority princess; Ray has not reconciled with his late father; William craves validation from rock stars—the common thread: likable individuals stubbornly clinging to a niche lifestyle that has not panned out. Successful protagonists without transformational arcs: *Four Weddings and a Funeral*, *Coming to America*, *Stand and Deliver*, *Get Out*, *The Bourne Identity*, *There's Something About Mary*, *Say Anything...*, *Die Hard*, *Rear Window*, *Erin Brockovich*.

**Original**: (1) Follows the basic conventions of a viable genre while injecting fresh, intriguing elements; (2) The central dramatic situation presents an easily grasped yet tantalizing hook; (3) A deep passion for elements within the work forms a distinct "voice"; (4) The concept offers clear points of departure compared to similar well-known projects; (5) Primary characters are specific and vivid. Multiple similar projects in the same market: 1998's *Deep Impact* and *Armageddon*; 1988's *18 Again!*, *Like Father Like Son*, and *Big*; 1992's two Columbus projects.

**Believable**: (1) Audiences grasp the premise from a logline and choose to watch without hesitation; (2) Character traits, decisions, and behaviors feel authentic under the given circumstances; (3) Setting and "rules of engagement" are clarified from the opening; (4) Everyone's motivation is distinct and relatable on a human level; (5) The entertaining suspense could genuinely happen.

**Life-Altering**: (1) Stakes match one of the eight levels; (2) Problems, goals, and actions focus predominantly on external life; (3) Stakes persist across the entire story; (4) Negative consequences of failure grow increasingly personal and pronounced; (5) A once-in-a-lifetime challenge forces fundamental transformation.

**Entertaining**: (1) Crystal-clear on the precise emotional entertainment delivered to the audience; (2) Provides extraordinary value for genre fans; (3) A larger-than-life quality transforms the story into an escapist playground; (4) Focuses on fundamental, universal emotional drivers; (5) Continual delivery of surprise, curiosity, and delight. Ten core emotions: amusement/laughter, fear, fascination, shock, desire, excitement, awe, romantic love, resonance, and eager anticipation.

**Meaningful**: (1) Explores universally significant questions with no easy answers; (2) Characters are propelled by fundamental unmet desires; (3) The final act crystallizes character change, validating the journey; (4) Leaves a distinctly positive imprint on the audience's life; (5) Avoids heavy-handed thematic messaging, revealing growth subtly through an engaging plot.

**The Levitan Method** (Television Conflict Web): Pick any two characters -> Consider their basic life situations -> Draw a line between them representing their dynamic ("that line is the acting") -> Ask what happens when they share a scene, how their interactions spark conflict, and how that reveals their respective overarching problems -> Select another pair and repeat. Ten characters in *Modern Family* paired off (Manny infatuated with his beautiful older step-sister; Phil desperate to impress an in-law who never respects him).

## II. Walter's Three Sample Query Letters

**Sample 1**: "Dear Mr. Lasfargues: I am a graduate student in the MFA screenwriting program at UCLA. I have written a feature screenplay entitled *Ghost Family*, an action/adventure story set in contemporary New York and 9th-century Scotland. May I send you the script for your consideration? Sincerely, [Name]"—First paragraph introduces oneself in a single sentence; second paragraph covers only premise and genre; sent to multiple legitimate agencies with positive responses from every one, eventually selling to Fox for four million dollars.

**Sample 2**: "Dear Mr. Lasfargues: I am a probation officer in Dayton, Ohio, and I have written a screenplay entitled *Long Shot*. It is a gentle comedy about an AWOL sailor searching for his long-lost father. May I send you the script for your consideration?"—"Inexperienced writers are far too eager to tell everyone what their script is about. You should tease the agent and awaken the desire to know more. The less said, the better."

**Sample 3**: "Dear Mr. Lasfargues: On January 4, 2004, I walked into my apartment and found my fiancée making love to my roommate. She was handcuffed to the headboard, her chest smeared with honey. Unfortunately, discovering them led to my arrest and imprisonment. Six years later, I have still not escaped the shadow of that case. But you know, what people say about lemons and lemonade isn't quite the same thing."—Unearthing the irresistible hook of unique personal experience, delivered in an old-fashioned letter of two or three sentences.

**Rules**: Do not attach the script or a synopsis (if a summary is requested, keep it under one page, double-spaced, written like a trailer); address a specific agent by name (the switchboard fishing technique: "May I speak with Mr. Barry Stein?"—"We don't have a Barry Stein here, but we do have Hal Steinberg"); do not call, do not demand return receipts, do not use gimmicks; once agreed, send the script with a brief cover note stating it is submitted by invitation; yielded a 96% read rate in testing.

**Hicks Query Letter**: First paragraph offers an intriguing, minimalist premise summary ("The story of a young man who hacks into the Department of Defense computer with a home computer, nearly triggering World War III"); second paragraph details personal background (highlight specific domain expertise rather than an exhaustive résumé); inquire about interest in reading with a promise to send via courier; enclose a self-addressed stamped postcard: "Yes, I am interested in reading your screenplay. ____ Signature"; wait six to eight weeks, sending a polite follow-up after two months without a response; target two to three agencies at a time initially.

**Television Fan Letter Technique** (Walter): Praise ("Your *Show Name* changed my life") -> Specific highlight ("When she said... and he replied... I nearly fell out of my recliner; the remote dropped and my schnauzer grabbed it immediately") -> Do not identify as a writer or ask for agent recommendations -> Inquire about writing habits ("Do you write with a pencil on yellow legal pads or on a computer?"); only after rapport is established, venture: "Inspired by your work, I wrote a spec episode; I kept quiet earlier to avoid any misunderstanding..."

## III. Hicks: Market and Contract Essentials

- **Four-Step Pitch**: Establish mood and tone; clearly define the protagonist (metaphorical highlights rather than tedious physical descriptions); capture the core conflict (the dilemma of the first ten minutes); craft a captivating logline ("A wagon train in space"). In meetings: speak instead of read, never memorize mechanically, watch their eyes, pitch as a duo like a sports broadcast (one handles play-by-play, the other provides color commentary), keep it brief.
- **Option Agreement** mandatory clause: "For the purposes of this agreement, the Writer must be recognized as a professional writer and covered under the provisions of the Writers Guild of America Minimum Basic Agreement." Fee typically around 10% of the purchase price; script cannot be shopped elsewhere during the term; rights revert upon expiration.
- **Development Phase**: Ideal outcome is the company providing development funding while the producer hires you for rewrites; most likely scenario is hiring a more experienced writer to rewrite, leaving you with full payment upon exit; or structured installments upon commencement of principal photography.
- **Four Credit Forms**: Written by (one person creates story and screenplay); Story by (story credit only); Screen Story by (source material derived from newspaper/magazine articles or other material); Screenplay by (wrote the final draft based on earlier material by others); "&" denotes a writing team, "and" denotes separate writers with equal non-team contributions; a second writer must contribute more than 50% for credit.
- **Ten Creative Rights** (1988 MBA): No dissemination of critiques or summaries without consent; right to restrict transfer to third parties; original writer retains first rewrite rights; consultation required for every revision; participation in tone, location scouting, and casting discussions; arranged meeting with the director; first-class travel accommodations; viewing prior to final cut; invitation to the Los Angeles premiere screening; complimentary videocassette/copy provided.
- **Money**: 5% of net profit = 5% of zero; accept gross points immediately; MBA minimums (roughly $45,000 for budgets under $25M, roughly $85,000 above, adjusted upward annually); segregate rights (novelization/stage/sequel); character, merchandising, and ancillary market rights negotiated separately; work-for-hire holds no copyright; step deals establish floor rates for each phase; production bonuses; deferred payment equates to zero payment for newcomers.
- **An agent is NOT** an employment agency, a babysitter, or a banker; **an agent IS** a businessperson (10%), a team member, a business partner, and a badge of industry validation; consult the WGA agency list; mega-agencies are closed to newcomers, while boutique firms hold sway in specific niches; no legitimate agent charges a "reading fee."
- **Entertainment Attorney**: Charges $200–$300 per hour to review contracts; **Personal Manager**: Charges 15%, not bound by WGA constraints.
- **True Stories**: Consult an attorney first, sign a Life Story Option Agreement; secure releases from family members too; public figures hold rights that extend to heirs; private figures hold vital rights to privacy.
- **Copyright**: Exists upon completion; registration (two script copies, $20, application form) unlocks statutory damages and attorney's fees.
- **Parable of Sparky the Dog's Portrait**: Pay a portraitist to paint your dog -> add your own colors -> a second painter covers it in white gesso to paint a comic strip -> a third cuts a hole to insert a plaster head wearing a clown ruff—only the signature of the original first painter carries enduring value.

## IV. Diamond & Weissman: Notes Handling and Terminology

- Three types of notes: immensely helpful; take-it-or-leave-it; valid notes where you should ignore the suggested fix. The "Classic Car" anecdote: A Fox executive noted that the three boys needed their own goal in Act One (a great note), but pitched the specific fix of "a classic car" -> politely declined without offering a compelling alternative -> "It's the classic car, gentlemen."
- Mark Gordon's candid walk-and-talk regarding *Whiz Kid*: "12-year-olds don't want to watch movies about 12-year-olds anymore; they all want to watch *Speed*."
- Submission pipeline: Friends first -> pick one producer and one executive -> leverage introductions to known representation; "It's not about contacts, it's not about competition; it's about relationships."
- Screenplay Contests: The Academy Nicholl Fellowship is life-changing; prominent festival competitions; obscure local contests offer no career lift; pay-to-upload platforms are not inherently evil, but avoid accumulating debt.
- Terminology: Amp Up (frequent Hollywood buzzword); Buzz (ignites a career even without a production greenlight); Coverage (new writers' scripts are vetted through coverage first); Execution-Dependent (the standard polite pass); Meld (story consolidation); Runner (recurring comedic bit); Set Piece; Slugline; Spec Screenplay (serves as a calling-card writing sample if unsold); Tentpole; Writing Sample.
- Batting Average: 15 films produced out of 47 projects (0.319).

## VI. Masterclass: Pitching and Credits (Four Hollywood Lectures)

- Richards: Include a one-line logline (or two to three sentences) plus two or three comp titles in the script file ("*The Godfather* meets *Furious 7*"); has written over 150 loglines; bring a one-to-two-page synopsis for financing pitches, including one or two key dialogue lines, "reading like a movie flashing before the eyes, leaving room to breathe"; WGA registration costs $20 per project (open to non-members), US Copyright registration costs $35; seven writers worked on *Sahara* without collaborating, leading the WGA to arbitrate credits based on individual written statements.
- Banks: Answer twelve structural beat questions within five minutes (Situation? Genre? Main characters and archetypes? Core dilemma? Page 10 catalyst? Page 30 act turn? Page 45 engine and theme? Page 60 midpoint? Page 75 sequence climax? Act Two break? Act Three climax?) focusing purely on archetypes rather than plot minutiae; Group A spent four and a half minutes rambling through exposition while "investors were already checking their phones"; "clarity over comprehensiveness"; writers who refuse notes live in Hollywood Boulevard apartments, while those who collaborate live in Beverly Hills; "retain the original edge while fitting the idea into the financier's box, without sacrificing the story's depth."
- Stentz: Partnership credit "with" (collaboration) vs. "and" (rewrites); three anonymous veteran arbiters evaluate contribution rankings; "I'm not the boss, you're not the boss; the story is the boss"; screenwriters do not own copyright, whereas novelists retain it; "The hallmark of a professional screenwriter is convincing others to spend money on you"; pitch a two-hour film in one minute—practice on your spouse, rehearse in the mirror, script the outline word-for-word, anticipate curveball questions; financing spec scripts is tough: keep budgets modest, anchor the story around compelling characters, and attract attached talent or directors.
- Film vs. TV Production: Films run $100M–$300M over 80–120 shoot days plus reshoots; TV episodes take 8–14 days at $3M–$8M per episode (*Game of Thrones* topped out at $40M); bottle episodes; evolving development landscape: streamers ordering straight-to-series or greenlighting an initial three-to-four episode block.

## VII. Henson: Breaking In

- Agent Paul Small: "Playing it by ear and adapting on your feet is the eternal golden rule"; "When meeting an agent, bring a well-stocked inventory" of at least three or four polished scripts.
- Belvaux: "Never let money blind you; commit fully to the screenwriting journey and worry about finances later"; "Watching a beloved script rot on the shelf breaks your heart, but business is business."
- Ducsay: "Only by understanding your opponent can you hit where it counts"; passion and conviction must burn like "a fire in your chest."
- Phone Pitching: Know the script inside and out, outline the core spine and major beats, summarize the standout set pieces, and rehearse privately beforehand; "If you cannot summarize your own screenplay, how can you carry an entire project?"
- First contracts routinely defer payment until the first day of principal photography (*E.T.* took 8 years, *Forrest Gump* 10 years, *Ghost* 11 years); "Never abandon your specialty."

## VIII. Snyder: Breaking In

- Landing an agent: ambition (plastering flyers until public television aired *The Blank Show*) meets serendipity (a girlfriend's friend met at a bar became his agent).
- Hierarchy of contact: In-person > phone > query letter > email; after every rejection, always ask, "Is there anyone else you would recommend?"; the first buyer is paramount—take the job even if the money is low.
- Networking: Film festivals, producer seminars, writer groups, building authority via film criticism, moving to Los Angeles to work as a script reader, personal websites.
- Avoid: Screenplay competitions (echo chambers, unless judged by professionals with actual sales track records); foolish gimmicks (shipping yourself in a wooden crate to William Morris).
- Legendary pitches: *Leave It to Beaver* featured the original series' actress delivering milk and cookies; *Tick-Tock* involved mailing alarm clocks timed to ring simultaneously to studio executives; *Nuclear Family* arrived in a radioactive isotope container; *Blank Check* arrived with a backpack stuffed with one million dollars in play money—all closed for $1M.
- "It is what it is": Do the work, then leave the rest to fate.

## IX. Mei Feng: Oscar Industry Data

- *American Beauty* grossed $130M on a $5M budget; DreamWorks spent $774,000 on FYC advertising over four weeks following nominations (doubling the ad space of any competitor across three major trade publications); only two Best Picture winners in the 1990s also captured Best Original Screenplay (the other being *Shakespeare in Love*).
- 1996, Year of the Independents: For the first time, not a single major studio film was nominated for Best Picture; *Fargo* earned seven nominations, losing Best Picture to *The English Patient* while winning Best Original Screenplay and Best Actress, alongside Best Director at Cannes that same year.
- 1991: *The Silence of the Lambs* won Best Picture from seven nominations (an unprecedented feat for a horror-thriller); *Beauty and the Beast* became the first animated feature nominated for Best Picture; 23-year-old John Singleton became the youngest and first Black nominee for Best Director; *Thelma & Louise* received roughly $2M in marketing from MGM, landing on the cover of *Time* magazine upon release.
- *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* was budgeted at $2M; Charlie Kaufman had already garnered three Oscar nominations (losing to *American Beauty* in 1999 and *The Pianist* in 2002); "Hollywood has yet to formulate a systematic methodology for packaging and marketing films of this nature."
- Hitchcock's rights economics: Bought *The Man Who Knew Too Much* for 250 pounds, selling it for double; bought *To Catch a Thief* for $15,000, selling it twenty years later to Paramount for $105,000; used intermediaries to suppress acquisition costs: $7,500 for *Strangers on a Train*, $11,000 for *The Trouble with Harry*, $9,000 for *Psycho*.

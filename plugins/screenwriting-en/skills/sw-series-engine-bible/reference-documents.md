## 8. The Full Structure of Rabkin's Mooresville Pitch Document (ten sections)

**Source**: Rabkin, *Writing the Pilot: Creating the Series* (2017), appendix "Mooresville Pitch." It is the finished product of the NASCAR derivation in chapter 7 — a **written pitch (series pitch document)** addressed to NASCAR executives and the networks, not a pilot script, and therefore a complete physical sample of a mini-bible.

**Two caveats on use**: ① the total page count and the word counts of each section are **not recorded**; these notes preserve only two clues about length — the Overview is "a one-page positioning," and How We Got Here describes itself as the short version of "about 60 pages of pilot script." ② This book (2017, the sequel to the 2011 original) **does not contain** a pilot typology — grepping the whole text for "premise pilot / cold open / four-act" returns nothing; so the premise vs. typical-episode test has to come from Blum ch. 17, Smith ch. 20, Landau and Oberg 3.2 (see sections 4 and 9 of this file), and must not be remembered as Rabkin's position.

### 8.1 The Ten Sections, Section by Section

| # | Section heading (as printed) | What this section answers | Sense of length | Key content demonstrated in the book |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Overview** | What this show is and why it is worth buying — one selling point per line | **one page** (the note reads "one-page positioning") | Four lines, four selling points, each hung on a genre or a hook: ① "a **fish-out-of-water comedy** about a lifelong New Yorker forced to live on the NASCAR circuit."; ② "a **romance** about the growing, impossible attraction between two completely incompatible personalities."; ③ "a **drama** about the challenges of staying free in a world that wants you to conform … what it means … and what it costs." (= the chapter 7 theme "individual vs. community" turned into pitch copy); ④ "most of all, it's about the **thrill of NASCAR Racing** – not the sterile sport we see on TV, but the raw power of the real thing." (a direct promise to the buyer, NASCAR, with "the spirit that comes straight down from the original moonshiners racing on the Daytona sands"). The closing line: "Mooresville. It's all about the love for speed and freedom." |
| 2 | **Our Hero** | Who the protagonist is, what he wants, and why he is trapped here | not recorded | Open with an **analogy** (a Spartan warrior dropped into the boardroom of a Manhattan private-equity firm), then invert it: Marshall Breckman = the **"Warrior of the Deal"**, born in a Fifth Avenue penthouse, New Haven and Cambridge (Yale, Harvard MBA), "prepped to win"; then drop him into a land where "people like plain talk, not smooth words … the only market people care about is what's on sale at the A&P." The situation: exiled from Wall Street to Mooresville, North Carolina, as the new CFO of "the last independent racing team in NASCAR"; he has never watched a race, **cannot drive**, thinks the audience are hicks, and regards the job as "only slightly better than a life sentence in Guantanamo." The engine sentence (want and weekly task fused): **"For him, the only road back to Manhattan is the straightaway at Daytona …"** |
| 3 | **Last of a Dying Breed** | Who the female lead is, her family history and the source of her refusal to yield | not recorded | First three generations of family history: grandfather **Jack Stagger** (former moonshine runner, racing pioneer); father **Jimmy Lee Stagger** — a mid-pack driver famous for his independence (to everyone else, "the orneriest SOB on the circuit"), who painted out the logos in white when the sponsors left and kept racing, and who with his wife drove a school bus off a cliff on the way to a church picnic (a piece of black comedy dispatched in one line). Then the lead, **LEIGH PETTY STAGGER (Pet)**, 27, named after Jack's old friend and rival (Richard Petty); "thrown out of more schools than most people apply to"; bright, but **"she's got this thing about authority. Any authority."**; she walked out mid-Thanksgiving five years ago and has come back to guard her father's legacy: "She'll do anything to keep Stagger Racing independent … She'll even put up with that no-good, fast-talking slickster from New York, Marshall Breckman. Well, up to a point, anyway." The craft point: **write the female lead's page around the one conflicting attribute that must grind against the protagonist** (chapter 7: "she exists to fill his opposite space") |
| 4 | **How We Got Here** | What the pilot tells, and how it establishes the series' normal | Described as the short version of "about **60 pages** of pilot script"; the paragraph count is not recorded, but the note reads "one turn per paragraph" | Marshall is a rising star at the PE firm Barzun Keegan, sent to Mooresville for a day trip to help Jack complete the sale of the team to NASCAR giant **Raymond Fester Industries (RFI)** → he meets Pet and "his resolve started to waver" (this is where she is described as "absolutely, knock-down gorgeous … It's part of her power. She uses it, she dresses for it." — he hates being manipulated by looks and cannot stop thinking about her) → he takes another look at the paperwork → finds the deal is wrong → catches a colleague working for RFI → the truth: **Barzun Keegan is negotiating to buy RFI, and Marshall was expected to lose his own side deliberately** → instead he gives the Staggers a new plan that keeps the team alive long enough to find sponsors, and "went to war" against RFI and his own firm → **he is fired and blacklisted across the industry** ("this man can't be trusted") → the only offer left: stay on as CFO of Stagger Racing. The craft point: **a pilot synopsis tells only "how we got here," and must land on the establishment of the series' normal (he is trapped here), not on the plot climax** |
| 5 | **Life in Mooresville** | What this world is, and what the protagonist has lost | not recorded (the content is a **two-column comparison list**) | **What Manhattan had** (as written): a two-bedroom with a Central Park view; opera season tickets (orchestra); the private numbers of the city's best maîtres d' and a table guaranteed at any hour; a 24-hour car service; bespoke suits; elegant women eager to be seen with the "Warrior of the Deal"; the best art, music and culture; an expense account that covers everything. **What Mooresville has**: a "two-room suite" at the local Motel 7 (slogan "It's one better than 6!", in fact two adjacent rooms with no connecting door); a 24-hour Wal-Mart across the street; a young female cab driver calling herself JONES (giving him a discount in exchange for a promise to take her to New York one day). For **36 weeks a year** the office is a cubbyhole at the back of the transporter and home is a partitioned RV shared with Jones (roommates only). Plus his read on Pet: he comes from "a world of subtext, of hidden meanings, of codes" while she is "as blunt and direct as a hurricane – and she leaves the same kind of messes behind her"; "To someone like Marshall, she's practically a savage … she should be locked in a cage. If only he could stop thinking about her …" The craft point: **the two-column list makes the fish-out-of-water gap concrete, and the world's physical constraints (36 weeks on the road, an office in a trailer) are themselves the container for weekly stories** |
| 6 | **It Goes the Other Way, Too …** | The protagonist seen from the other side — why the attraction is **mutual** | not recorded | To her he is "that little wormy thing wriggling on the ground" (three-piece suit, calculation, too many words); the deeper hatred is that he nominally works for her yet runs her business in ways she cannot follow, so "he's turned himself into the one thing she hates more than everything else in the world: **an authority figure**." She already has a boyfriend, **KYLE COKER** (the team's only driver, "strong, quiet, ruggedly handsome, defiant … and fast"). Why she still thinks about Marshall: everyone who has known her for years indulges her as a wilful child (love without respect; Kyle sees himself as her protector and rock, not her equal); **Marshall treats her like a CEO and argues when he disagrees, but "start[s] from the position that they are both rational, intelligent adults"** — "When Marshall treats her like an adult, she starts to act like one … she likes herself a lot better when he's around." (= the source of the "rationality axis" item in section 6 of reference.md) |
| 7 | **Live Free or … Well, You Know** | What the weekly operational reality is, and what the protagonist's continuing task is | not recorded | Operational reality: a crew of 5; one small disaster can destroy the team; **everything non-mechanical is Marshall's.** The car, **THE BIRD**: a converted 1992 third-generation Pontiac Firebird from the last batch (with a backup, Bird 2), every ounce of metal and rubber replaced many times over, "hopelessly primitive" next to a hand-built aerodynamic racer — but Pet holds to her grandfather's philosophy: NASCAR means "stock car," and she runs only genuine production cars. The final statement of the engine: Pet loathes authority → she hires only people like herself → "Incredibly talented technicians who have been fired from every other crew on the circuit" → **"It's going to be up to Marshall to take this collection of misfits and turn them into a team."** ("he's got to learn to be Daddy") |
| 8 | **The Rest of the Characters** | What team function each supporting character serves, and what undetonated charge they carry | not recorded (one paragraph each, seven people; see 8.2) | The formula: **each supporting character = one team function + one concrete form of "stubbornness/freedom" (serving the theme) + one undetonated relationship or desire (a landmine)** |
| 9 | **The Antagonist** | What the villain wants, why he has to crush the protagonists, and what personal history he has with them | not recorded | RAYMOND FESTER (see the last row of 8.2). The craft point: **the villain's motive is supplied by the theme** (the extreme-individualist version of "individual vs. community"), and he has a personal history with the female lead |
| 10 | **Sample Story Areas** | Can this pattern repeat — the evidence | Six items (see 8.3); the length of each is not recorded, transcribed in the notes as three or four sentences per paragraph | All six press the same pattern, and each presses both the A line (the business) and the B line (the triangle / the relationship); **this is how a pitch document verifies the story pattern of chapter 8 and the theme of chapter 7** |

### 8.2 Nine Characters Plus the Antagonist: Team Function, Thematic Position, Landmine

(Same source; Rabkin's appendix, Mooresville Pitch, §The Rest of the Characters and §The Antagonist. The theme is the "individual vs. community" derived in chapter 7.)

| Character | Team function | Position on "individual vs. community" | Landmine planted (undetonated relationship / desire / contradiction) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Marshall Breckman** | CFO / outsider | **Community** (a deal is a compromise; his task is to weld a set of misfits into a team) | Wanting to get back to Manhattan vs. falling in love with Pet; his friendship with Kyle |
| **Pet Stagger** | The boss | **Extreme individual** (against all authority) | She sees Marshall as an authority, and grows because of him; she keeps postponing the wedding with Kyle |
| **Kyle Coker** | The only driver | Individual (with a self-contained cosmological theory) | Two obstacles: ① a self-taught "theorist of the universe" (cancer is a bioweapon invented by the priests of Atlantis, the cure is in ruins off the California coast… point a camera at him and it is over); ② he still drives well in an old car, which proves the talent. Why he does not leave: **he has loved Pet for 25 years** (he is 28), they met in pre-school, and he always cleans up after her; he proposed after her parents' funeral and she accepted, **but keeps postponing the wedding.** He regards Marshall as his best friend (the only person who will seriously argue theory with him), and "There's no way Marshall could ever betray him – people just don't act like that." → **the triangle landmine** |
| **S.T.A.N. (Spiro T. Agnew Nathan)** | Genius mechanic | Individual (**can only communicate by arguing**) | Builds high technology out of junkyard parts, roughly twice a race; lives off a hoard of scrapped Firebirds, and spends half his road time hunting cheap third-generation Firebirds. Every team has fired him ("Sometimes within minutes of hiring him") — if you concede too quickly he switches sides and keeps arguing. Family history: his father Fred Nathan never forgave Nixon for resigning ("a quitter"), so the son born on 11 October 1973 was named Spiro T. Agnew Nathan as a rebuke to the president |
| **Aurora Nathan** | Crew chief (drives the transporter plus a hundred other jobs) | Individual (the only person who can argue as long and as hard as S.T.A.N.) | A frail, shy exterior plus "either fighting furiously – or making love violently" (thin motel walls) = the marital war itself is a repeatable source of scenes |
| **Winfield Winfield (Dub)** | Jack-of-all-trades / slacker / troublemaker | Individual (no sense of responsibility) | The last of a Georgia dynasty (WW → Dub); his real identity is **the great-grandson of the long-term sponsor**, whose agreement requires the team to give him a job (but does not require him to do any work); he charms his way through everything; he has never felt anything strongly — **except being madly in love with Jones** |
| **Jones** | Assistant / driver | **The opportunist version of community** (rising by attachment) | A Trump and *The Apprentice* devotee who has memorized all his books and his quote-a-day calendar, and wants her name on the Manhattan skyline; her NC State degree was cut short when her ex-husband stole her student loan to build a mobile meth lab; she drives a cab to repay it and refuses to make quick money on her looks ("a good girl with strong morals"). When Marshall gets into her cab it is as if "God has sent her an angel" — she intends to make herself indispensable and the team successful so she will be taken back to New York. She **is starting to love him**: "She's the only one he's completely honest with … So why is he so hung up on Pet?" |
| **Elwood Winfield** | Principal sponsor (owner of Winfield's Chew) | **Old-fashioned loyal community** | He has sponsored Jack since the Daytona beach-race days (when sponsorship meant buying a case of oil); the older he gets the odder and more demanding — every request for money comes with a bizarre condition (making Marshall mow his 40 acres of lawn). Marshall wants to replace the principal sponsor and Pet will not hear of it — "the flip side of her hatred of authority is a fierce loyalty to those who stand by her" — and Winfield **will pull his money the instant he catches wind of it** |
| **Jack Stagger** | Adviser ("wartime consigliere") | Individual (retired in the 1970s when the sport went on ABC's Wide World of Sports, as "too commercial") | The Zeus of NASCAR — everyone has heard the stories and nobody remembers why; his opinion is fact; even Pet is afraid of him; under the shell are a warm heart and a sharp mind, and Marshall has to take a few hours of abuse before he can ask him anything |
| **RAYMOND FESTER (the antagonist)** | Chairman of RFI | **Individualism at its extreme** (swallowing the whole sport) | "bought up every independent racer in the field"; not content with the biggest team, **he wants to own the sport**, now with Barzun Keegan money behind him, and he will steal, cheat, lie and extort. The Staggers are no direct threat — "It's much worse than that. Because they're a **symbol** – proof that you can stand up to Fester and win … a 180 mph billboard advertising RFI's impotence." The landmine: his father Hank Fester was also an independent racer and a friend of Pet's father, and as children the families joked about a marriage — "he'd still be just as happy to marry Pet as to wipe her out"; Hank went bankrupt in the mid-90s → "only the strong and rich survive. He will never be weak – or let himself appear weak – again." |

**How to read this table**: not one of the nine is a "functional supporting character" — every row fills all three columns at once (function, thematic position, landmine). Set it against Landau's familial-role casting bank in section 6 of reference.md and the story-landmines item in section 7: Rabkin's appendix is both those rules executed simultaneously, in the flesh.

### 8.3 The Six Sample Story Areas (key points as written) and What They Prove

1. Marshall lands a new corporate sponsor, Elwood is enraged and pulls out; the new sponsor turns out to be an RFI trick — Marshall has to win Winfield back before Pet discovers he has bankrupted the team.
2. On the 25th anniversary of their meeting, Kyle proposes again and demands a date or his freedom; Pet refuses anything she is *told* to do, and Kyle quits for another team — Marshall has to get Kyle back before the next race, and **the only way is to make the woman he is starting to love marry someone else.**
3. Overnighting in a small town on the way to a race, S.T.A.N. gets into a fight and is jailed; Pet wants to break him out, Marshall insists on doing it properly; Marshall argues before the judge — and ends up in the next cell, and now has to get both of them out before Pet launches a "daring and stupid jailbreak."
4. An old Manhattan friend calls with a bank job at double the salary; the condition is that Pet release him from his contract — she agrees, but only if he **teaches Kyle to do his job first.**
5. Three of Kyle's pay cheques bounce and he takes a job running moonshine across state lines; Marshall and Pet chase him down to stop him; but Kyle never delivered, and the bootleggers hold him hostage until Marshall and Pet make the delivery for him…
6. With the team near bankruptcy, Marshall arranges a bank loan through his old New York friends; Pet finds out and is furious — she refuses to owe anyone anything, or she walks; and she is right: the bank belongs to Barzun Keegan, the note has already been sold to RFI, and the moment they miss a payment it forecloses.

**The shape all six share (Rabkin's own analysis)**: crisis → **Marshall solves it with a deal, a procedure or an outside resource** → that triggers Pet's anti-authority or anti-dependence reflex, or the team's individualism → **blowback** → Marshall has to finish by switching to the "community" method (pulling people back into the team). And every one presses both the A line (business) and the B line (the triangle / the relationship).

→ Three checks you can run directly: ① is the **driver** of all six the protagonist? (If two or more are driven by supporting characters, your engine is really an ensemble engine and the "weekly task" sentence in section 7 needs rewriting.) ② Can all six be summed up by one story-pattern sentence? (If not, the pattern has not formed yet.) ③ Do all six touch both the A and B lines? (Any that touches only one will become filler on screen.) Set this against Blum's **five-story test** (section 7 of reference.md): Blum checks that "source and ending shape must not be alike," Rabkin checks that "the shape must be alike" — **the two do not conflict: the shape (pattern) should be the same, the material and the endings should differ.**

### 8.4 The Document Rules Extracted From This Physical Sample (Rabkin's own item 21)

A pitch document must contain: ① an Overview positioning it across several genres (one selling point per line); ② **both sides'** view of the protagonist and the adversary (two sections, not one); ③ a pilot synopsis (How We Got Here) landing on the series' normal rather than on the climax; ④ a **two-column loss/gain comparison** of the world; ⑤ one "weekly task" sentence; ⑥ each supporting character's function plus landmine; ⑦ an antagonist driven by the theme; ⑧ **at least 5–6 Sample Story Areas proving the pattern repeats.**

How it lines up against the other books (disagreements preserved): Rabkin's ten sections give "the other side's view" a section of its own, which **appears in none of Landau's, Oberg's or Smith's field tables** — relationship shows and two-hander shows should add it. Conversely, Rabkin's ten sections have **no** logline, no explicit theme section, no season arc, no episode-logline table and no package or budget section; take those from Oberg's twelve components (section 10) and Smith's eleven sections (section 9).

## 9. Smith's Eleven-Section Series Format (the selling document for a half-hour comedy, 10–15 pages)

**Source**: Evan S. Smith, *Writing Television Sitcoms* (2009), chapter 20, "Creating a New Series," p0292–0295. **Year note**: this chapter's market judgements ("most network executives prefer to see a typical-episode pilot," the ratings argument for market analysis, new-media sizzle, the WGA fee figures) all belong to the **2009 American broadcast-network context** — the mechanism still holds, the networks and the numbers are out of date.

**What it is**: a series format is "a brief selling document that describes the series premise and hints at how future episodes would develop." Experienced writers sometimes pitch with a format instead of a pilot script, or take both (p0291–292). Length is **typically 10–15 pages, with plenty of white space** (excluding attachments such as the article that inspired the show, ratings data, or letters of intent from partners).

### 9.1 The Eleven Sections, Field by Field

> **Small projects, follow this one sentence**: for a project with no credits, no star, no budget sheet and no option, **write only sections 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6** (five sections), and drop sections 2, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11 entirely — not out of laziness; it is this skill's own rule. Do not assume you have missed six sections merely because the list has eleven rows.

| # | Section | Required / optional | What to put in it (key points as written) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title page** | required | Series title, creator's name, contact details for the creator's rep (or the creator); formatted per the title-page specification in Appendix A |
| 2 | **Opening sell copy** | **optional** | A one-line logline, the protagonist's personal creed, a statement or question expressing the theme, or a quotation — the sole purpose is to make someone want to keep listening. Warning: **many formats omit this section, and done badly it reads as cutesy** |
| 3 | **Description of the series premise** | required, **1–3 pages** | Characters, relationships, setting, time frame, story type and **the source of continuing conflict.** As written: "This is a tough section to write because it must introduce the show's universe... in an amusing and compelling fashion. And it must convince the reader that the show has **legs**, a premise rich enough to generate a hundred or more episodes down the road." Two techniques: ① you may **carry all this information on the pilot's story**, "disguising" it in comedy and drama; ② **introduce each character in this section with a single phrase only**, letting her actions and lines define her (detailed character descriptions belong to section 6) |
| 4 | **The pilot story** | conditionally required, **1–2 pages** | If section 3 did not use the pilot story, supply it here. The reason: illustrating the premise with a real story "is the best way to prove that the series will actually be funny," and proves you can write this show |
| 5 | **8–10 loglines / springboards for future episodes** | required | Proves the legs and hooks the reader with funny concepts. A serialized show substitutes **several main story arcs for season one**, or interleaves the two (threading the arcs through the springboard list) |
| 6 | **The regular character list** | required | A paragraph each, more for leads and less for supporting players; **must name the key relationships that generate conflict and comedy (the character mix).** Explicit instruction: detailed character description goes in this section and **must not be pushed into section 3** (it will sink that section) |
| 7 | **Market analysis** | optional | Recent ratings performance of comparable shows plus current shows competing for the same audience. "It's best to keep this section very short and objective," and include it only where the numbers strongly support you; **some argue for omitting it entirely** — dryness dilutes the excitement of the creative sections |
| 8 | **Budget summary** | optional, **1–2 pages** | Supplied by an experienced producer, or a cost estimate for the pilot and the first batch of episodes; any money-saving production or co-financing approach goes here |
| 9 | **Unique aspects** | optional | A star already attached, a basis in a published work (**which you have optioned**), a substantial producing or financing partner. **Warning (2009 context, still valid)**: plenty of people in the industry are eager to attach themselves to find work — "Do not assume that this (used-to-be-a-star) actor or that (working-his-way-up) producer will help you to make a sale"; if they are not currently in demand, **attaching them will sink your project instead** |
| 10 | **Credits of the creator and anyone attached** | optional | **No credits and no names → drop the whole section** |
| 11 | **Closing sell copy** | optional | "a corny closer can kill a sale" |

**Three general rules** (same page): ① this is only one blueprint, and **the order and combination may be adjusted per project**; ② **the format is itself a sample of your writing** — "keep those adjectives and adverbs at bay," and prove throughout that the show is timely, unique and **funny**: "you can't sell a sitcom with a document that reads like an annual report"; ③ length 10–15 pages, **with plenty of white space.**

### 9.2 How to Prove "Legs = a Hundred Episodes" (Smith's three-link chain of evidence)

1. **Section 3 promises it directly**: the premise description must "convince the reader that the show has legs, a premise rich enough to generate **a hundred or more episodes** down the road." That is: legs are not declared with adjectives, they are proven by writing out "the source of continuing conflict" in this section.
2. **Section 4 uses the pilot story as the sample**: one actually written story proving "this show really is funny" and "you can write it" — the **quality** proof of legs.
3. **Section 5 uses 8–10 springboards as the capacity proof** — the **quantity** proof of legs. The companion test in the pilot chapter (p0295–298): **proximity counts** — "you need your characters to keep bumping into each other so they can keep generating conflict and comedy in episode after episode"; you need not build yet another office or living-room show (single-camera can roam), but **the premise must contain some setting or dramatic device that squeezes the characters together on a regular basis.**

**The thresholds from the various books, kept side by side (they do not override one another)**: Smith ch. 20's "**a hundred or more**" / the four questions in Goldberg & Rabkin ch. 17, which likewise demand "**100 or more episodes**" (section 7 of reference.md) / Landau 1e Tip 7's **six-episode test** / Landau 2e ch. 14's **three** for a pitch and Blum ch. 17's **five or six** for a presentation / Rabkin ch. 6's **99 stories.** How to read all three tiers together: **99/100 episodes is the design standard for the engine, 5–10 is the delivery standard for the document, and 3 is the live standard for a spoken pitch.**

### 9.3 How to Write the 8–10 Springboards

- **Form**: a half-hour comedy writes them as loglines or springboards (starters), not synopses; the test is **"hook them with a funny concept"** — the concept itself must be funny in one line, not funny only once executed.
- **The serialized substitute**: replace this section with **several main story arcs for season one**, or interleave the two — thread the arcs through the springboard list (i.e. "which step of the arc does the episode-3 springboard also advance?").
- **A physical example**: the **14 episode loglines** for *B-List* in Miller's appendix are a filled-in sample of this section (see section 12.8), and Miller explicitly says it is the most successful part of the whole document — "He knocked it out of the park with his proposed episode loglines. They made me laugh, and I totally got Henry's show."; the last entry is nothing but a title (*Attack of the Giant Clam*.), which is itself the joke.
- **Another way to generate them**: Jeffries's "ten gripping questions at the end of the pilot → ten short future-episode synopses" (item 40 in section 4 of reference.md) — questions first, springboards derived from the questions.

### 9.4 Where Smith Disagrees With the Other Books (kept side by side)

| Point at issue | Smith ch. 20 (2009) | The other side | How to use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sell copy at the top | Optional, and "many formats omit it"; done badly, cutesy | Oberg 3.1, component 3: the Series Overview may contain an **opening statement** (the creator's personal connection to the material, "a discrete way to tie yourself to the show"), which may stand as its own section | If you have a genuine personal connection, write it as an opening statement (not ad copy); if not, drop it |
| Whether to include market analysis | Optional, must be very short and objective; "some argue for omitting it entirely" | Of Miller's six appendix samples only *Minotaur* includes a **Markets** section, whose argument formula = the target platform's existing tone plus the gap it has (section 12.5) | If the numbers are not strong, omit it; if you write it, write "the platform's gap," not a ratings table |
| Where characters go | **Their own section 6**; section 3 gives each only a phrase | Oberg: Character Breakdowns, 3–5 leads at ≤1 page each; Douglas 3e ch. 2: one page each, only a handful; Landau: introduce characters inside the story, never as a list | The hard rule all three share is **do not stuff a character list into the overview**; they differ only on the length of the detailed character section |
| Premise or typical-episode pilot | **"most network executives prefer to see a typical-episode pilot"**; a premise pilot cannot demonstrate a normal episode ("that Martian won't crash-land in someone's yard every week") and reruns badly | Landau: not a trap, just say which it is in the pitch | See the four tests in item 37, section 4 of reference.md; for half-hour comedy, follow Smith |
| Can a format replace a pilot script | An experienced writer can; or take both | Oberg 3, First Up: anyone with **no track record is strongly advised to write the pilot first** and prepare a written leave-behind; Landau: "write your pilot scripts on spec"; the dissent, Aranya: "a script is also 60 pages of reasons to say no" | No credits → write the script first; credits → lead with the format |

### 9.5 Where This Document Meets the Pitch Room (p0299–0302, 2009 context)

- **Pitching an episode, bring a long list of springboards; pitching a new series, bring only the one big idea, plus one or two backup series ideas.** When they like the big idea, **never go on to the backups** ("you'll confuse matters and kill the sale"); only if the big idea is rejected do you produce the backups one at a time; if all are rejected, **ask what kind of show they want** and go home and develop to that.
- When the project is based on an existing work or on real events and **you already hold the rights**, you may bring the book, the article or the tape into the room (that is a relevant visual aid, not a gimmick). The companion hard rule: **"You can't sell what you don't own."** — option or buy before adapting; the first question any producer or executive asks about an adaptation is always "Do you own the rights?", and if you cannot answer, "you'll look like an amateur."
- Register with the Copyright Office or the WGA before sending it out — "Unlike an ordinary spec, this is an entirely original work." (Contrast the opposite detail in Miller ch. 10: **do not print registration numbers or copyright notices on the title page.**)
- **Fees (2009–2011 WGA figures, out of date, for order of magnitude only)**: for a half-hour prime-time sitcom on the four networks or pay cable (HBO, Showtime), the **pilot script minimum = 150% of the per-episode minimum** — 2 May 2009 to 1 May 2010: **$33,349.50**; 2 May 2010 to 1 May 2011: **$34,350.00**; additional rewrites are negotiated separately at **$5,000–$10,000**; the writer is usually also hired as one of the pilot's producers, a fee outside WGA jurisdiction at **$10,000–$50,000 or more**; a series adds bonuses, an episode commitment, **a "Created By" credit and a per-episode royalty**, possibly a share of net profits ("though few series ever see profits") and residuals. Basic cable runs a little over half the network rate; a non-WGA member plus a non-signatory network = "everything's up for grabs." (Full treatment of the business side is in `sw-industry-business`.)

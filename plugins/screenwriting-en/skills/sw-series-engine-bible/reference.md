> This file covers engines, types and character webs. Document templates are in [reference-documents.md](reference-documents.md) (Rabkin's ten-section pitch document, Smith's eleven-section series format, Oberg's twelve bible components); filled-in samples are in [reference-samples.md](reference-samples.md) (two sets of writers' guidelines, Miller's six-treatment matrix, the Succession table of seven wants).


## 1. Document Tiers and Template Fields (Blum / Landau / Miller / Calvisi / Oberg / Douglas / Smith)

**50. Three document tiers** (Blum ch. 17, as written): the **creative concept** = "a blueprint of the arena/setting, the characters and the conflict" → the **series presentation** = "the concept plus the arena/setting, the characters, a story outline (the pilot's story premise) and sample storylines (future stories)" → the **series bible** = "a completed sample script (the pilot), a specific character arc for every principal, and a step outline."

| Document | Source | Specification |
|---|---|---|
| bible / mini-bible | Landau 2e ch. 1 | **10–85 pages** / **about 5 pages**; start writing it as soon as the pilot's first draft is done |
| mythology document | Landau 2e ch. 14 | **one page maximum including the origin story.** "Keep it simple." |
| spoken pitch | Landau 2e ch. 14 / Oberg 3 | **a 20-minute sweet spot**, and time it / Oberg: within 15–20 minutes |
| written pitch bible | Oberg 3 | **10–30 pages (excluding the script), sweet spot around 15**; also called format, book, pitch document, proposal, TV treatment |
| treatment | Miller, introduction | **drama 10–15 pages / comedy 8–10 pages**; a bible runs 70–100 pages, is a paid item under the WGA MBA, and is usually written only after the sale |
| series format (half-hour comedy) | Smith ch. 20 | **10–15 pages, with plenty of white space** |
| Douglas's three bible tiers | Douglas 3e ch. 2 | ① the full version, **Star Trek: TNG at about 100 pages** (including **each crew member's relationship history with every other**); ② a few pages; ③ **most shows have no bible at all.** The accumulative model: Northern Exposure's **"facts to wax,"** "amassed rather than generated" |

**52. Four field checklists (pick one)**: **Landau 2e ch. 1 Essential Guideposts** (about 25 fields, usable as a bible table of contents; the **logline "must contain the word 'but'"**; the **central question is about the future, the central mystery about the past**); **the 11-item pitch recipe in Landau 2e ch. 14** (why this idea / why you / why now / tone = genre / pitch the teaser first / pitch the whole pilot / **embed characters in story** / mythology ≤1 page / **at least three future-episode examples** / stick the landing); **Miller's 10-element treatment** (title / type of show / idea / **logline ≤30 words** / world / characters / story engine / themes / tone / **season arcs across three seasons, or 10 episode loglines**, with six big questions: relevance / difference / envelope / research / influences / **why me**); **Calvisi's 17-column Basic Story Map** (including **COMPELLING CRISIS**, **WEEK-TO-WEEK**, DEFINING CHARACTERISTIC, MISBEHAVIOR, ACHILLES HEEL, DYNAMIC ALLY, SHADOW, PILOT ARC — **the same columns are filled in twice, once at episode level and once at series level**; write the PILOT ARC in three stages, not as two points).

**53. The five things most often got wrong**
1. **The overview is not a pilot synopsis** (Douglas 3e ch. 2 names this a common error): write the world and the quest of the whole show, and **give the springboards inside the overview** — "make it soar." Oberg adds one: **do not use vocabulary like story-type inside a bible.**
2. **Do not list characters**: Landau — "on a need-to-know basis within the context of the story vs. as a laundry list"; Douglas — one page each, only a handful of them (past the third or fourth, the listener's eyes glaze); Oberg — 3–5 leads, at most a page each; Smith — put detailed character descriptions in their own section.
3. **Do not tell them everything you know**: **"there is no need to tell the execs everything you know."** The success criterion is **"An effective pitch will entice your listeners to ask you questions."**
4. **Do not over-plan**: Oberg — **"Put 90% of your energy into making the best first season … and 90% of that energy into making your pilot the most exciting episode."**; "Showing rigidity regarding what might happen in Season 5 … is more likely to trigger alarm bells." Landau 2e: **the bible is a selling tool, not a road map the room is obliged to follow.**
5. **The prose style must match the show's genre**: Landau — "If it's a comedy, your pitch had better be funny."; Oberg — "Sell the sizzle, not the sausage."; Smith — "you can't sell a sitcom with a document that reads like an annual report."

## 2. A Skeleton for the Story-Line Document (one page per line; devised by this pack)

| Field | What goes in it | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Line name** | Driver plus verb ("Joyce gets Will back," "Greg finds a master") | If it can only be named with an abstract noun, there is no want yet |
| **Purpose** | The one thing it does for the whole show (serve the engine / explore down the Maslow pyramid / carry the opposite pole of the theme / relieve pressure on the ensemble) | If you cannot say it, cut the line |
| **Driver** | This line's protagonist = **the person who goes through the most conflict** (Oberg's definition), not necessarily a main character | If they share a goal with someone else and are triggered by the same event, this is really a strand of the A line (A1/A2/A3) |
| **Stakes** | The concrete consequence of failure plus its position on Maslow | If you cannot say "what happens if it fails," cut it |
| **Entry this episode** | Which scene it enters on and what triggers it | A line entering after the episode's midpoint should not be in this episode |
| **Exit this episode** | The state it leaves in (won / lost / suspended), and **who now knows what** | The exit must change somebody's state of knowledge |
| **Cross-episode beats** | The 3–8 beats the season gives it, noting which episode carries each | A line with no beat for two consecutive episodes gets forgotten |
| **Relation to the controlling idea** | Which pole of the thematic opposition it stands on, and when it switches | Test it with Rabkin's thematic filter (TWD's "does survival or civilization define humanity?" instantly kills an idea like "a birthday party mistaken for a murder") — **"When you have your theme … you're halfway to knowing what an episode looks like."** |
| **Causal relation to other lines** | Who it affects and who affects it (at least one) | **The pull-the-line test**: if removing it changes nothing, cut it or add causality by planting and paying off |

## 3. Series Type, Story-Type, Maslow and Arena (Oberg / Miller)

**42. Eight series types** (Oberg 1.2): procedural (closed-ended) / serial / serial-procedural hybrid (three variants) / sitcom / mini-series and limited / anthology / mini-series series / web series. The key points: a procedural is plot-led every episode, "like a mini-feature film lasting under an hour," watchable out of order, with its main problem at the bottom of the Maslow pyramid → the widest audience, at the cost of little room for character development; a serial may be any story-type at season or series level, **though each episode usually still has a plot-led main line**; the three hybrid variants are alternating mythology episodes (The X-Files, Lost) / a procedural element blown up to fill a season (The Wire's five seasons = drugs / the docks / politics / schools and the mayoral race / the media; Broadchurch; Stranger Things) / a procedural with strong serial elements (Sex Education); the unwritten rule of the sitcom is **"characters don't change, and it's fine"**; limited (4–8 episodes) is **the easiest first step from film into television.**

**44. Determining story-type** (Oberg 2.2): ask **"Where does the main problem in the story come from, at season level?"** — external → **plot-led** (Stranger Things, TWD); internal → **character-led** (Sex Education, After Life; no antagonist needed, but a **catalyst character** is, such as Maeve); society or the story world → **theme-led** (Big Little Lies, Succession, GoT, Deadwood: no overall three acts, but each line may be plot-led or character-led on its own); plus **hybrid** (Breaking Bad turning from plot-led to character-led) and **exception** (Fleabag, Mr. Robot, Happy Valley, True Detective S1: held together by managing information).

**45. The reverse test specific to series** (same section): **because series are almost always multi-line, you have to check backwards** — is there a protagonist and a main dramatic action/evolution taking shape at season level? If so, it is not theme-led. A second hint: do the protagonists of the lines **share a goal and get triggered by the same inciting incident**? If yes → very likely plot-led. **Chernobyl** looks theme-led, but all its lines share one inciting event and each protagonist has a conscious goal matching the main problem → it is in fact plot-led, "like many disaster series."

**47. Maslow and the M-Factor** (Oberg 2.3, 2.6): **"the lower in the pyramid, the larger the potential audience"** (TWD, Lost and Squid Game all sit at the bottom). **You must explore vertically**: Occupied's main problem is at the top (geopolitics), and had its lines not reached down to protecting the family and survival, "it's unlikely it would have met such commercial success"; Money Heist is the reverse (main problem on levels one and two, with the lines exploring up into relationships, friendship and morality); a sitcom explores the middle and reaches down with food, sex and toilet gags — **"If you go low with gags, you can set the main problem as high as you want."** A warning: a murder that has already happened plus a whodunit = problem-solving at the top of the pyramid, "primarily intellectual … not a thriller at all." The **M-Factor** = where the audience, genre and story-type sit relative to Maslow, plus whether the budget is self-consistent (scored 1–5). **Rooting motive at the bottom lets a morally complex protagonist still be identified with**: Breaking Bad's main problem is ego and pride (the esteem level) while its motive is rooted in "security of family, of health."

**49. The innate problems of an arena** (Miller ch. 1, a 2015 American broadcast-network perspective): **high school / college** has a "biological clock" (Glee was forced into a two-strand show after graduation and the ratings never recovered); **family shows** get asked "What's episode 44?", and the fix is a family show plus a strong engine (Empire = King Lear + a hip-hop label + drug-money crime; Sons of Anarchy = Hamlet + a motorcycle club); **relationship shows** can only bring in so many new characters (Jane the Virgin props up its engine with a crime element); **news shows** have reporters who can "meddle" but hold **no licence to intervene** — "A character's agency completes the story engine," which agrees with Rabkin ("A reporter is an observer, and a series demands a protagonist who is a participant"); **anthologies** give the audience a chance to quit at every season reset. Note: this is a judgement about one market in one year — Downton, Succession and Shtisel show that a family ensemble's engine can come from institutional friction (inheritance, class, religion).

**43. Two extra rules for the mini-series series** (Rabkin ch. 12): American Crime Story opened the floodgates because it had a concept **"not defined by execution"** (all the information is in the public domain and anyone could legally imitate it tomorrow); **the first episode of every season of an anthology or mini-series series must supply a fresh immediate narrative hook connecting the audience to the protagonist** (the interrogation-room device of True Detective S1) — you cannot assume continuity of mood, tone and style will carry the audience over (Better Call Saul is the exception, its lead already familiar). **Landau 2e ch. 1 (2022)**: **limited series are harder to sell**, platforms still prefer a sustainable franchise; Chernobyl and Unorthodox are "awards bait," and a short order generally does not make money; **10 episodes a season is the benchmark for a new show.**

**46. Choosing the wrong type locks you in** (Oberg's introduction and question 9 of 3.1): **"The wrong combination of series type and story-type is the root cause of many series failures."** The commonest box-in: **a character-led show resolving the protagonist's internal problem completely in season one (film thinking)** → season two has to change story-type → the engine changes, and "This almost never works, unless it's an anthological series or maybe a serial-procedural hybrid." Mare of Easttown is fine as a limited series, but a sequel could hardly avoid breaking the engine; Sex Education and Stranger Things are models of balance, resolving part of the main problem at season level while leaving enough for the next season without changing engine.

## 4. The Pilot-Type Table, Network Strategies and Douglas's Four Pilot Steps

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **premise pilot** | What happens in episode 1 is necessary for the show to begin at day one; Douglas: start from before and push the protagonist's quest | Lost, Barry, Grey's Anatomy, The Killing, The X-Files, Homeland, The Good Wife, Northern Exposure; **FNL** is counted hybrid by some, premise by Landau (it ends on the injury that paralyses Jason Street, affecting all of Dillon) |
| **non-premise / ongoing / midstream** | "It may be the audience's first episode, but it's not day one for the characters."; the difficulty is **no outsider to induct the audience** | Succession, WandaVision, Mad Men, GoT, Downton, E.R., The West Wing, The Wire, The Office, Fleabag, Scandal |
| **hybrid** | Open at the climactic moment then flash forward (Watchmen, Mrs. Maisel) or flash back (Breaking Bad, BCS, Chernobyl); Douglas: use an external event to push an already existing relationship | **The Sopranos is hybrid — the criminal world has been running all along, but this is day one of Tony's therapy with Melfi**; Boardwalk Empire (the day Prohibition takes effect) |
| **semi-cold start** (Oberg) | A short teaser sets the tone → about 10 minutes of flashback delivers the backstory → shot at minute 12 → wakes at minute 15, **the protagonist as ignorant as the audience** | The Walking Dead |

**37. Three network strategies and the "premise pilot trap"** (Blum ch. 17, in a 1999–2001 context; **the networks are dated, the mechanism still holds**): the original wording is that a premise pilot "is likely to be **a poor barometer** for predicting where the series will go." ① **NBC and ABC** — "the first half of the opening episode completes the setup of the premise, and the rest reflects a 'typical' plot"; ② **CBS** — "almost always refused a premise pilot in favour of a midstream one"; ③ the pure premise pilot, which reruns badly. **Smith ch. 20 agrees for half-hour comedy**: a premise pilot cannot demonstrate a normal episode — "that Martian won't crash-land in someone's yard every week"; **"most network executives prefer to see a typical-episode pilot."** **Landau's dissent**: it is not a trap, it only requires you to say which kind it is in the pitch.

**39. Douglas's four pilot steps** (3e ch. 4): "In the beginning is the world." — at the core is **a secret**, deep enough to take 88 or 100 hours to unwrap; **a character growth arc is usually not on the agenda on day one.** ① **Create the World** (Lindelof: **"the franchise is the world."**; "The question is who's going to be interacting with that world?" — Grey's and E.R. are "exactly the same show… only because of the people"); ② **Find the Story Springboards** ("This potential for future stories is the essential that differentiates writing a pilot from writing anything else on screen."); ③ **Populate the World** (use "draw out" rather than "introduce"; if you cannot name three, four or five leads, go back to step one; **beginners should not attempt a large ensemble**); ④ **Make a Plan** (**the first ten pages decide life or death**; accelerate the action until we think she is about to get there → **twist it, pull the rug**; **"do not describe the world in prose"** — "If you have to explain it, something's not alive here.").

**38. Three ways to close**: ① **"The end of your pilot is the beginning of your series."** (Landau) — "If you're still only talking about plot, you're already off-base."; ② **stick the landing** — "End your pitch of the pilot episode on a high note that portends future conflicts versus too much resolution and harmony."; ③ **a cliffhanger is not compulsory** (Miller ch. 6) — "Other shows work better with some resolution and acclimation to the hero's new life." (Masters of Sex vs. GoT / 24 / The Affair). Calvisi's balancing sentence: the ending must not pay everything off, or it will "feel too complete or 'closed-ended'—like a feature."

**40. Ten anxious questions** (Jeffries, the guest speaker in the same chapter, who teaches the pilot class at USC): **list at least ten questions gripping the audience at the moment the pilot ends** — an audience that thinks it knows the answers will not come back for episode two; the same ten questions also generate **ten short future-episode synopses.** She builds a 3–4 inch file of material for every pilot; the first note she ever receives is always "bring us into the heightened drama earlier" — **hook the executive and keep cards up your sleeve at the same time.**

## 5. Engine Breakdowns Case by Case (the Sopranos / Breaking Bad / Torchwood / Downton / Succession / Sakamoto)

**7. Story pattern = the shape that repeats every episode** (Rabkin ch. 8). Sons of Anarchy is not "about a motorcycle club," it is Jax protecting **three families**: sense a threat → move to protect → the blowback endangers a different family. Breaking Bad: "Walt has a problem. His solution works, but puts him in direct conflict with people more evil than him… it requires him to become more evil than his enemies." (Verified on S5E5 "Dead Freight"; S1E4 "Cancerman" does not fit the pattern and so "feels wrong – slow, dull, disjointed.") The sentence the author repeats seven times: **"Every episode feels like it's moving a story forward, but it's really just repeating a pattern that was set in the pilot."**
→ Write the pattern in one sentence and run it through completely in **the back half of the pilot** (the front half only sets up and triggers). The pattern has two layers: structural constants (three big fights and two small ones an episode on Martial Law; Law & Order handing off from the cops to the prosecutors at the midpoint) and the character pattern (the more important one).

**9. Concept strength does not determine sustainability: Torchwood is the disproof** (Rabkin ch. 9). Doctor Who's "Rose" (2005) and the Torchwood pilot are near-identical structurally, and on concept you would certainly bet on Torchwood ("an alien-crime police show" with endless cases) — yet the former reached its tenth season and the latter ended at four. The root cause: the disagreement between Torchwood's two leads is that "she thinks Torchwood can help people, and he **sort of agrees**"; alien threats are only **premises and pretexts, not stories** — "The story is what happens between the characters when they set out to deal with the alien menace." Doctor Who's conflict is alive inside the pilot's scenes (**he sees the macro, she sees the individual**). → **"I cannot think of three episodes after the pilot" is a disease of character conflict, not of concept.**

**26. Sakamoto's out-of-sync co-leads** (*I Fell in Love Like a Flower Bouquet*): value turns come not from opposition but from **consideration** (scene 85, drying her hair, he says "I've decided to look for a job"); each lead gets their own voice-over, and the film closes on a strictly symmetrical pair of monologues; four mirrored couples are written in (the one that broke first, the one that married first, the leads' own past, the leads' replacements), and **the largest dialogue budget goes to "the leads' own past"** (36 lines for the couple at the next table who appear only in the final scene, seven times the 5 lines given to the romantic rival).

**34. The three parts of a sustainable conflict web** (Blum ch. 17, test three): **"if all the leads of a serial love one another, all they can do is smile and agree with each other."** The engine = **a plot pattern of "group or family bonds" + personality attributes preinstalled in each character that must grind against the others + a coincidence or situation that makes them unable to leave each other**; missing any one of the three, it will not last three to five years. **Downton Abbey** is exactly this (Calvisi's Compelling Crisis: "the British aristocracy struggling to keep the estate and the old order under the pressures of the twentieth century, with the family tearing apart along the way") — class and the law of inheritance supply an inexhaustible friction surface. **Succession** needs no conflict designed on top, because seven or eight wants are put in one room; it adds three devices: **humiliation conducted downward** (Logan → Roman → Greg, which makes office politics generate scenes by itself); **giving each character a small want per episode that can be completed that week and that directly conflicts with someone else's**; and **compressing the whole family into one building and then cutting communications** (the phone pouches, the castle's phone check-in desk). The season 4 contrast: **Tom is the only character the scripts never give a need — "which is exactly why he can win."**

**41. The Sopranos' refusal to explain** (Chase's foreword p0012 plus the pilot): the pilot deliberately never explains Tony's formal position (only "The guy who says how things go is who I think I am," with Jackie Aprile never appearing) or how "waste management" works ("you don't need to know the details"). The rationale: **"rarely is anything black and white in life"** — he admired the foreign films of his youth "for not having the endings spelled out or telling the audience what to think or feel." **That is also why the pilot does not close on a cliffhanger**: every line "settles," yet the Junior/Livia conspiracy has only just germinated, and the last shot is a pool with no ducks in it. The networks passed not because of nudity, language or violence but because of "the details, the complexity, the different pacing" — **"network television is about people saying exactly what's on their minds. This show is about people acting in a passive-aggressive—or aggressive—way."**

**A model for choosing the starting point (Landau 2e ch. 2, item 7)**: the novel *The Queen's Gambit* begins with Beth already in the orphanage at eight; the Netflix version begins with **adult Beth waking hungover in a Paris hotel bathtub** — more dynamic, establishing the "damaged girl vs. international champion" gap immediately, with the gap itself triggering the first flashback; and inside that flashback **young Beth is present to witness her mother's death**, consolidating empathy.

## 6. The Character Web: the Role Bank, the Design Menu and Examples

**19. Every series is a family series** (Landau 2e ch. 6): **"Every television series is about a family – whether they're related by blood or not."** Quoting the first line of Tolstoy's *Anna Karenina*, the corollary is that **every family on television must be imperfect and discontented on some level.** Six kinds: the literal family drama (Succession, The Crown) / the workplace "family" (The Office, Ted Lasso) / the friend "family" (Friends, Insecure) / the hybrid (Better Call Saul, Pose) / the clan (Mare of Easttown, Gomorrah, Shtisel) / the **chosen family** (Pose, Vida).

**20. The familial-role casting bank** (same chapter): authority figures, siblings (rivalry and alliance), spouses, wise elders, prodigal sons and daughters, black-sheep outcasts, and **characters who behave like children.** You do not need a complete set of genders and ages; you need to settle "how each character will treat the others in both a positive and a negative direction." Law-enforcement partners (The Wire, True Detective S1) **inevitably behave like a bickering married couple sometimes**; workplace colleagues competing for the boss's favour must pass through the envy of sibling rivalry.

**23. Division of expertise** (Landau 1e Tip 20): different skill sets exist to **create conflict, present multiple POVs, and help the audience tell characters apart.** The Breaking Bad division table: Walt (chemistry = cooking) / Jesse (small-time dealer = distribution) / Saul (drug lawyer = laundering) / Mike (ex-cop = cleaner) / Skyler (bookkeeping = accounting) / Lydia (corporate executive = global logistics, purely to stay alive); Grey's Anatomy **defines its characters by specialty**; Mad Men's Harry Crane makes himself irreplaceable by "becoming the television expert before television became essential." Two companion rules: **House's principle of built-in disagreement** (he deliberately surrounds himself with doctors who disagree with him, and in season 4 rejects an applicant precisely because his thinking is too close to House's own); and the **Out of Their Element generator** (24's analyst Chloe forced into the field; The Wire's Prez, unfamiliar with field work, failing to identify himself and shooting a plainclothes officer dead).

**25. Complementary co-leads** (Landau 1e Tip 9): **"two incomplete people together you get one whole."** (Mulder/Scully, belief and doubt; Booth/Brennan, law and science; The Americans' Elizabeth loyal and Philip wavering.) **The Hacks template** (2e ch. 9): **"their antagonistic relationship is rooted in the fact that they need one another. What's keeping them from admitting their mutual need is their individual shame."** — what both do instead of dealing with shame is tell jokes. **Gomorrah's logline generator**: "but **what if your family is also the danger?**"

**27. Shame and voids** (Landau 2e ch. 7, citing Brené Brown's *Daring Greatly*): **shame = our fear of disconnection**; the chain is "connection requires being vulnerable → people tend to hide vulnerability → trauma creates **emotional voids** → voids produce coping mechanisms that avoid vulnerability." **Three classes of defence (the design menu)**: building walls (Shtisel, The Crown, Ozark) / numbing self-medication (Euphoria, The Queen's Gambit, Succession) / a defensive or self-destructive coping mechanism (Fleabag, Barry). **"Anyone who challenges any of these constructs becomes an antagonist."**

**29. The same backstory stated twice** (Landau 2e ch. 7, Mare of Easttown): in E3 she tells a visiting professor that her father "was my best friend. He passed away when I was thirteen. He was a detective…"; only in E6 does she tell the therapist the truth — "Well, he shot himself. I was thirteen years old." → **Give the edited version first, then the true version several episodes later in a safer or more coerced setting; the gap between them is the character's shame.** Ensemble balancing: the outsider is defined by shame too (the cold case Zabel "solved" was really someone else's work) — **"In this town, everyone is atoning for something."**

**31. Settle the nature of the antagonistic force before deciding whether it needs a face** (Landau 2e ch. 9): Chernobyl = the Soviet power structure; Fauda = **it depends on POV**; Le Bureau = the past; **Succession = greed**; Barry = PTSD; **Mare of Easttown and Fleabag = grief**; Hacks = the fear of obscurity; Pose = transphobia. The governing principle (footnoted to UCLA professor emeritus Howard Suber): **"All villains are antagonists, but not all antagonists are villains."** Oberg 3.1 adds: a plot-led show's antagonist should be strong, memorable and possibly charismatic (Negan, Gus Fring), but **the villain's dangerousness must not be a mystery** — only their nature may be.

**32. The villain is the hero of their own story** (same chapter): **"Every villain is the hero of his/her/their own story."** — "they usually feel justified, entitled, and downright righteous" (The Handmaid's Tale's Aunt Lydia sincerely believes she is helping "her girls" find redemption). Four riders: an antagonist usually wants what the protagonist has; they **almost always try to subvert the truth** in order to preserve the status quo (in Mare of Easttown the whole town conspires to keep the killer's secret); when tension flags, **strengthen the antagonist** (the more Barry resists the hitman's routine, the more bodies pile up); and the most iconic antagonists carry both positive and negative traits (Hannibal Lecter, gourmet and distinguished psychiatrist; Euphoria's Nate — "Hurt people hurt people."). The structural judgement: **"as soon as that primary goal is successfully attained, the series is usually over – unless it pivots to another overarching mission or goal."** A mismatch case: Rue treats her mother as the antagonist, but "in truth her mother is her chief ally… **Her addiction is her antagonist.**"

**33. Do not build straw men** (the Kings, Landau 2e ch. 4): "98 percent of TV is setting up straw men and straw women to be knocked down because everybody would rather enjoy propaganda than drama. **Propaganda is confirming what you already believe.**" What is worth far more is **regular characters we like disagreeing with each other, each with a real case** (The Good Wife S4E14 "Red Team, Blue Team" stages a mock trial inside the firm).

**22. Three scene-level rules** (same chapter): **"the people who love you most know how to hurt you the most."** → Before writing a two-hander, settle which of the other's buttons he knows, then decide whether he presses it. The new-character test — "ask yourself not only who this new character is, but also **how the arrival of this new character might impact your regular characters**." The subtext formula — **"The subtext of virtually any scene is familial power dynamics, psychological wounds from the past, and subtle movements that reaffirm clan position."**

**24. The rationality axis** (the Mooresville pitch in Rabkin's appendix): the female lead loathes all authority and everyone around her indulges her as a wilful child (love without respect); the male lead treats her like a CEO and argues when he disagrees, but **"start from the position that they are both rational, intelligent adults"** — "When Marshall treats her like an adult, she starts to act like one." → In a relationship show, the mutual attraction needs a **structural** reason, not just chemistry.

**30. The pacing of a flaw's revelation** (Landau 1e Tip 8): a flaw has to grow from backstory to run long, but in the pilot the backstory is only **hinted at obliquely** — "what we don't know about a character is often more compelling than what we do know." Don Draper is a philanderer in the pilot, and **only in season 6 do we learn his mother was a prostitute**; Scandal waits until episode 6 to reveal that Fitz's marriage is a political convenience. The counter-example: Hannah's OCD at the end of Girls season 2 "seemed to emerge from out of nowhere," and the audience pushed back.

**28. A grounded superpower is simultaneously a liability** (Landau 2e ch. 5, Gilligan's character-building method): Walt's superpower is his capacity for self-deception; Olivia Pope can fix everyone's problems but her own; Beth Harmon is a chess god and an addict. The 1e Tip 8 sidebar: **"when a character's greatest strength also becomes his or her greatest liability… The trick to dramatic sustainability is keeping your main character in this form of limbo."**

## 7. Long Quotations and the Tests in the Original Wording

**12. Extant's triple error** (same chapter; the textbook case of the premise trap): ① every question reduces to "how did she get pregnant," which has an answer within nine months at the outside; ② **the actions needed to answer those questions cannot generate interesting stories**; ③ the pilot **dramatizes the sex scene**, leaving only "when will the lie be exposed" as suspense. The verdict: **"execution is not the problem here. Conception is."** → Do not dramatize the central mystery inside the pilot.

**13. Story landmines** (Rabkin ch. 9) = character conflicts planted in the pilot's **subtext** that detonate several episodes later. The reasons: 60 pages cannot hold them; they would distract the audience; and **a show needs room to grow** — "if there is nothing in episode 35 that wasn't already fully explored in episode one, why would anyone bother with the 33 in between?" Positive examples: the Dalek in the museum in Doctor Who S1E6 (Rose sees a suffering individual, the Doctor sees the remnant of the most evil species); Crazy Ex-Girlfriend's Rebecca realizing at the end of the pilot that giving up everything for love "would be crazy" → her fear that she really is insane. → Plant one per main character (an unstated fear, desire or contradiction) and write down which episode it detonates; it has to be at the character level — "she is a detective's daughter" does not count.

**15. The sweet spot** (Landau 1e Tip 16, the sidebar at p0228): **"the intersection between the show's genre, tone, theme, central conflict, franchise, and central question/mystery"**; the one-hour version is **"a series must deliver the promise of its premise every week"** — "This is the tacit contract the showrunner is making with the audience." TWD straddles the fence between horror and family drama ("Remove the blood and guts and the show collapses. But if there were only gore, the show would also falter."); Homeland = duality. The 2e ch. 4 formula: **sweet spot = character development × franchise.** The companion concept, **currency**: Homeland's currency is not knowing whom to trust, TWD's is the survival of the core group — "spend it wisely."

**16. The marketing-intern test** (Rabkin ch. 10): play a junior marketing person trying to talk an *Entertainment Weekly* editor into a few column inches. "A caring doctor who heals patients by really listening" — "Um, well, it's really good…"; "King Arthur resurrected in modern-day London to battle Morgana Le Fay" — "I'll get that disc in the mail right away!" **Bigger does not mean a bigger budget, it means the scale of the conflict that defines the show** (the Ironside remake died after three episodes, up against The Blacklist that same season).

**17. "Big does not have to mean shallow."** Three ways to enlarge (same chapter): **change the profession** — the Sopranos began as Chase wanting to write about a **television writer** battered by the networks, tormented by an awful mother, walking into a psychiatrist's office after a panic attack; nobody wanted it, and **exactly one thing changed: the job**; **change the location** — The Bridge is a conventional two-detective partnership, and what matters is the US–Mexico border, a world never before on television; **change the era** — the same theme, having rejected the present day and his own adolescence, landed on a family drama of Cain and Abel ("that's when you know you've hit the thing you need to write"). The conclusion: **"the way to write for the market is to write the things that are most personal and most specifically unique to you."** The opposing position (Miller ch. 1–2): **"having great characters trumps high concept every time"** (Modern Family's concept is ordinary and it runs entirely on character; Gotham is high concept and mediocre), and he objects to "this-meets-that" pitching.

**48. "What's at stake?"** (Oberg glossary) = "the question development people ask most often when they don't care about your story": what happens if the main problem goes unsolved, and it must make someone **sad, anxious or frightened.** Two floors: **"What you really want to avoid is the situation where we don't know what's at stake in any given episode"**; and multiple lines must connect (theme-led by theme, plot-led by plot). Landau 1e Tip 11: **"Determine what they value most and put it at risk… a series with no suspense will be D.O.A."** For half-hours and relationship shows (2e ch. 9): **"the stakes aren't life or death, but they will ideally feel like life and death to the characters."** (Ted discovers his fiancée has never seen *Star Wars* — "If Stella doesn't like this movie, I can't marry her.": a trivial problem hung on a much deeper fear of intimacy.)

**8. Five tests for whether it has legs**

| Test | Source | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| 99 stories | Rabkin ch. 6 | If you cannot think of a second one, it does not work |
| **The six-episode test** | Landau 1e Tip 7 (p0122) | "at least **six** future episode ideas beyond the pilot"; if you cannot → "re-tool, revise, rejigger, and then re-launch" |
| Three ready for a pitch / five or six for a presentation | Landau 2e ch. 14 / Blum ch. 17 | "at least **three** examples" / "usually includes ideas for about **five or six** new stories" |
| **100 episodes and four questions** | Goldberg & Rabkin ch. 17 | What is the franchise? What are the central conflicts at the heart of the franchise? Are those conflicts complex and interesting enough to drive **100 or more episodes**? Is it fresh and different, and yet familiar enough to fit into mainstream television? |
| **The five-story test** | Blum ch. 17 | If five or six future stories are alike in **source** (the lead's private life / technology / a paid commission / a past institution / a public issue) and in **ending shape** (a rescue / a persuasion / a prevention / a compromise / an incomplete victory), the engine does not work |

**14. Suffering is not conflict; a person's two attributes must obstruct each other** (same chapter; the counter-example is Feed the Beast): **"suffering isn't conflict, and so suffering alone can't drive story"** (the wife dies in a drunk-driving crash and Tommy can only mope; had she gone missing he could search, were she dying he could fight, had she been murdered he could investigate); Dion is both a chef and an addict, but the two do not conflict (compare Vinyl's Richie, whose addiction fuels his creativity). Everything the show added later was **plot landmines rather than character landmines** (the lawyer is a detective's daughter, a pregnancy) → it degenerated into soap opera.

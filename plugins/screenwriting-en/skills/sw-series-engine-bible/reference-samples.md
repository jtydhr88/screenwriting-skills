## 10. Oberg's Twelve Bible Components With Specifications, and the Difference Between Bible, Pitch Deck and Format

**Source**: Emmanuel Oberg, *Writing a Successful TV Series* (2023, Screenplay Unlimited, volume 3 of the Story-Type Method), chapter 3 "Project Development," sections 3 First Up and 3.1 "Creating an Irresistible Bible" (the Bible Components subsection); terminology from the same book's glossary; the compressed version of the twelve specifications is rule 19 of the 20 operational rules at the end of chapter 3. **Year note**: this is the 2023 European/American streaming context — "streaming mostly goes straight-to-series," Netflix commissioning a single-camera comedy pilot for the first time in twelve years, Amazon letting viewers vote on Transparent, the rise of FAST channels: those **market facts will date**; the page counts and field specifications are craft and do not change with the year.

### 10.1 First, Separate Three Words: Bible / Pitch Deck / Format

The three are routinely used interchangeably in the industry. Oberg's approach is **to concede that there is no standard and then require you to ask, in the meeting, which one they mean** — "there is no industry standard, especially internationally … make sure that you clarify what they mean." (3 First Up)

| Word | Oberg's definition | What it contains | When it is used |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bible** | A document containing characters, setting, theme, tone, style, structure and story lines; used **both** to pitch producers, networks and studios **and** to guide writers and the showrunner in production (glossary §1) | Usually a logline, an introduction, episode summaries, breakdowns of the main characters, a description of the story world, and a vision for future seasons | Throughout; also called **format / book / TV treatment / pitch document / proposal** |
| **Pitch deck** | "**a more visual version of a bible**" (glossary §1); not a separate component but the visual version of the bible — **the same sections, less text, more images, often landscape** (Components §12) | Synopsis, characters, several plot scenes, target audience, production detail, the creative team | "A no-brainer" where there is a strong visual element (a comics adaptation, spectacular landscape, a distinctive style); may accompany a pilot script or a treatment |
| **Format** | **Three mutually distinct meanings** (glossary §1): (1) in industry speech it means only **episode length plus broad genre** (most often one-hour drama, half-hour comedy); (2) in adapting a foreign show or IP, the **underlying concept/rights** you buy — the US versions of The Office and The Bridge both bought the format from the British and Danish rights holders; (3) some people call a **pitching bible** a format and reserve "bible" for the continuously updated reference document after production begins | Depends on which meaning | Rule 1 of the book's 20 rules requires you to label three things as the first step of development: series type, story-type and **format (episode length plus broad genre)** |

**A physical example**: the benchmark pitch deck is the Duffer brothers' for **Montauk** (later Stranger Things) — with a moodboard and a list of inspirational films, set in the typeface of contemporaneous Stephen King novels to play the retro card; the distributor later released the Blu-ray in a fake VHS case, so **one visual strategy ran from the pitch all the way to selling it to the audience** (Components §12, glossary §1). Oberg also recommends studying six real pitch bibles: **Stranger Things, Fargo, Grey's Anatomy, Lost, New Girl, True Detective** (the introduction to 3.1).

**One more layer: the same bible exists in three forms (3 First Up)**, and you should settle which you are making before you write. ① **For a spoken pitch** (the typical American path: pitch → commission a pilot script → shoot the pilot → decide on a series, at which point **there may be no pilot script yet**): you need only the arcs of the first few seasons plus a few episode loglines; **the spoken version runs within 15–20 minutes**, and "anyone with no track record is strongly advised to write the pilot first and prepare a written leave-behind." ② **For straight-to-series** (common on streaming and in Europe): the first episode is usually already written on spec, a serial attaches a few seasons of arc, and **a synopsis (at minimum a logline) for every episode of season one**; the **written pitch bible runs 10–30 pages (excluding the script), sweet spot around 15.** ③ **The reference document after production begins**: grown out of the pitch bible into a living document, updated continuously by an assistant or coordinator, length not recorded — Oberg notes that many in the industry call **only** this a bible.

### 10.2 The Twelve Components With Specifications (key points as written)

(Source: 3.1 Bible Components. Items marked **hard spec** carry a number or a prohibition and can be used directly as a delivery checklist.)

| # | Component | Hard spec | How to write it (key points as written) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Series Title** | **≤3 words**; together with the author's name, **the only mandatory item in the whole document** | "an up-to-three-word pitch" — it does the first hooking on a wall at Netflix, next to one image; it should encapsulate tone and concept and **ideally imply the conflict.** Good examples: Sex Education, Breaking Bad, Occupied, Killing Eve, Stranger Things, Fleabag, Squid Game. **Ironic or punning titles are riskier**: Happy Valley (a valley awash in drugs), Transparent (trans parent), Life in Pieces. The title page may carry an epigraph or a table of contents |
| 2 | **Series Logline** | **three lines maximum** | For plot-led or character-led shows, state **who wants or needs what and why**, the obstacle, and the cost of failure; theme-led or multi-protagonist shows "often cannot," but it is still the core selling point at any stage. The physical companion (3.2 Killing Eve): the official logline, "…two fiercely intelligent women, equally obsessed with each other, go head to head in an epic game of cat and mouse." — containing genre, premise and franchise, original characters and abundant conflict, so that "**the DNA of the series lies in these few lines.**" |
| 3 | **Series Overview / One-Sheet** | **Two prohibitions**: ① it is **not a pilot synopsis**; ② **do not use vocabulary like story-type** | Write the world and the **story spine** (is it a quest? a transformative arc? a social problem?); it may include format (comedy/drama plus episode count and length) and series type; it may include an **opening statement** (the creator's personal connection to the material — "a discrete way to tie yourself to the show," which may stand as its own section); state time, place, genre, tone, theme, context and the main characters (briefly, castably). The reason for the vocabulary ban is that "readers with different theoretical frames will drag the discussion into a cul-de-sac" — the Stranger Things and Fargo bibles stay plain even where they touch on this |
| 4 | **Character Breakdowns** | **3–5 leads at a page each maximum, less for the rest** | Name plus approximate age plus want/need, motivation, background and principal traits — "**convey their essence**," unique yet relatable. The test: "there is no charismatic actor on the page to save you"; the aim is that **"we won't tire of seeing them week after week, even if they never change"**, achieved through **clarity, complexity and originality** |
| 5 | **Story World** | No page limit; **may be folded into the character section** | Give it its own section only when the world is complex or unusual — a sitcom may need only a description of the main settings; you may discuss rules, production design, costume, geography and scale. The boundary sentence: "**You're brushing the big picture, not briefing the art department.**" |
| 6 | **Pilot Story** | **a full synopsis, 3–5 pages** | **Tell the whole story**, not only the opening; adjust to local convention |
| 7 | **Arc of the First Season (and Possibly More)** | **The first-season arc is mandatory**; **each season arc must end on a hook or cliffhanger** (except procedurals, limited series and anthologies) | A serial may sketch what follows. **Unless you are adapting well-known IP, do not detail season two and beyond** — experienced showrunners often develop in blocks of 5–6 episodes to absorb change; casting and audience reaction redirect things (#JusticeforBarb affected Stranger Things season 2), and "**all you have is the current season**" (a 2023 example) |
| 8 | **Arc of the Series** | **Not needed** for procedurals or sitcoms; optional for serials | Writing one can display command, but "**Showing rigidity regarding what might happen in Season 5 … is more likely to trigger alarm bells.**" The alternative: rewrite it as "themes, characters and stories I would like to explore later" |
| 9 | **Tone, Genre and Style** | **The bible's prose must match the show's genre** (a comedy must be funny, a thriller must have moments of suspense) | A straightforward sitcom need not include this section; shows like The Office and Fleabag must explain **how** mockumentary and breaking the fourth wall generate comedy and drama. Target audience may go here — the US commonly segments kids 6–11, teens 12–17, adults 18–34 / 18–49 / 25–54 / 55+ (**2023 context; the segments shift with the market**). The closing line: "**Sell the sizzle, not the sausage.**" |
| 10 | **Episode Loglines or Synopses** | **A serial usually gives 8–15 loglines**; a mini or limited series may give as few as 2, with fuller synopses; a developed bible gives roughly **a page** of synopsis per episode (the Grey's Anatomy bible is the example); a sitcom or procedural gives only a few, to demonstrate the franchise | The warning: "**Having to write dozens of loglines or synopses might not be a good sign…**" — if the world and characters are rich enough and the franchise and springboard are clear, the reader can imagine infinite episodes unaided |
| 11 | **Package** | No page limit; **do not name actors** unless (1) you are certain you can get them and (2) it is a plus for this particular buyer | Producers, creator, showrunner, writers, cast, budget, visuals, moodboard and other non-story elements. Using a well-known actor as a **"casting example"** to aid imagination is fine |
| 12 | **Pitch Deck** | Not a separate component | See 10.1 |

### 10.3 Overall Thresholds and Why Not to Write Too Much

- **The whole document is 10–30 pages, sweet spot around 15** (3 First Up, form ②); the spoken version runs **within 15–20 minutes**, and elsewhere the book gives the dual formulation "a 15–25 minute spoken pitch, or a written bible / pitch deck" (the note to the Components subsection).
- The **mechanical reason** not to fill it in too completely (question 9 of 3.1, "Are You Overplanning or Boxing Yourself In?"): you do not know how long it will run, whom the audience will love, or what chemistry the actors will have (**Jesse Pinkman was originally meant to die in season one**, and the Cranston/Paul chemistry changed Gilligan's plan). The quota sentence: **"Put 90% of your energy into making the best first season … and 90% of that energy into making your pilot the most exciting episode."** Another operates at the character level (question 8 of 3.1): **do not imply that we already know where a character's arc ends** (limited series excepted) — "**In series, often characters don't change that much, it's how the audience perceives them that changes.**"
- How the twelve components complement **Rabkin's ten sections** (section 8) and **Smith's eleven** (section 9): Oberg alone has **Series Title ≤3 words**, **Package**, **Pitch Deck** and **Arc of the Series**; Rabkin alone has **the adversary's viewpoint as its own section** and the **two-column loss list**; Smith alone has **market analysis**, **budget summary**, **unique aspects**, **creator credits** and **closing sell copy**. All three share only **title / logline (Rabkin has no logline section) / overview / characters / pilot story / episode loglines or springboards** — **those six are the minimum deliverable set.**

### 10.4 Rule 19 of the Book's 20 Chapter-3 Rules (the one-line compression of the bible spec, transcribed as written)

> Bible: title ≤3 words, logline ≤3 lines, an overview that neither restates the pilot nor uses story-type vocabulary, 3–5 leads at ≤1 page each, a pilot synopsis of 3–5 pages, a mandatory first-season arc ending on a hook, only a direction for season two onward, 8–15 episode loglines for a serial; 10–30 pages overall (sweet spot 15); prose matching the show's genre.

**The prerequisite rule that must be read with it** (rule 1 of the 16 at the end of the glossary volume): **the first step of development is to label three things — series type, story-type and format (episode length plus broad genre); if any one of the three is vague, do not write an outline yet**, let alone a bible. The reason is item 46 in section 3 of reference.md: **"The wrong combination of series type and story-type is the root cause of many series failures."**

## 11. Goldberg & Rabkin's Two Sets of Writers' Guidelines (the mini-bible sent to outside writers)

**Source**: Lee Goldberg & William Rabkin, *Successful Television Writing* (2003), Appendix B *Diagnosis Murder* Writers' Guidelines (**p0155–p0158**) and Appendix C *Martial Law* Writers' Guidelines (**p0159–p0168**); the methodology precedes them in Appendix A *Plotting a Mystery* (**p0149–p0153**). **Year note**: this is the 2003 American broadcast one-hour context — the fairness argument framed around VHS, the four-act commercial format, the economics of shooting days and standing sets, the "$7.50 movie ticket" all belong to that year; the document shape of **tone statement + prohibition list + character tension web** still holds.

**What these are**: not selling documents but **the documents a show already in production sends to freelancers invited to pitch**, and the only physical **mini-bible** in this file (compare the page table in section 1: a mini-bible is about 5 pages; DM is four, ML is ten, and **ML is longer because it is a season-two reboot**). Compared with Rabkin's Mooresville pitch in section 8, **their fields barely overlap** — a pitch document sells possibility, guidelines sell executable boundaries.

### 11.1 The Two Documents' Sections Compared

| Section | *Diagnosis Murder* (structure: basic principles → characters → the two hardest scene types → the bottom line) | *Martial Law* (structure: positioning statement → principles list → character overview → character detail → what they share) |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive positioning** | "**We are a mystery series. We always have a murder.** And usually there is a medical clue that plays a major role in solving the crime. **But that's it. Beyond that, we have no formula.**" | "action-adventure comedy," delivering the thrills "until now, you had to pay $7.50 to see" |
| **Negative positioning** | It does not want to be **Murder, She Wrote**; the bottom-line section says it no longer wants to be "an old, tired Murder, She Wrote knockoff — and people were right to see it that way back then" | "**This is not a cop show.**"; "Our stories are not about crimes, suspects, and clues, they are about **fun, comedy, and pure adrenaline**." |
| **Tone reference points** | **not recorded** (only negative references given) | An homage to the 1960s — **The Wild Wild West, I Spy, The Avengers, The Man from U.N.C.L.E.** — plus a contemporary turn and "the fights only Sammo Hung and Stanley Tong can deliver"; the best of Hong Kong's wild action **plus the character comedy of 48 Hours and Lethal Weapon** |
| **Continuity / reboot statement** | A statement about the star: Dick Van Dyke is highly versatile, and using that is encouraged | "**last season didn't exist.**" No backstory carries over; **only the central concept remains**: a policeman from China working at the LAPD through an exchange programme |
| **Principles list** | Fairness (see item 1 of 11.2); per-season quotas and variations; preferred kinds of clue | Ten: start hot / heroes in conflict / stakes / no formula for the fights / a big turn at the end of Act Two / crimes must not be dark / take Sammo out of his own milieu / Terrell never gets his gun kicked away again / do not rely on forensics / not every villain knows martial arts |
| **Structural requirement** | Each season needs a few classic whodunits ("a murder, four suspects, one of them did it") plus a few **Columbo-style open mysteries**, **each of which must find a distinctive twist to avoid formula** | "**Each story should have a major turn at the end of Act Two**—and if you can find major turns for the ends of Acts One and Three, we won't object."; "**If we know the whole story in Act One, then it's not a story we should be doing.**" |
| **Production parameters** | Scripts run **68–70 pages**; **Mark Sloan must not appear beyond pages 32–35** (Van Dyke's working schedule); **7 shooting days**; at least **2–3 days on standing sets** (the hospital, the police station, Mark's beach house); action is welcome but there is no budget for it | **not recorded** (this document gives no page count or shooting days, only equivalent prohibitions: crimes must not be dark, not every villain knows martial arts) |
| **Character section** | Three, one paragraph each (Mark / Amanda / Jesse), each with a profession, a family tie and one emotional gap | Two layers: a **character overview** (four people plus the unit's positioning) and **The Nitty-Gritty character detail** (a long paragraph each, including a list of cultural assumptions and a comparison table) |
| **The two hardest scene types** | Yes, in two separate sections: **Cat-and-Mouse Scenes** and **Our Big Finales** (11.4) | **not recorded** (the equivalent content is folded into the "no formula for the fights" and "heroes in conflict" principles) |
| **Closing** | **The Bottom Line**: two seasons of effort to change industry and public perception, and it worked (good press, **a top-thirty ratings position**) | **What they share**: "Despite their differences, they genuinely like and respect each other, and together make an extraordinary team." |

### 11.2 The "Never Do This" List (thirteen prohibitions, each as written)

This is what distinguishes guidelines from a pitch bible: **a pitch bible says what the show can have, guidelines say what it will never have.**

| # | Prohibition | Source | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Never hide a clue from the audience**; Mark never finds evidence off screen or during a commercial | DM | "**Assume the viewers are taping the show and when it's over, will rewind the tape to see what clues they missed.**" (a VHS-era framing; it survives translation to pause and rewind). The note on the reverse in Appendix A: on television "most mysteries … do it all the time," because cheating is easier |
| 2 | **Never** catch anyone with fingerprints, DNA or "easy forensic stuff" | DM; ML agrees | Prefer to convict on **behaviour, habit and personal quirk**; "Usually it's a combination of a medical clue, a behavior, and a mistake." |
| 3 | **Do not resurrect old bits from The Dick Van Dyke Show or his films** | DM | This is a new character |
| 4 | **Do not let a cat-and-mouse scene slide into imitating Columbo** | DM | Mark has his own style and humour — "**he's Dick Van Dyke, after all**" |
| 5 | **The big finale must not be a Mark speech**; and no "What are you talking about?" / "You're crazy" / "This is ridiculous" used as filler | DM | Those lines "reveal nothing about character, advance nothing in the story, and do not shake Mark in the slightest" |
| 6 | **"Terrell will never, ever have a gun kicked out of his hand again."** | ML | Do not elevate the martial artist by making the other leads look stupid, weak or incompetent |
| 7 | **Crimes must not be ugly or dark** — this is not a show about children killed in drive-bys or pregnant women raped | ML | "**This show is an escape, not a dark reality. If it's not fun, we don't do it.**" |
| 8 | Stakes must not rest on **"family or friends as victims or villains,"** nor on someone being taken and facing death every week | ML | "**That's the easy way out.**" (But every story must give the protagonist a stake) |
| 9 | **No formula for the fights**: not one at the end of every act, not necessarily ending on a fight, not necessarily closing every episode with Sammo against the villain; **the heroes can lose, and should** | ML | Let the story decide where the fight is, with whom, and how long; it must never be "irrelevant, tacked on or obligatory" |
| 10 | **Do not put Sammo back in Chinatown** | ML | Helping Chinese people in Chinatown "isn't funny and isn't fresh"; **helping Hasidic Jews in the Fairfax district is far more interesting than helping a family who run a Chinese restaurant** |
| 11 | **Not everyone in Los Angeles knows martial arts — especially the villains** | ML | "**He's only interesting bare-handed against a bad guy with an Uzi, a bomb or a tank.**" |
| 12 | **Sammo uses neither handcuffs nor a gun** | ML | Substitutes: thumbs bound with a rubber band, a stick winding up all four limbs, someone wrapped around a lamp post; **minimum necessary force** — "the Chinese MacGyver of self-defense" |
| 13 | **"If the story you have in mind can be done on any other show, then it's dead wrong for Martial Law."** | ML | The general principle behind every prohibition: **interchangeability = disqualification** |

### 11.3 The Protagonist's "Licence to Intervene," Written as an Institution

The test in item 49 of section 3 (Miller) is **"A character's agency completes the story engine"** — a reporter can meddle but holds **no licence to intervene.** What makes these two sets of guidelines valuable is that they **write that licence as a checkable set of job arrangements**:

- ***Diagnosis Murder***: **Dr. Mark Sloan** = head of internal medicine at Community General Hospital **plus an LAPD consultant**; his son **Steve** (a homicide detective) **lives with him in the Malibu beach house**; **Dr. Amanda Bentley** = a pathologist and **assistant county medical examiner** who **often reaches the crime scene before Mark or Steve**; **Dr. Jesse Travis** = an ER resident, Mark's "second son," and "an apprentice at solving crimes." → Three things are institutionalized simultaneously: ① the protagonist can legitimately be present (the consultancy); ② bodies and medical evidence legitimately flow into his hands (the ME's office); ③ police authority lives in the same house (father and son cohabiting). So the "medical clue" is not coincidence but a by-product of the job — the companion principle in Appendix A: **"The medical clue comes out of character."**
- ***Martial Law***: **Sammo Law** = a Shanghai police officer **seconded to the LAPD through an exchange programme**, attached to an elite major crime unit "that handles high-profile and international cases and operates **outside the LAPD bureaucracy**"; the unit exists "for people who don't fit in anywhere else in the department, but are **too damn good to let go**," and **does not merely react but prevents** — undercover work, stings, traps. → The licence delivers three things: ① he can turn up on any case; ② he can ignore procedure; ③ **he can go looking for trouble** (the thing procedurals most often lack: why the protagonist does not simply wait for a case). A bonus: the exchange-programme status makes him **permanently a fish out of water.**

**An executable test**: break the protagonist's licence into four questions — **① what makes their presence legitimate? ② what makes evidence or cases flow to them? ③ what lets them ignore procedure? ④ what lets them take the initiative rather than wait?** If any one of the four can only be answered by coincidence, the engine is missing a part.

### 11.4 The Two Hardest Scene Types Each Week, and How DM Says to Write Them

- **Cat-and-mouse scenes** (Mark interrogating a suspect, the investigative scene) = "**the hardest scene of every episode and usually the best.**" Mark has to extract a great deal of information while doing it "**almost effortlessly, without Mark seeming to 'question' the person**" — the other person must give it up **without knowing they are being interrogated**; he never resembles an accuser or an interrogator, **only an interested person.** This is "Mark's charm and his greatest investigative weapon" (the prohibition is item 4 of 11.2).
- **Our big finales** (laying out the clues at the end) are "always hard to write," but should be the show's **most satisfying scene** — Mark enjoys them, and "it's probably the reason why he's a detective at all." The method: deliver the clues and the explanation **dramatically (or comically)**; the core sentence is **"We need to make these finales a dialogue, not a speech. Mark is talking with, not to, the suspect."**; the concrete means is **giving the killer an attitude** — rebutting the accusation, pointing out holes in Mark's reasoning, so that Mark then out-argues him; otherwise "it's just a lecture."

### 11.5 An Eight-Section Template Extracted From These Two Documents (a synthesis by this pack's notes, not a table of contents in the book)

① **Positive positioning plus negative positioning**; ② **tone reference points**; ③ **a bespoke prohibition list** (11.2); ④ **structural requirements**; ⑤ **production parameters**; ⑥ **characters plus the two-way tension vector of every pair**; ⑦ **how to write the two hardest scene types** (11.4); ⑧ **the bottom line and the invitation.** The most worth copying is section 6's **two-way vector** — write one "envies / cannot stand" line per relationship and **write both ends** (Grace envies Amy's political smoothness, Amy envies Grace's ability to blend into any setting, which she cannot **"because she is all cop"**; the Sammo↔Terrell comparison table closes on "**Terrell improvises / Sammo plans**"), while making clear that **the disagreements are not personal** (the paradigms being Kirk/Spock/McCoy and Murtaugh/Riggs). → Read against Landau's familial-role casting bank in section 6 and Rabkin's "It Goes the Other Way, Too …" in section 8.

## 12. Miller's Field Matrix Across Six Student Treatments

**Source**: Kam Miller, *The Hero Succeeds* (2016, v1.2, self-published), appendix, Treatments subsection **L8197–9624** (Jerusalem, TN **L8200** / Girl Out of Water **L8393** / Minotaur **L8601** / The Guardian **L8730** / Exit Stage Left **L9085** / B-List **L9333**); the template itself is the **10 components** of chapter 2 (L0623–641), and the Egri form is at L0785–850. **Year note**: a 2016 American context — the platform list in Markets and the market evidence in relevance will date; the field structure does not. **The disclaimer is itself the methodology**: "**Treatments and outlines are living, breathing documents** … **That's the way it's supposed to work.**" Also: **a treatment is not a bible** — a treatment runs 10–15 pages (8–10 for a sitcom), while a bible runs 70–100 and is a paid item under the WGA MBA.

**Conclusion (the delivery floor)**: only five elements appear in all six — **type of show / logline / world / characters / story engine**; tone, theme, season plan and the six questions are what you thicken as the project requires. *The Guardian* lets world-building swallow theme, engine and tone (Miller's comment is precisely that it "skillfully focuses on world-building") — **a fantasy show may hide the engine inside the rules of its world, but then it must write the engine into the world**: its engine is that a guardian dies and is reborn to maintain the balance, that **seeker crystals** in the towers of each country catch a beam from the sky to locate them, that recognition is by the eyes, and that destructive magic **costs health and even life.**

### 12.3 *Jerusalem, TN*: Two Story Models Plus a Three-Season Capability-Growth Table

The general formula: "The story in this show comes from **collisions between institutions and individuals.**" The institutions are the church, the SBC, a factory farm, a nascent cult, and the town itself. **Model 1** = Luke or Ann feels threatened by an institution, or sees that an institution can give them what they need, and acts; **model 2** = a conflict between two institutions puts the protagonist **right in the middle** ("solve it, inflame it, or be destroyed by it"). An **emotional anchor character** is also designated: Kacey "is **the human center of the show**."

| Season | Plot scale | Can | Cannot / the cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | The private consequences of the power plus small-town politics; the impending closure of the factory farm is the main driver; followers coalesce into a cult by the finale | Heal wounds, illness and disability; walk on water | **Gains no new powers, only intensified old ones**; early on it takes an emotional crisis to trigger, and it is "unpredictable and unreliable," becoming usable at will by the finale |
| S2 | The SBC absorbs them; Nick seizes control of the cult and **Luke is demoted to figurehead** | Feed the whole cult, drive off a tornado, emit a messianic radiance | At the season's end he tries to resurrect his wife — "He succeeds in the darkest possible way, in a way he regrets deeply the moment it happens." |
| S3 | A time jump; the ATF besieges the compound, and **the whole season is that siege**; the three leads fail to defuse it | Only heals those who can be healed | **The power matters least**: "his powers are simply not of that nature" |

### 12.4 *Girl Out of Water*: Egri Three Dimensions Plus a Signature-Trait Form

**The form**: three paragraphs per person — **physiology / sociology / psychology** — closing on a single line, "Signature trait:". The full Egri character bone-structure field list is at L0785–850 (three physiological items / nine sociological / eleven psychological). The way the signature trait is posed: **"what does this lead do every week that the audience will love watching?"** — with the note that "not every lead has one."

| Character | Signature trait |
|---|---|
| Sierra (18, the lead) | "**Sierra is a winner in a losing game. She is a fighter.**" |

**Three other cells**: the **World is only three lines** ("Sierra's school and family home, as well as the offices of her physical therapist and psychologist."); the **engine is one sentence with four sources** ("sports and sporting events, the mental-health system, business ventures, and family"); and the **three-season arc advances three character lines at once each season** (Sierra / Felicity / Steven-Maria), flipping a state at each season's end (S1 an attempted suicide; S2 a patent lawsuit; S3 **Maria discovers the cancer has returned and conceals it**).

### 12.5 *Minotaur*: The Only Markets / Platform Analysis

**Key points as written**: the best fit is **FX** — it already has "moody, violent, prestige" shows like *American Horror Story*, *The Americans*, *Sons of Anarchy*, *Justified* and *Fargo*, **but no superhero show**, exactly the gap; and it reruns *Buffy the Vampire Slayer*, which is not superhero but close in protagonist and story structure. **HBO** would also work (likewise no superhero show, and committed to sex, violence and prestige); **Showtime, A&E, even NBC** are possible.

→ **The argument formula = a list of the target platform's existing tone + the gap it currently has + fallback platforms**, and it **never uses a ratings figure.** That is the answer to the "whether to write market analysis" cell in 9.4: if the numbers are not strong, omit it; if you write it, write **the platform's gap.** Two other things worth copying from this sample: **the opening enters the story instead of naming fields** ("The minotaur waits in the center of the labyrinth… **Leon will not survive.**"), and the **engine = four sources plus a weekly-promise sentence** ("Every week, we get to see Leon as the Minotaur…").

### 12.6 *The Guardian* and *Exit Stage Left*

*The Guardian* runs **world first → subsections by country → a separate Magic section → characters last**; a model of three-dimensional relationship-building: Myron has a knife scar on his face left when **Sargon** refused to join, **Sargon is his brother**, and **Terra is his niece.** *Exit Stage Left* writes its **engine in two layers**: "**Each new show brings a fresh round of building/directing/prop-finding challenges.**" plus Georgia balancing the theatre against her biology coursework; and it **sells time compression as a feature**: "the same students would be in college for between six and eight TV seasons." Miller points out that what it lacks is precisely **an ongoing character conflict** and contemporary social relevance.

### 12.7 How to Fill In the Six Questions

**Unique** states the difference against precedents; **research** names concrete findings **and explains how they are planted** (*B-List* researched three Roger Corman tactics — provocative titles and posters / reused sets and small budgets / hiring talented young actors by offering them experience and lead roles); **influences** says what is borrowed from each; **why me** supplies a viewpoint nobody else has **and concedes the subject's difficulty** (*Jerusalem, TN*: "**I stand at a distance from faith, but I am not alienated by it or embittered toward it.**").

### 12.8 *B-List*: 14 Episode Loglines Instead of a Season Plan

Miller's own words: **"He knocked it out of the park with his proposed episode loglines. They made me laugh, and I totally got Henry's show."** — this is the **filled-in sample** for the cell in 9.3 (Smith's 8–10 springboards), and the only evidence for "a comedy may substitute 13–14 episode loglines for a season plan."

### 13.1 Why This Table Is Sustainable and Not Disposable

1. **Seven wants collide directly in pairs, so no extra conflict has to be designed.** Item 6 of the S1 notes, as written: **"Give each main character a small want each episode that can be completed that week, and make it conflict directly with someone else's; an ensemble show needs no extra conflict designed, only seven wants put in one room."**
2. **Every want demands the same indivisible thing** (Logan's chair), so **there is no win-win solution**; Shiv's want is self-contradictory in itself (win on her own merits outside the family, and keep the Roy leverage), so she must stand on both sides forever.
3. **The unfilled needs guarantee that nobody exits by getting their want.** The S1 need column records what the show thinks each lacks: Logan = admitting he will die / Kendall = existing without his father's approval / Roman = growing up / Shiv = picking a side / Tom = admitting he wants power and not love / Greg = admitting he is in fact very calculating / Connor = accepting that he is not at the table — **not one of the seven is satisfied, so the wants can run unchanged for another season.**
4. **"Unable to leave each other" is written physically.** The S1 pressure-chamber episodes (E2 the hospital, E5 the Thanksgiving apartment, E8 the warehouse party, E9/E10 the castle) each come with a communications restriction (**phone pouches**, a phone check-in desk plus a signal blocker plus falconry), the pattern as written: **"restrict the flow of information first, then make information travel by body."** S4 upgrades this to the container episode: 8 of 10 episodes compress the whole episode into a container with a fixed procedure, with **the procedure's checkpoints replacing act breaks.**
5. **The chain of conducted humiliation generates scenes by itself.** Item 13 of the S1 notes: **"humiliation must be conducted downward — anyone who receives a humiliation must pass it to someone weaker in the next scene (Logan → Roman → Greg)."**

### 13.2 When This Web Stops Working: the Engine Pressure-Loss Test

Season 4 did not end because the characters were used up — **"the motor in the room was still running hot"** — but because **"the big plot engine, the brutal materialist plumbing which ran under the show, was looking to me like it would eventually start to lose pressure"** (the S4 foreword). Three supporting pieces of evidence: ① the outside world supplied a deadline (legacy media against tech "**was not a fair fight, it was a rout**"; Redstone's death, Murdoch's retreat — **2023 industry facts, which will date**); ② one of the four immovable anchors is at the **level of the world**: "the GoJo deal **must close**," on the grounds that "**That was the case in the world, so it had to be the case for us.**"; ③ the ending is derived from the logic of the world rather than the characters' wishes — the children "arrive at the wrong time."

→ **How to use this table fully**: after filling in the seven rows, ask the S4 question — **"in which season does the material engine driving these seven wants begin to lose pressure?"** However well the seven wants interlock, once the engine loses pressure you can only change engines or end (compare rule 10 of SKILL.md and exercise 11).

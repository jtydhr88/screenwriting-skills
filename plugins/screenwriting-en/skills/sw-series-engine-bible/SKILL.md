# Series Engine and Series Bible

This skill covers four layers: **the engine** (what guarantees a story every week), **the character web** (where conflict generates itself), **pilot types and strategy**, and **the documents** (logline → pitch → treatment → bible).

**Not covered here**: act structure, page counts, act outs and cliffhanger taxonomy for the episode and the season (→ `sw-series-structure`); room process and pitch-meeting etiquette (→ `sw-writers-room`); joke mechanics and character configuration in half-hour comedy (→ `sw-sitcom-comedy`); mainland-Chinese project proposals, episode outlines, episode counts and censorship (→ `sw-chinese-series-practice`). Medium-independent material such as premise, controlling idea, three-dimensional character and dialogue is not rewritten here (→ `sw-premise-theme`, `sw-character-conflict`).

Reference material is split across three files: engine breakdowns, series types and character webs in [reference.md](reference.md); the full field tables for the document templates (Rabkin's ten-section pitch document, Smith's eleven-section series format, Oberg's twelve bible components) in [reference-documents.md](reference-documents.md); filled-in samples (two sets of writers' guidelines, Miller's six-treatment matrix, the Succession table of seven wants) in [reference-samples.md](reference-samples.md).

## 1. Defining the Engine

**1. Franchise = concept + conflict + theme + story pattern** (Rabkin ch. 5, ch. 8). He judges his own 2011 seven-item list (character, setting, story type, dialogue style, mode of interaction, narrative style, central conflict) to be like describing an elephant as "grey-skinned, long-nosed, big-eared": a fine checklist for writing a story on an existing show, but **no help in building your own engine.**

**2. Theme may not be one word; it must be an arguable opposition**: "There is no such thing as a one-word theme for a television series." (same chapter) The Shield — "how much evil can a good man do in pursuit of good before he becomes the evil he fights?"; Mad Men — "a man whose trade is selling the idea that consumption brings happiness, while knowing it does not hold in his own life"; Lost — "Faith versus reason."

**3. Theme must be embodied as conflict between characters** ("TV doesn't handle abstract concepts too well"). The Shield's minimum configuration: a good man who can fight the evil + an evil + **other good men who fight the evil by conventional means and fail** (otherwise the audience cannot see why the protagonist adopts his enemy's methods). Concept is only a container and is replaceable (the concept of Lost came from an ABC executive).

**4. The interlock test** (Rabkin ch. 6): "Quick, come up with a story that naturally explores both concept and conflict. Now come up with 99 more." The counter-example is **Agents of SHIELD**: its one central conflict (Skye opposing centralized authority while enjoying being inside SHIELD) lives inside a single person and has nothing to do with the concept — "you can feel its emptiness." The author had the same disease on NBC's first season of **Baywatch** (a concept with no conflict and no theme, so "every week you are reinventing the franchise").

**5. The analogy is not the novel, it is jazz improvisation** (Rabkin ch. 4): conflict and theme are the repeating chord changes; each episode is a new melody improvised over them.

**6. Five industry phrasings of the same thing**
- Landau 2e ch. 4: a pilot is a **tacit contract** with the audience, each episode "fundamentally the same – only different"; when an executive asks "What's the franchise?" they are asking **what the protagonist does every week.** The 1e Tip 3 formula for a soft-concept show: **"Each week, the characters will struggle to achieve ______."**
- Goldberg & Rabkin ch. 2: **"It has to be the same show every week, and yet at the same time, it has to be new, fresh, and different."** A franchise is **a rule set that licenses invention.** The counter-example is **Stingray**, which changed genre every week; one showrunner's verdict was "It's got no franchise," and it was cancelled in two months.
- Douglas 3e ch. 1: a franchise is "both a boundary and an opportunity"; every episode is started by a **springboard** — case-of-the-week shows have one built in (a crime, a client, a patient), while family, workplace, school and romance shows must be started by **a personal (possibly internal) inciting incident.**
- Oberg 3.1: **"A series isn't a story that brings answers. It's a story engine that generates questions."** A film is an express train, a series a stopping service — but each episode must still deliver a feature's worth of plot.
- Miller ch. 1 / Calvisi §9: the engine depends on a character having **licence or agency to intervene in other people's lives** (able to arrest, to defend, to treat), or on a unifying situation that pulls new characters into the protagonist's world; Calvisi's two mandatory fields are **Compelling Crisis** and **Week-to-Week** (a network always asks "what does he DO each week?"). Blum ch. 17 gives the economic reason: a series has to stay on air three to five years before it returns anything financially.

**7. Story pattern = the shape that repeats every episode** (Rabkin ch. 8): Sons of Anarchy is not "about a motorcycle club," it is Jax protecting **three families** — sense a threat → move to protect → the blowback endangers a different family. → Write the pattern in one sentence and run it through completely in **the back half of the pilot** (the front half only sets up and triggers). (The original wording of Breaking Bad's pattern, its two layers of structural constant and character pattern, and the counter-examples are in reference.md §5.)

**8. Five tests for whether it has legs**: 99 stories (Rabkin ch. 6) / **the six-episode test** (Landau 1e Tip 7) / have three ready for a pitch and five or six for a presentation (Landau 2e ch. 14 / Blum ch. 17) / **100 episodes and the four questions** (Goldberg & Rabkin ch. 17) / **the five-story test** (Blum ch. 17). (Thresholds, original wording and sources for all five are tabulated in reference.md §7.)

> ⚠️ **Limited series, mini-series and closed long-form told once do not run this test.** The 100 episodes and "can it last five seasons" are tests from the renewal economics of American broadcast networks; a closed county, one office, a single cold case cannot reach 100 episodes by the nature of the genre, and applying this literally will reject qualifying projects. Closed long-form uses **the engine pressure-loss calculation in rule 10** instead: work out at which episode the material engine loses pressure, and that episode is where the show should end — the episode count is then **argued for**, not accepted. Pick one of the two tests by medium and commissioning model.

**9. Concept strength does not determine sustainability: Torchwood is the disproof** (Rabkin ch. 9): on concept alone you would bet on Torchwood, and yet it ran four seasons while Doctor Who reached its tenth — alien threats are only **premises and pretexts, not stories.** → **"I cannot think of three episodes after the pilot" is a disease of character conflict, not of concept.** (The full case is in reference.md §5.)

**10. Engine pressure loss** (the Succession S4 foreword): to judge whether a show should end, look at the **material plot engine** (the deal, the empire, the job), not at whether the characters still have material — Armstrong ended the show not because the characters were used up ("the motor in the room was still running hot") but because "**the big plot engine, the brutal materialist plumbing which ran under the show**, was looking to me like it would eventually start to lose pressure."

> How to use it: this rule is a **substitute** for rule 8, not a supplement. An indefinitely renewable show uses rule 8 (can it run another 100 episodes); closed long-form and limited series use this one (at which episode does the engine lose pressure). **Determine medium and commissioning model first, then pick the test.**

**One sentence on each of four sample engines** (detail in reference.md §5): **the Sopranos** = the therapy room plus two families (the ethical ground rule Melfi lays down in the first scene — that she must report a murder — is itself the engine); **Fleabag** = a character web already busy hurting one another plus one device (talking to camera); **Sakamoto's *Flower Bouquet*** = the two leads' wants falling out of sync across five years, with no antagonist anywhere; **Succession** = "succession," a problem that can never be solved.

## 2. Writing the Engine as a Sentence

**11. The central question must be a process sentence** (Rabkin ch. 3): **"Pilots need to raise questions that lead to processes, not solutions."** A film asks "Who killed Marion Crane?"; a series asks: how does the Earl of Grantham keep the estate inside an economy that no longer suits it (Downton)? How does a mild chemistry teacher survive the violent drug trade (Breaking Bad)? Can Richie Finestra revive rock before drink and drugs kill him (Vinyl)? → Write it as "How will X keep doing Y in a world that Z?"

**12. Do not dramatize the central mystery inside the pilot** (same chapter; Extant's triple error is the textbook case): the verdict is **"execution is not the problem here. Conception is."** (The three errors are itemized in reference.md §7.)

**13. Story landmines** (Rabkin ch. 9) = character conflicts planted in the pilot's **subtext** that detonate several episodes later. → Plant one per main character (an unstated fear, desire or contradiction) and write down which episode it detonates; it has to be at the character level — "she is a detective's daughter" does not count. (The three reasons, plus the Doctor Who and Crazy Ex-Girlfriend positive examples, are in reference.md §7.)

> **The conflict between landmine count and "one overarching mystery," and the fix.** Eight main characters = eight landmines, which in a long-form Chinese series is eight concurrent mystery lines — and that system treats concurrent mysteries as a disease (see the "one problem" principle and the *Huangchenggenr* counter-example in `sw-chinese-series-practice`). The fix is not to reduce the landmines but to **change how they hang**: hang every landmine beneath **a single overarching mystery** as its layers, reveal one layer an episode, and immediately add a new time-limited hook each time you reveal one. The audience is then always chasing one question while you still hold eight timed charges. When laying out the schedule, give each landmine two episode numbers: the episode it detonates, and which layer of the overarching mystery it peels away.

**14. Suffering is not conflict; a person's two attributes must obstruct each other** (same chapter; the counter-example is Feed the Beast): **"suffering isn't conflict, and so suffering alone can't drive story."** (The Tommy/Dion breakdown and how the show slid into soap opera are in reference.md §7.)

**15. The sweet spot** (Landau 1e Tip 16) = the intersection of genre, tone, theme, central conflict, franchise and central question/mystery; the 2e ch. 4 formula is **sweet spot = character development × franchise**, and the one-hour version is **"a series must deliver the promise of its premise every week"** — which is the tacit contract. (The original wording, the TWD and Homeland examples, and the concept of currency are in reference.md §7.)

**16. The marketing-intern test** (Rabkin ch. 10) | **optional, a zero-cost re-check, not a stage of work** — run it after the logline is written, and it usually amounts to rereading it once and declaring a pass; its value lies in having that reader in mind *while* writing the logline, not in checking afterwards: can one sentence get a junior marketing person a few column inches in *Entertainment Weekly* on your behalf? **Bigger does not mean a bigger budget, it means the scale of the conflict that defines the show.** (Worked comparisons are in reference.md §7.)

**17. "Big does not have to mean shallow."** Three ways to enlarge (same chapter): **change the profession** (the origin of the Sopranos changed exactly one thing: the job), **change the location**, **change the era**. The opposing position (Miller ch. 1–2): **"having great characters trumps high concept every time."** (All three examples in full and Miller's argument are in reference.md §7.)

**18. The ledger unit: whatever an episode "resolves" must leave a debt to be paid in some later episode.** (An extension by this pack, pushing Succession's season-level practice down to the episode level — the original rule is that the finale resolves all ten lines while every resolution creates debt for the next season: Greg's photocopies, Tom's cruise ships, Roman's manslaughter, Shiv's double loyalty; see `succession-series-writing`.)

An ordinary case-of-the-week show starts repeating itself around episode 12, because closing a case each week resets the board to zero. Change "closing" to "booking" and it stops: the deal signed this week, the person protected, the complaint suppressed each leave an entry on the ledger, to be settled by a nominated later episode. When laying out the outline, write each entry as a line — `episode N did X → episode M has Y pay for it` — where M must be a specific episode number. Six to ten such debts per season is right; too few and the episodes remain islands, too many and the audience loses track.

- **Relation to "a case an episode"**: this is the third road between anthology and serial — the unit still closes (the audience gets a result each week) but the consequences do not.
- **Relation to story pattern (rule 7)**: story pattern governs the repetition of each episode's *shape*; the ledger governs the accumulation of each episode's *consequences*. Both must be present: shape without accumulation is an anthology; accumulation without shape leaves the audience unsure what they buy each week.
- **Self-check**: point at any episode at random and say who it owes, who pays, and in which episode. Any episode you cannot answer for is an episode that got reset to zero.

**19. Divide a long series by "which use of the same skill this is," not by chronology.** (An extension by this pack, on the basis of the movement method for seasons in Landau 1e Tip 13: pin only the tent poles at the top of a season, cut the season into movements of 4–6 episodes, one big event per movement.)

The protagonist's skill does not change; **each new use of it** is one section, so the number of uses is the number of sections: learning to win with it → winning while investigating, or using it for private business on the side → turning it around and detonating a far larger cost → abandoning the original goal and using it to change the rules. One person, four uses, four consequences — four natural sections; 24 episodes divided by four is 6 an episode-section, exactly inside the movement band of 4–6.

- The benefit: the section boundaries grow out of the character, not out of the calendar; and each section's climax is "the blowback of this particular use," which needs no separate design.
- The test: if you cannot say how the protagonist's use of the skill in one section **differs** from the last, that section is padding.
- Relation to the season arc: settle the sequence of uses first, then the tent poles, and only then lay out the episodes — reverse the order and you end up drawing a timetable and filling events into it.

## 3. The Character Web

**20. Internal conflict must be externalized into a ring of people pulling at him** (Rabkin ch. 6). Buffy's "wanting a normal life vs. saving the world" is externalized into a mother who wants her normal, a watcher who wants her killing vampires, friends in the everyday world and a lover who is a century-old vampire; SHIELD's Skye is the reverse, keeping the conflict inside one person, and it generates no story.

**21. Every series is a family series** (Landau 2e ch. 6): **"Every television series is about a family – whether they're related by blood or not."** The corollary: **every family on television must be imperfect and discontented on some level.** (Six family types with examples are in reference.md §6.)

**22. The familial-role casting bank** (same chapter): you do not need a complete set of genders and ages, you need to settle "how each character will treat the others in both a positive and a negative direction." (The full role bank and its corollaries for law-enforcement partners and workplace colleagues are in reference.md §6.)

**23. The transactional family (the Logan Roy paradigm)** (same chapter): "seeking the boss's approval substitutes for seeking a parent's — and the former comes with a salary as extra confirmation"; Logan is "withholding his approval and validation," and "His relationships with his kids are all transactional."

**24. Three scene-level rules** (same chapter): **"the people who love you most know how to hurt you the most."** → Before writing a two-hander, settle which of the other's buttons he knows, then decide whether he presses it. (The new-character test and the subtext formula in the original wording are in reference.md §6.)

**25. Division of expertise** (Landau 1e Tip 20): different skill sets exist to **create conflict, present multiple POVs, and help the audience tell characters apart**; Grey's Anatomy **defines its characters by specialty.** (The Breaking Bad division table, House's principle of built-in disagreement, and the Out of Their Element generator are in reference.md §6.)

**26. The rationality axis** (the Mooresville pitch in Rabkin's appendix): **"start from the position that they are both rational, intelligent adults"** → in a relationship show, the mutual attraction needs a **structural** reason, not just chemistry. (The full case is in reference.md §6.)

**27. Complementary co-leads** (Landau 1e Tip 9): **"two incomplete people together you get one whole."** (Mulder/Scully = belief and doubt.) (The Hacks mutual-need/shame template and the Gomorrah logline generator are in reference.md §6.)

**28. Sakamoto's out-of-sync co-leads** (*Flower Bouquet*): value turns come not from opposition but from **consideration** (scene 85, drying her hair, he says "I've decided to look for a job"). (The four mirrored characters and the dialogue-budget allocation are in reference.md §5.)

**29. Shame and voids** (Landau 2e ch. 7, citing Brené Brown's *Daring Greatly*): **shame = our fear of disconnection**; trauma creates **emotional voids** → voids produce coping mechanisms that avoid vulnerability. **"Anyone who challenges any of these constructs becomes an antagonist."** (A design menu of the three classes of defence is in reference.md §6.)

**30. A grounded superpower is simultaneously a liability** (Landau 2e ch. 5, Gilligan's character-building method): Walt's superpower is his capacity for self-deception. 1e Tip 8: **"when a character's greatest strength also becomes his or her greatest liability… The trick to dramatic sustainability is keeping your main character in this form of limbo."** (Three examples and the full sidebar are in reference.md §6.)

**31. The same backstory stated twice** (Landau 2e ch. 7, Mare of Easttown): **give the edited version first, then the true version several episodes later in a safer or more coerced setting; the gap between them is the character's shame.** (Both speeches in the original wording and the ensemble balancing are in reference.md §6.)

**32. The pacing of a flaw's revelation** (Landau 1e Tip 8): a flaw has to grow from backstory to run long, but in the pilot the backstory is only **hinted at obliquely** — "what we don't know about a character is often more compelling than what we do know." (The delayed reveals of Don Draper and Scandal, and the Girls counter-example, are in reference.md §6.)

**33. Settle the nature of the antagonistic force before deciding whether it needs a face** (Landau 2e ch. 9): **Succession = greed**; **Mare of Easttown and Fleabag = grief.** The governing principle (footnoted to UCLA professor emeritus Howard Suber): **"All villains are antagonists, but not all antagonists are villains."** (A comparison across nine shows and Oberg's addendum are in reference.md §6.)

**34. The villain is the hero of their own story** (same chapter): **"Every villain is the hero of his/her/their own story."** — "they usually feel justified, entitled, and downright righteous." The structural judgement: **"as soon as that primary goal is successfully attained, the series is usually over – unless it pivots to another overarching mission or goal."** (Four riders and the Rue mismatch case are in reference.md §6.)

**35. Do not build straw men** (the Kings, Landau 2e ch. 4): **"Propaganda is confirming what you already believe."** What is worth far more is **regular characters we like disagreeing with each other, each with a real case.** (The full passage and The Good Wife example are in reference.md §6.)

**36. The three parts of a sustainable conflict web** (Blum ch. 17, test three): **"if all the leads of a serial love one another, all they can do is smile and agree with each other."** The engine = **a plot pattern of "group or family bonds" + personality attributes preinstalled in each character that must grind against the others + a coincidence or situation that makes them unable to leave each other**; missing any one of the three, it will not last three to five years. (The itemized breakdowns of Downton and Succession, Succession's three devices and the Tom counter-proof are in reference.md §5; **a filled-in sample of this web = the Succession table of seven wants / what each can lose / who they are bound to / their engine function, in reference-samples.md §13.**)

## 4. Pilot Types and Strategy

**37. Three categories** (Landau 1e Tip 1 / 2e ch. 14; Douglas 3e ch. 2, Oberg 3.2 and Calvisi §6 say the same thing under other names): **the premise pilot** (what happens in episode 1 is necessary for the show to begin at day one) / **non-premise / ongoing / midstream** ("It may be the audience's first episode, but it's not day one for the characters.") / **hybrid** (open at the climactic moment then flash forward or back); Oberg adds a **semi-cold start** (The Walking Dead). (The four definitions, the example lists, how the Sopranos classifies and the 47-show classification are in reference.md §4.)

**38. Two hard rules of entry**: Oberg 3.2 — a premise pilot **must not save the inciting incident for the end of the episode**, "You can't afford to have a pilot that's just a long first dramatic act."; Calvisi §6 — **"Enter the action of your story as late as possible in the world but as soon as possible in the script."** (In the Scandal script the client did not walk in until page 12; the cut episode moved it to **minute 6**.)

**39. Three network strategies and the "premise pilot trap"** (Blum ch. 17, in a 1999–2001 context; **the networks are dated, the mechanism still holds**): the original wording is that a premise pilot "is likely to be **a poor barometer** for predicting where the series will go"; **Smith ch. 20 agrees for half-hour comedy** — **"most network executives prefer to see a typical-episode pilot"**; **Landau's dissent**: it is not a trap, it only requires you to say which kind it is in the pitch. (The three strategies in the original wording and Smith's argument are in reference.md §4.)
→ **Four tests**: ① a conservative mass-audience channel or a half-hour comedy → typical or half-premise; ② a complex world whose rules must be explained → premise (Lost, Stranger Things, TWD); ③ the character web itself is the engine → midstream (Fleabag, The Office, Succession); ④ you need both → the Breaking Bad move: set up in the front half, run the story pattern once in the back half.

**40. Three ways to close**: ① **"The end of your pilot is the beginning of your series."** (Landau); ② **stick the landing**; ③ **a cliffhanger is not compulsory** (Miller ch. 6). (All three in the original wording plus Calvisi's balancing sentence are in reference.md §4.)

**41. Douglas's four pilot steps** (3e ch. 4): ① **Create the World** (Lindelof: **"the franchise is the world."**) ② **Find the Story Springboards** ③ **Populate the World** ④ **Make a Plan.** At the core is **a secret**, deep enough to take 88 or 100 hours to unwrap; **a character growth arc is usually not on the agenda on day one**; **the first ten pages decide life or death.** (All four steps in full with the quotations are in reference.md §4.)

**42. Ten anxious questions** (Jeffries, who teaches the pilot class at USC): **list at least ten questions gripping the audience at the moment the pilot ends**, and use those ten to generate **ten short synopses for future episodes**; **hook the executive and keep cards up your sleeve at the same time.** (The material file and that eternal note are in reference.md §4.)

**43. The Sopranos' refusal to explain** (Chase's foreword p0012 plus the pilot): the pilot deliberately never explains Tony's formal position or how "waste management" works, on the grounds that **"rarely is anything black and white in life"**; **that is also why the pilot does not close on a cliffhanger.** (The full case and the real reason the networks passed are in reference.md §5.)

## 5. Series Typology and Story-Type (mainly Oberg)

**44. Eight series types** (Oberg 1.2): procedural (closed-ended) / serial / serial-procedural hybrid (three variants) / sitcom / mini-series and limited / anthology / mini-series series / web series. The unwritten rule of the sitcom is that **"characters don't change, and it's fine"**; limited (4–8 episodes) is **the easiest first step from film into television.** (All eight definitions, what each means for audience, and the three hybrid variants are tabulated in reference.md §3.)

**45. Two extra rules for the mini-series series** (Rabkin ch. 12): the concept must not be "defined by execution" (all the information in American Crime Story is in the public domain); **the first episode of every season of an anthology or mini-series series must supply a fresh immediate narrative hook connecting the audience to the protagonist.** **Landau 2e ch. 1 (2022)**: **limited series are harder to sell**, and **10 episodes a season is the benchmark for a new show.** (The full text and the exceptions are in reference.md §3.)

**46. Determining story-type** (Oberg 2.2): ask **"Where does the main problem in the story come from, at season level?"** — external → **plot-led**; internal → **character-led** (which needs no antagonist but does need a **catalyst character**); society or the story world → **theme-led**; plus **hybrid** and **exception.** (Examples of all three and the exception mechanism are in reference.md §3.)

**47. The reverse test specific to series** (same section): **because series are almost always multi-line, you have to check backwards** — is there a protagonist and a main dramatic action/evolution taking shape at season level? If so, it is not theme-led. (The second hint and the Chernobyl determination are in reference.md §3.)

**48. Choosing the wrong type locks you in** (Oberg's introduction and question 9 of 3.1): **"The wrong combination of series type and story-type is the root cause of many series failures"**; the most common box-in is **a character-led show resolving the protagonist's internal problem completely in season one (film thinking)** → season two then has to change story-type. (Three examples and the model of balance are in reference.md §3.)

**49. Maslow and the M-Factor** (Oberg 2.3, 2.6): **"the lower in the pyramid, the larger the potential audience"**, and **you must explore vertically.** The **M-Factor** = where the audience, genre and story-type sit relative to Maslow, plus whether the budget is self-consistent (scored 1–5). (Worked upward and downward exploration in Occupied / Money Heist / the sitcom, the whodunit warning, and how Breaking Bad roots motive are in reference.md §3.)

**50. "What's at stake?"** (Oberg glossary) = "the question development people ask most often when they don't care about your story": what happens if the main problem goes unsolved, and it must make someone **sad, anxious or frightened**; the floor is that no episode may exist whose stake you cannot state, and multiple lines must connect. (Both floors in the original wording, Landau's two, and the half-hour and relationship-show versions are in reference.md §7.)

**51. The innate problems of an arena** (Miller ch. 1, a 2015 American broadcast-network perspective): high school / family / relationship / news / anthology each carry a native disease, the core being **"A character's agency completes the story engine"** — a journalist can "meddle" but has **no licence to intervene.** (All five arenas item by item, the fixes and a note on scope are in reference.md §3.)

## 6. Documents: From Logline to Bible

**52. Three document tiers** (Blum ch. 17): **the creative concept** → **the series presentation** → **the series bible.** (The three as originally defined and what each must contain are in reference.md §1.)

**53. Page-count quick reference**: bible **10–85 pages** / mini-bible **about 5 pages**; a written pitch bible **10–30 pages** (the sweet spot around 15); treatment **10–15 pages for drama, 8–10 for comedy**; a half-hour comedy series format **10–15 pages**; a spoken pitch has a **20-minute sweet spot**, and time it; the mythology document plus origin story total **one page maximum.** (The full table, its sources and Douglas's three bible tiers are in reference.md §1; Oberg's twelve bible components with specifications, and the difference between bible, pitch deck and format, are in reference-samples.md §10.)

**54. Four field checklists (pick one)**: **Landau 2e ch. 1 Essential Guideposts** (about 25 fields, usable as a bible table of contents; the **logline "must contain the word 'but'"**; the **central question is about the future, the central mystery about the past**) / **the 11-item pitch recipe in Landau 2e ch. 14** / **Miller's 10-element treatment plus six big questions** / **Calvisi's 17-column Basic Story Map** (**the same columns are filled in twice, once at episode level and once at series level**). (All four in full are in reference.md §1; a matrix comparing Miller's 10 elements across six student treatments is in reference-samples.md §12.)

**55. The five things most often got wrong**: ① **the overview is not a pilot synopsis** (Douglas names this a common error: write the world and the quest of the whole show, and **give the springboards inside the overview itself**); ② **do not list characters**; ③ **do not tell them everything you know** (being asked follow-up questions is the success condition); ④ **do not over-plan**; ⑤ **the prose style must match the show's genre.** (All five in the original wording, each book's addenda, and the hard rules from Oberg, Landau and Smith are in reference.md §1.)

**56. A skeleton for the story-line document (devised by this pack; it is in none of the books).** Every book gives documents for "the whole show," and **not one gives a document for "a single story line"**; a ten-field table (line name / purpose / driver / stakes / entry this episode / exit this episode / cross-episode beats / relation to the controlling idea / causal relation to other lines, including a **pull-the-line test**) is in reference.md §2 — use it before breaking a season to write each line onto its own page and then align them horizontally.

**Landau's story tentacles (2e ch. 4) are the positive version**: when breaking story, "**choose the plotline that leads to more story**" — look for fertile ground rather than one-off dead ends.

## 7. General Principles for Adapting Into a Series

(The five-source typology of adaptation in the Chinese system and worked episode- and scene-level examples are in `sw-chinese-series-practice`; stage-adaptation detail is in `sw-format-adaptation`. Except where noted, the following is from Landau 2e ch. 2.)

1. **What can be adapted**: sprawling epics do not make good films, but **streaming is the ideal medium for a multi-decade, thousand-character epic** (GoT, My Brilliant Friend, Bridgerton; "Russian literature is ideal for the ongoing TV series format"); comics are natively episodic.
2. **Chain of title**: without a clean legal chain of authorship nothing gets made. Two caveats: **structure is not protected by copyright** (Rashomon, Memento, Groundhog Day, Russian Doll); and **scène à faire** is not infringement. Smith's hard rule: **"You can't sell what you don't own."**
3. **Timing is everything**: what is a taboo on the page may be unbearable on screen (the novel of Room has a childlike innocence; the film is "unbearably gruesome").
4. **There is no formula**: "The best approach to any adaptation is bespoke."; a multi-year epic may map **one period per season**; **one overarching theme per season**, changing theme at a pivot (The Wire is the model).
5. **Changing characters, era and place is all allowed, with a reason**: Euphoria changed nearly everything and kept only the essence (reckless pleasure, psychologically damaged teenagers) — the original had no Rue and no Jules; the Berlin line of Unorthodox is entirely invented, and **deliberately distances the invented character from the real person** ("we made her a musician," out of concern for the original author's privacy).
6. **Honouring the spirit without being married to it**: you may merge people into a composite character and cut subplots; "the moving image supersedes the written word." Elementary's formula: "Sherlock Holmes in repair" — a man who used to be ten steps ahead is now only two.
7. **A model for choosing the starting point**: an adaptation should pick a start that is **more dynamic, establishes a gap immediately, and whose gap itself can trigger the flashbacks.** (The Queen's Gambit novel and Netflix starting points compared are in reference.md §5.)
8. **Finding the (emotional) core**: **"It's not solely who does what when, but who knows what when and the corresponding emotional fallout."**; "When in doubt, simplify."
9. **Externalizing inner psychology**: omniscience or interior monologue → multiple POVs or one mysterious narrator (Fleabag's asides make us her best friend, but she is an **unreliable narrator** crushed by guilt over Boo's death). Walter Mosley: **"Plot Is Revelation."**
10. **When handed a thesis or an IP, first work out what you actually have** (Saracho on Vida, 2e ch. 6): Vida also had a **world-building document** described as "a short story" — it contained two bars and a pair of sisters, **but it was not a story.** → **Determine whether what you hold is a world-building document or a story; the former supplies only a world.** The wrong way to write an issue show: "if I just embody gentrification in the two sisters… The characters just embody the issue."

## 8. Preserved Disagreements

| Point at issue | A | B | Which to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Planning multiple seasons in advance | Rabkin ch. 4 + Lindelof: "it's hubris to plot out… the second and third and fourth seasons" (St. Elsewhere resolved all its conflicts in season 5 and was renewed for a sixth) | A cable executive quoted in the same chapter: a pitch needs "a strong, clear vision for the arc of the first season, and well-developed ideas for where subsequent years would go"; Miller demands a three-season arc | Pitch by B, work in the room by A; Oberg's middle way: season one clear, later seasons only a direction |
| Knowing the finale before entering the room | Armstrong: **he knew the finale while writing episode 1**; Gomorrah completed a full season outline first | Gould (BCS): "feeling our way through it rather than having a master plan"; Feldman did not know Steve would die at the end of S1 | Multi-line ensemble shows lean A; shows evolving out of actor chemistry lean B |
| Write the script first or pitch first | Landau: "write your pilot scripts on spec" | Aranya: "**a script is also 60 pages of reasons to say no**"; Your Honor sold on a spoken pitch plus three pages | No credits → A (Oberg agrees); credits or a package → B |
| Premise or typical pilot | Blum: "a poor barometer"; Smith: most executives prefer typical | Landau: not a trap, just say which it is | See the four tests in rule 39 |
| The standing of theme | Rabkin puts theme first among the four elements | The Kings: "the number of times we actually used the word 'theme' in the writers' room is less than two" | In development follow A (theme is the pitch and the filter); while breaking story B is acceptable |
| Do characters change | Landau 2e: the protagonist must change; Calvisi: every season needs an Assumption of Power at the end | Oberg: **"it's how the audience perceives them that changes"**; Armstrong rejects the "fallacy of growth" (people also **diminish**); Shore: "nobody changes" | **What can change is the situation and the goal; the author defines the constant core**; a limited series may write a complete arc |
| Is an act structural | Douglas: "Put away your books on three-act structure" | Oberg: four or five acts are only a **logistical format** (the sausage-string image: it decides how many and how long) | Handled fully in `sw-series-structure`; the consensus here: **the engine has nothing to do with act count** |
| Do you need high concept | Rabkin: if it is not big enough nobody samples it | Miller: "great characters trumps high concept every time" | Both: use high concept to make an executive read, and character to keep the show alive |
| Are family / relationship engines weak | Miller (a 2015 broadcast-network view): both carry innate problems | Landau: **every series is a family series**; the engines of Downton and Succession come from class and inheritance | Miller's judgement is bounded by a specific market and year; a family ensemble needs a strong engine or institutional friction alongside |
| The reality route for an arena | Gomorrah shot in real neighbourhoods (a bar exterior in S1 was blown up overnight; a local man playing a bodyguard disappeared on day five) | Costanzo built an entire Naples backlot in Caserta for safety | Depends on risk and budget; Blum: location choice is the quotient of **"glamour ÷ shootability"** |

## 9. Diagnostic Checklist

**Engine**: ① Is the theme an arguable opposition rather than one word? ② Is the theme embodied as conflict between two characters, and is internal conflict externalized? ③ Can the interlock test produce a second and a sixth story? ④ Is the central question a process sentence or an answer sentence? ⑤ Can the story pattern be written in one sentence and run completely in the pilot's back half? ⑥ Do five or six future stories share the same source and the same ending shape? ⑦ Can this set of conflicts sustain 100 episodes? (**Closed long-form, limited series and mini-series skip the "100 episodes / five seasons" question** and run the engine pressure-loss calculation of rule 10 instead: work out at which episode the material engine loses pressure, and that is where the show should end. The body text already draws this distinction; this mirrors it.) ⑧ Can you state each episode's stake, where it sits on Maslow, and whether the lines explore downward? ⑨ Is the protagonist a participant or an observer, and do they have licence/agency? ⑩ The marketing-intern test: can a one-sentence pitch say anything beyond "it's really good"?

**Character web**: ⑪ Does every main character carry a subtext landmine (at the character level, not a plot reveal), with a projected detonation episode? ⑫ Do each person's two attributes interfere with each other? ⑬ Have you mistaken suffering for conflict, and does the character have an action available? ⑭ Is the nature of the antagonistic force settled — and only after that, whether it needs a face? ⑮ Is the villain the hero of their own story? ⑯ Have you written out the relationship vector for every pair (which button gets pressed, who envies whom, who cannot stand whom)? ⑰ Does every new character answer "how does their arrival change the regulars"? ⑱ Is the protagonist's grounded superpower simultaneously a liability? ⑲ Is there one instance of "the same backstory stated twice"? ⑳ Would the show end once the primary goal is attained, and is there a next overarching mission?

**Pilot and documents** (**series level: run this only when setting up a project, writing a pilot or making a proposal; skip the whole block when revising a single episode of a show already on air**): ㉑ Is the pilot premise, non-premise or hybrid, and why? ㉒ Has the central mystery been dramatized? ㉓ Does the ending leave ten gripping questions, and can they generate ten future episodes? ㉔ Does the pilot demonstrate the franchise (has the heist begun? has the therapy begun?) ㉕ Is the overview written as a pilot synopsis, and does it give springboards? ㉖ Are characters introduced embedded in story, or as a list? ㉗ Does the prose style match the genre? ㉘ Have you told them everything you know (being asked follow-up questions is the success condition)? ㉙ Is season five written too rigidly? ㉚ Does a character-led show resolve the internal problem completely in season one (the most common box-in)?

**Who the document is for**: ㉛ **Who is this page for?** Working documents (the landmine table, the button table, the binding agents, the story-line documents) and submission documents (logline, overview, characters, pilot synopsis, future stories) are two different things, and must not be handed over as one. A platform reader will not look at your working tables; but a "proposal" with all the working tables stripped out will leave you gasping at episode 14. **Write both, hand over one.** The test: if a page contains this skill's vocabulary (franchise, landmine, unity of opposites, story pattern), it is for you.

**Cost and sectioning**: ㉜ Does everything an episode "resolves" leave a debt to be paid in some later episode? (See §2 rule 18.) ㉝ Is the series sectioned by "which use of the same skill this is," or merely by chronology? (See rule 19.)

## 10. Workflow: From an Idea to a Bible

1. **Research, then build the show** (Miller ch. 1): pick an arena → read books and articles from opposing positions, watch documentaries, interview experts, do a ride-along. While researching, answer three questions (what is this arena's world? who are the people who live in it? **how does a new character intersect with the people in this world?**). "I don't create the show before I do my research."
2. **Answer three whys** (Landau 2e ch. 1): why this idea / why you / why now (**do not chase a platform's current mandate** — by the time you finish writing, it will have changed).
3. **Dig the world** (step 1 of Douglas's four): if you cannot name three, four or five leads, go back to this step.
4. **Find the engine**: settle the franchise type and the **springboard**; an arena with no ready-made springboard needs "a personal inciting incident" designed as its weekly starter. **Focus on verbs** (Landau 2e ch. 4): investigating, diagnosing, litigating, battling, competing, saving, confronting, stealing, avenging — the verb is the micro mission.
5. **Run Rabkin's three-element derivation** (the NASCAR case in ch. 7, which you can copy wholesale): research → **invert the problem** (the protagonist must be an outsider like the audience) → **strengthen the conflict** ("doesn't understand it" is too weak → "hates it and is trapped in this world") → **characters as a clash of equals** (drawn from opposing sides; veto observer protagonists) → the situation (the only way out is to do this thing) → **the conflict must be intrinsic to character and concept** (ruling out frame-ups, villainous bosses and other "plot devices that can't power a series through a hundred episodes") → distil the theme (an evergreen argument inside the era, and one **you personally care about**) → **use the theme to correct the concept** ("individual vs. community" needs a community → a pit crew → a group of misfits as stubborn as the boss).
6. **Settle the type** (Oberg): format → series type → season-level story-type → per-episode story-type → genre → M-Factor check. Format self-check: could it be 10×60 → 8×45? Is the first third backstory? **If it only gets good at episode 3, compress or cut the first two** — "starting the story too early is a common flaw."
7. **Build the character web**: familial roles → the positive and negative vector of every pair → division of expertise → one landmine each → the nature of the antagonistic force → check that attributes interfere with each other.
8. **Fill in Calvisi's one-page 17-column form** as the foundation for every later document.
9. **Write the treatment (Miller's 10 elements) or the presentation (Blum's four sections)**: a serialized show gets a three-season arc, a stand-alone show a list of 10 episode loglines. (Six completed treatment samples, the Egri three-dimension plus signature-trait form, the capability-growth table and how to write Markets are in reference-samples.md §12.)
10. **Generate and test 5–6 future stories** (different in both source and ending shape); if they are alike, go back to step 4.
11. **Write a one-page story-line document for each line** (rule 56), align them into a "character × episode" master grid, and run the **pull-the-line test** on each.
12. **Design and write the pilot**: choose the type (the four tests in rule 39) → list the "first ten pages plan" → run the story pattern once in the back half → plant the landmines → write out the ten questions at the ending → write the script (beats and acts are in `sw-series-structure`).
13. **Update it into a pitch document / mini-bible** (Landau's 11 items or Oberg's twelve components, with specifications in reference-samples.md §10; real mini-bibles issued to outside writers are in reference-samples.md §11), attaching the mythology document plus origin story (≤1 page).
14. **Rehearse the pitch against a clock** (20 minutes), with at least three future-episode examples ready; in-room etiquette is in `sw-writers-room`.
15. **Write the production bible only after the sale**, and let it become a continuously updated living document ("facts to wax").

## 11. Exercises

1. **Reverse-engineer engines** (Goldberg & Rabkin ch. 2/9/10): watch television for a whole evening and after each episode answer "what is this series about" in ≤4 lines (Law & Order, CSI and Star Trek are barred — that is cheating); then take one main title apart shot by shot (format / mood / character sequence) and answer "Does your story fulfill what the main title promises?"
2. **Three episodes of one show**: watch three episodes and ask what they have in common and **why they could not be told on another show** — that is its franchise.
3. **99 stories**: write 20 episode loglines in 30 minutes, each touching both concept and conflict; if you cannot reach 6, go back and fix the engine.
4. **Process-sentence rewrite**: write the central question of five shows you love as a process sentence each; if your own project can only be written as an answer sentence, change the container (profession / location / era).
5. **Landmine table**: one row per main character — "unstated fear / desire / contradiction + projected detonation episode."
6. **Character-web vector table**: pair every main character with every other and write in each cell "which of the other's buttons he presses in this relationship"; more than a third of the cells empty means the ensemble is not yet a web.
7. **Blum's five-story test**: write 5 future stories, annotating **source** and **ending shape**; if they are alike, redo them.
8. **Fill the 17 columns at two levels**: complete the Basic Story Map for your project twice — once for "this episode," once for "the series."
9. **The six-episode test plus ten questions**: write the ten gripping questions at the end of the pilot, then expand each into a future-episode logline (Jeffries's classroom assignment).
10. **Adaptation exercise**: pick a long novel and write the period each season covers, the overarching theme of each season, and the choice of pilot starting point with three reasons (in the argument format of The Queen's Gambit).
11. **Engine pressure-loss self-check** (the Succession S4 foreword): write down this show's "material plot engine" (the deal, the empire, the job, the source of cases) and ask: in which season does it begin to lose pressure? What engine replaces it then, or does the show end?

# Dialogue (对白)

There is only one core proposition: **dialogue is action** (McKee: dia + legein = action through words). Beneath every sentence lie desire, intent, and strategy; "flaws in dialogue are flaws in story"—when dialogue cannot be fixed, step back first to examine event and character design. This skill merges the comprehensive system of Robert McKee's *Dialogue*, American practical taboos from Richard Walter and Neill D. Hicks, Lu Jun's theory of dramatic language (including Chinese opera lyrics), Lajos Egri's character-dialogue relationship, and screwball comedy dialogue patterns. For beat-by-beat analyses of seven sample scenes and lyric examples, see [reference.md](reference.md).

## 1. What Dialogue Is

- **Complete definition**: Any words spoken by any character to anyone—to another person, to oneself ("all inner monologue is dialogue"), to the audience (monologue, voice-over, direct address to camera). "No character speaks to another person or even to themselves without a reason." Beneath every line must lie desire-directed action = verbal strategy.
- **Dramatic dialogue** (inside the scene, charged with conflict, every line aimed at action causing reaction) vs. **narrative dialogue** (outside the scene, the fourth wall vanishes). Three forms of dialogue demand three writing approaches (*Doctor Glas*: diary form -> addressed to reader -> dramatized).
- **Three functions**: Exposition, characterization, action.
- **Three concentric spheres of content**: **The said** (vocabulary, diction, syntax, tone establishing education, wit, emotional range); **the unsaid** (consciously concealed thoughts and feelings; the reader instinctively leaps past speech to guess the Subtext; the writer must make it felt); **the unsayable** (subconscious needs, exposed only through choice under pressure). **As language, dialogue conveys characterization; as action, dialogue reveals true character.**
- **Action vs. behavior**: Eating ice cream is never because of hunger—chasing away sorrow / rebelling against a doctor / rewarding oneself is the action; any dialogue that fails to answer "what is the real action in the Subtext?" remains unfinished. Silence is also action; a pause is also a reaction.
- Three questions for writing dialogue: **What does the character want right now? What action pushes them toward achieving it? What words will they speak to execute that action?** Label Subtext action using **gerund phrases** ("crawling at her feet" / "turning a deaf ear to his plea").
- Medium differences: Theater is primarily auditory and can be poeticized or rhymed (the "as if" convention); cinema is primarily visual, where the camera magnifies falseness, demanding natural, actable lines written for actors that welcome improvisation (exceptions: stylized realism, non-realism, extreme characters); television is face-to-face, favoring conversation; literature is free. **The level of conflict determines the volume of dialogue: more external social conflict means less dialogue; more intimate personal conflict means more dialogue.**
- Walter: Most producers only read the dialogue—"the best reason to write dialogue that sparkles and weighs every single word"; yet **the finest dialogue is often no dialogue at all** (in *The Sopranos*, Carmela takes off her ring; Tony simply stares at her). Hicks: A good script resembles poetry; dialogue springs from emotion deep within.

## 2. Exposition: Dramatize It, Weaponize It, Guard Secrets

- Include only the absolute minimum required information; never release it ahead of time; list all facts ranked by importance and emphasize essential ones repeatedly. "Make them laugh, make them cry, make them wait."
- **Show vs. tell**: Charlie saying "We've known each other for twenty years from school until now, good morning" (aimless) -> "You're no different from the immature jerk you were at school twenty years ago; smell those clothes" — the audience learns "twenty years" and "school" without realizing it.
- **Narrative drive** (information leaked drop by drop so the audience anticipates what follows; *Catch-22*: "there's a catch") and **exposition as ammunition** (characters wield what they know as a weapon; "I am your father" disarms the son at the duel's climax).
- **The most critical reveal is a secret**; backstory is not a lifelong history, but a slice of the past withheld until a lesser-of-two-evils moment (*Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?*: "I ate the telegram") — "using backstory to create a turning point is the most potent expository technique."
- Direct telling serves two purposes: pace (García Márquez: "Many years later, as he faced the firing squad") and counterpoint (the narrator's ironic theme); convert "and then, and then" into dramatized telling or create internal conflict with another self.
- **Taboos of forced exposition**: Scrolling title cards, newspaper headlines, characters telling each other facts both already know ("We've known and loved each other for over twenty years since that college fraternity party..."); Walter's Principle 28: **Never let this character tell that character information the audience already knows**.
- Hicks: Put expository dialogue into unexpected environments to inject energy (wedding seating dispute -> jewelry store heist -> the cop is the brother-in-law who owes money); "What do you really want?" and "Are you okay?" are two legitimate on-the-nose exceptions.
- McKee: Do not write what common sense can infer; do not provide information unless its absence causes confusion; save secrets for last.
- Exercise: Rewrite a passage of clunky exposition three times—thrown as ammunition, slipped into a seduction scene, and hidden to make behavior credible.

## 3. Six Tasks and Four Flaws (McKee)

**Six tasks**: Every spoken utterance executes an internal action; every action/reaction beat builds the scene toward a turning point; statements and implications convey information and perspective; a distinct verbal style personalizes the character; progressive beats capture attention; the language strikes the reader while remaining true to the scene and character.

### Flaws of Credibility
- **Conversation expends energy; dialogue focuses on meaning**; credibility does not equal realism (*Alice in Wonderland*'s dialogue is impossible in reality but true to the characters).
- Empty words (telling the other what is already known); overwrought emotion (language far outstripping feeling); excessive obviousness (planting the writer's awareness inside character consciousness); over-perception (the character diagnosing themselves more profoundly than Freud plus Socrates combined); **confusing rationalization with motivation**—motivation is a subconscious need, dialogue is an excuse (the Crusaders: "to save souls for God"; Richard III: "because you are breathtakingly beautiful, I killed him").
- **The trouble with melodrama is not over-expression, but lack of motivation**; once motivation matches action, choose between direct or subtle—softly murmuring "nail him to the cross" while inspecting one's fingernails hits harder than roaring.

### Flaws of Language
- **Cliché**: Write the first word, then improvise several more, get wild -> select carefully and discard the rest; "no one needs to see your second-best choice."
- **Character-neutral language**: "Oh my God" -> Alabama: "Sweet, bleeding Jesus"; Detroit: "Lord have mercy"; New York: "Well, damn me to hell."
- **Overwritten language**: Shouting to the writer "look how clever my dialogue is," slamming the character shut; "when in doubt, tone it down."
- **Eight cures for dry language**: Write the concrete rather than the abstract; write the familiar rather than the obscure; write short words rather than long ones ("his embroidery is a falsification of reality" -> "he lied"; four phenomena: more agitated = shorter, more proactive = shorter, cleverer = more complex, more erudite = longer); write directly rather than circuitously; write active voice rather than passive ("She is playing around" -> "She plays around"); write short sentences rather than long paragraphs; write expressive language rather than mimicry ("do not imitate life, express life"); cut filler words ("well," "uh," "then," "also").
- **If dialogue does not imply thoughts and feelings unsaid on the surface, either enrich it or cut it.**

### Flaws of Content
- **On-the-nose**: Writing Subtext as text, "speaking in a way no human being ever speaks"; a candlelit dinner where one says "I love you, I love you" with total sincerity—"dead as a dog on the road, impossible to act." Always maintaining Subtext is the guiding principle of realism; non-realistic genres (myth, fairy tale, sci-fi, animation, fable) permit reduced Subtext.
- **The inner monologue fallacy**: True inner monologues (long passages uninterrupted, addressed to no one) degenerate into the writer's megaphone; convert them into action/reaction (inserting the other's or one's own nonverbal reactions); a garrulous character may torment other characters, but must never bore the audience (*August: Osage County*: the relatives' weary expressions; *The Stronger*: the silent Miss Y becomes the star role).
- **Duologue** "stiff as a brick" (*Gladiator*: Lucilla and Maximus in the dungeon) -> **rewrite into a trialogue**: two people using a third party or object as their battleground—the canary (*The Legs*), Heisenberg, the East-West divide, Godot, wine (*Sideways*), *Three Days of the Condor* (*Out of Sight*).

### Flaws of Design
- **Repetition**: Accidental echoes (record and read aloud); **repetitive beats** (same strategy with swapped words: pleading/refusing three times)—"the greatest sin is boredom," diminishing returns (the first ice cream tastes great; the third makes you gag; three crying scenes turn the third into comedy).
- **Defective dialogue**: Murky meaning ("apply force well with a large fastening spike" vs. "hammer a long nail"); mistimed meaning; **mistimed cueing**—the final word of every speech should ideally be the operative word triggering the partner's reaction (*The Miracle Worker*: operative words in the original all land at sentence ends); highlight the final phrase of each speech with a marker.
- **Poorly designed scenes**: Turning points arriving too early, too late, or missing entirely; **divergent scenes** (four causes for text/Subtext mismatch: internal completely spelled out makes dialogue flat / internal too weak makes dialogue overblown / the two are unrelated / no conflict); vulgarity cannot raise the temperature ("when criminals stop cursing, that is when you know trouble has started"); **the rewrite trap**—revising dialogue to be more and more explicit until Subtext vanishes entirely: "a failing scene rarely stems from words; the solution lies buried deep within event and character design."

## 4. Craft: Rhetoric, Syntactic Design, Economy, Pauses, Silence

- **Rhetoric**: The more sensory the rhetoric, the deeper the impression ("walks like a model" vs. "moves like a slow, sexy song"); Blanche: "Sometimes—there's God—so quickly!"
- **Line design** (placement of the operative word): **Periodic sentences** (suspense sentences, saving the core idea for the final word: "Then why did you give me that... look? gun? kiss?"; jokes also land their punchline at the tail); **cumulative sentences** (operative word placed upfront followed by modifiers, conversational and natural); **balanced sentences** (core in the center); **parallel structures**; any structure repeated too often becomes wallpaper -> **mix them** (*True Detective*: Rust Cohle balances twenty thoughts with a dozen periodic sentences and mixes the rest).
- **Economy**: "Make every word count"; Walter's Principle 26: **Screenwriters are not paid by the word** (*Escape from Alcatraz*: "What was your childhood like?" — "Short"); with every line ask: Does it advance the plot? Does it reveal anything new? Is there a more compact, efficient way?
- **Pauses**: Tighten before a turning point, absorb after it; "a pause must be hard-won"; Walter: no underlining for emphasis; ellipses once or twice are acceptable, three times is too many (reserve solely for one speaker finishing another's thought); no exclamation marks; almost no parentheticals ("(angrily, face turning pale)" -> spoken brightly, haughtily, innocently "like dangling a treat before a puppy" exposes pathology far better; multi-character scenes needing indication of addressee are an exception).
- **Silence**: When a scene turns talky, write for the eyes—paralanguage (nod, glance, wave) and physical action; Bergman's *The Silence*: the waiter drops a napkin, bends to sniff her from head to foot, she inhales deeply—"the turning point hangs on that single deep breath; silence is the most economical of all languages." Hicks: "The most vivid character may be the one who never speaks" (Tom Harkin); Hoxter exercise: two pages, two characters, only one speaks.
- **Argument** (Walter's Principle 27): Consensus is a major part of life, but not of art—let every line challenge the next; never let two characters fully agree; "view the script as one continuous argument from beginning to end" (*Edison, the Man*: polite, courteous disagreement remains tense).
- **Rhythm**: A daughter under two babbling in her crib, replaying adult conversation: "Di-di-dah? Dah-dah! Dee-dah? Dah!"—not a single real word, yet capturing rises, falls, queries, and pauses with pinpoint precision; "language is music with words." Whoever controls rhythm wins (Tony's staccato fragments vs. Melfi's deliberate drawl).

### Walter's Taboo Checklist
"I think," "well, um," "you know," "listen" are unforgivable; do not transcribe phonetic dialect (John Wayne refused to read *drivin'*); do not pursue "realistic" hemming and hawing ("What is so great about nature? Arsenic, botulism, the flu, and boredom are all natural"); **small talk is permissible only when it transcends itself to connect with theme** (*12 Angry Men*: on the steps, "I don't even know your name"); grand speeches are an exception, but every sentence must deliver information (Patton's opening, Joad's ending, Bernstein's girl with the parasol); "Hi Debbie" "Hi Tom" -> "That dress is very sexy!" — "You like it?" — "Love it!"

### Davies: rhythm revision across speeches and scenes

- Russell T Davies, with Benjamin Cook in *The Writer's Tale*, trims chained clauses into spoken blocks so commas and full stops set when a line is fast, slow, witty, or stark. Use that punctuation as blocking. Worked examples: [reference.md](reference.md).
- Davies spends much of revision cutting accidental word repeats, especially across a scene divide after a break. If a scene's closing speech and the next scene's opening speech both contain "hope", the repetition can make the join jar.
- This is Davies's alternative to Walter's blanket ban on fillers: plant um/er only when hesitancy or fear is the point. Keep Right decisive, Well calmer, So deliberate. Check whole-script repetitions actors may miss.

## 5. Character-Specific Dialogue

- "The more complex the psychology, the more unique the dialogue"; the foundation of dialogue is **vocabulary**—"nothing conveys or betrays character more than their choice of words, the names they give things in their life." From the inside out: content (what is seen and felt yet unsaid and unsayable) -> form (diction) -> effect.
- **Grammar and character**: **Nouns and verbs express scope of knowledge** ("large nail" vs. "shank, clout, spike, plug, brad"; "walks slowly across the room" vs. "ambles, waltzes, drags themselves, struts"); **modifiers, voice, and modality express personality** ("huge" vs. "magnificent"; active "The family planned the wedding" vs. passive "The wedding was planned by the family"; modal "could plan" vs. "must be planned by the family").
- **Culture and character**: Give the character a cultural touchstone to serve as a third element (the Rhode Island bronze statue, *Three Days of the Condor*, yacht acquisitions, Pinot grapes).
- **Principle of creative limitation**: Writing the first hackneyed sentence in mind is far easier than diving into imaginative depths to evoke imagery; "even if a character is inherently dull, their lines must express a lifeless soul—boredom itself must be written vividly."
- Step into the character's subjective lens to view the world: Cassius says "this narrow world" (to a city-state politician, the world is cramped enough for Caesar alone to seize); aristocratic schoolboys "think like wise men, speak like the common folk"—stringing short sentences and monosyllables conveys casual sincerity.
- Case comparisons: *True Detective*: Rust's abstract, multisyllabic Latinate roots vs. Marty's grounded imagery and rhythmic cadence; *30 Rock*: Jack's nouns (incomparable, Bo Derek, elk tongue) and modal verbs (no longer, you cannot, you must) "dress the character inside and out like tailored suits"; *Sideways*: Miles's sensitive modal adjectives (thin-skinned, temperamental, coaxed, delicate, heartbreaking) vs. Maya's bold nouns and verbs (life, sun shining, reached its peak, tastes fantastic).
- **Blake Snyder's cover-the-names test**: Covering the character names, can you still tell who is speaking? If everyone sounds like the screenwriter = "Hi, how are you? I'm fine"; *Big Ugly Baby*: one distinct linguistic fingerprint per person.
- Egri: Dialogue springs from Three-Dimensional Characters and conflict, revealing personality, backstory, and foreshadowing events; economize; **sacrifice brilliant lines for character integrity**; match professional identity (a mechanic views life through mechanical terms); avoid speeches and showing off; jokes must serve another function; arrange lists in escalating order, placing the most important last ("The mayor was there, the governor was there, the president was there!"); *Riders to the Sea*: the Aran Islands accents carry individual cadences.
- Hoxter: Speech patterns expose origins, class, education, occupation, gender, ethnicity; collective does not mean uniform (Who has the heaviest accent? Who relies most on slang?); Shane Black: "slightly heightened reality."
- Lu Jun: Know the person upon hearing the voice—"Every generation is worse than the last," "If you can't find it, take it," "Is there much? Not much," "I suspect every boat on the river"; four men composing lines on a snowy day (merchant: "Heavy snow falls thick upon the ground"; scholar: "All auspicious signs of royal grace"; landlord: "Let it fall three more years, what do I care?"; woodcutter: "Talk out of your ass"); *Prestige on the Train*: sixteen lines and three to four hundred words sketch Mr. Ma and tea-server Xiao Zhao ("Then you can ask the ticket inspector later, I have no objection"—that single "Then" reeks of self-conscious shame).

## 6. Lu Jun: "Language Must Be Beautiful"

- Script language = Stage Directions + spoken dialogue; spoken drama dialogue approaches everyday speech; Chinese opera balances singing and spoken passages; the shared rule is that language must be beautiful.
- **Refined beauty = poeticization** (richly emotional, visual, concise; Lawson: "A playwright who is not a poet is only half a playwright"; Tagore's *Sacrifice*: Raghupati's "The world is a relentless slaughterhouse"; *Romance of the Western Chamber*: "Blue clouds in the sky, yellow petals on the earth"; *The Peony Pavilion*: "Unawares, the water-chestnut mirror steals half my face"—describing looking in a mirror as the mirror looking at the person).
- **Everyday beauty (natural, unvarnished beauty) may be more vital than refined beauty**: The theater audience cannot pause for a single instant; Li Yu: "Take words from street corners and alleyways; take deeds directly stated and plainly told"; plain and everyday does not mean crude and vulgar—shallow words carrying deep meaning (*The Carpenter Breaks the Betrothal*: Peach Leaf's mother: "Hair like a stray hen's nest, clothes filthy like a mud-caked donkey, face like the Kitchen God"; *Eighth Sister Yang Goes for a Spring Outing*: "I want one ounce of moon, two ounces of stars, three ounces of fresh breeze, four ounces of clouds... and leave out Immortal Lu Dongbin"). **In Chinese opera lyrics, everyday beauty should outshine refined beauty, and the same holds for spoken drama**; the key is whether it fits character personality.
- **Three requirements for dialogue**:
  1. **Active quality** (*Thunderstorm*: forcing the medicine: "I told you to kneel!"—impossible to wedge a single word in; Zhou Ping's "Dad! I..." carries boundless weight in just two words);
  2. **Characterization** (Horace: "A god speaks, a hero speaks, a nurse speaks, a peddler speaks—all vastly different"; the worst sin is a line anyone could deliver; Lao She: "Know the person upon hearing the voice");
  3. **Subtext** (Liu Zhiji: "Words near, intent far; language shallow, meaning deep... see one event in the phrase, deduce three corners beyond the text"; *A Marriage Proposal*: "Bring him back!"; *The Grandmother*: "Kurt, why did you ask me if the story happened that way?"—probed three times).
- Lao She: "What is said matters, but how it is said matters far more... a single sigh outweighs an endless torrent; a hesitant word or half a moment's pause often cuts deeper than letting everything spill out."

### Three Essentials for Chinese Opera Lyrics
- **Select rhyme well**: Determine rhyme based on character temperament (the fiery Li Yutao uses the "jiao-xiao" rhyme: "Back, I'm back, team leader's wife Li Yutao"; warm-hearted Pork-Knuckle Mother uses the "jiang-yang" rhyme; frail, gentle Xiao Ai uses the "yi-xi" rhyme); determine rhyme based on the key line (draft the sparkling core lyric first—expressing theme / depicting emotion / revealing relational shifts—then fix the rhyme around it: *Bamboo Garden Tune*: "Your single word 'cruel' passes like wind" fixes the "wu-hu" rhyme, unfurling the parallel structure of "twenty years": "For whom have I bled and wept for twenty years?"); broad rhymes ("ren-chen", "jiang-yang", "yan-qian", abundant in words, easy to write, prone to sameness) vs. narrow rhymes ("ye-xie", "fa-hua", scarce in words, but fresh when apt: *A Night of Life and Death*: "The toad... nine out of ten shake their heads at the sight" counting backwards); the charm of **monorhyme using identical words** (dozens of "hand," nearly a hundred "person," "you," "I": Doctor Hong: "Lying to you was protecting you... never a peaceful you in a lifetime").
- **Weave structure well**: Avoid uniform seven-character or ten-character lines throughout; borrow diverse qupai sentence patterns ("Yin-Yang Blood" question-and-answer, "Fuzi-ban" narrative); shift with environment and performance format (trio, chorus, off-stage vocal, and antiphonal singing for the sedan-chair dance); palindromic phrasing evokes entangled heartache ("Troubled with no heart, I fail others; others fail my heart with no trouble")—sentence lengths and tune structures must avoid rigid uniformity.
- **Arrange placement well**: Avoid "singing when speech is called for, or speaking when song is demanded" and "scattering bits and pieces everywhere like chicken scratch"; carefully design major scenes (emotional outpourings of protagonists, fierce conflict, pivotal turning points of fate) into cohesive musical blocks; all enduring historic arias stand out from heavy dramatic scenes.

## 7. Comedy Dialogue

- Comic characters are driven by **blind obsession**; rigid thinking is what creates humor (short-sighted pursuit of immediate desire); "for a comedy writer, empathy is death"; the audience requires calm, safe distance; comic protagonists lack subconscious self-contradiction, replaced instead by surface vs. reality; minor characters carry only a single trait: "many comedies fail the moment supporting characters open their mouths with clichés."
- **Four techniques**:
  1. Clarity (clear from Subtext onward, minimal and sharpest words);
  2. Exaggeration (making a mountain out of a molehill: "You stole my mommy," or treating grave matters flippantly);
  3. Timing (setup—payoff; the punch lands on the sentence's final word with nothing following: "never step on your own laugh");
  4. Incongruity (civilized adults brawling like toddlers). Trivializing the noble; Subtext rising to the surface can be written on-the-nose; downward spiral from personality -> appearance -> emotion -> violence.
- Three energy systems of comedy: defensive emotions, aggressive emotions, sex; joke = setup / payoff.
- **Screwball comedy conventions**: Narrative weight always rests on scenes where the leads spar, roast, and outwit each other; the female lead must remain formidable and hold her ground; dialogue conceals sexual tension within Subtext; rapid-fire delivery maximized through editing; lines double as setups (*The Lady Eve*: "Washington and Valley Forge," "Napoleon and Josephine," "I need him like the ax needs the turkey"); pioneered the "sex comedy without sex" and authentic sound comedy.
- Indick: Place jokes immediately before or after peak tension; breaking taboos yields cheap laughs, but the finest humor is driven by character psychology. Field: Comedy = creating a situation where characters react to it with deadly seriousness; Woody Allen: "Playing funny is the worst thing you can do."

## 8. Dialogue in the Scene: Beat Analysis Method (McKee)

1. Before writing any scene, define each character's **desire / antagonism / scene intention / strategy** (conscious layer) + **super-intention** (subconscious layer, often opposing characterization).
2. Lay out 2–3 pairs of values at stake, marking opening charges; write beat by beat using "action/reaction" gerunds + Subtext + value shifts, confirming no repetitive beats, that each beat builds, and that the turning point arrives neither early nor late.
3. Contrast the two characters via content / length / rhythm.
4. Six conflict structures, each with its own approach: Balanced (*The Sopranos*: Tony vs. Melfi), Comic (*Frasier*), Asymmetrical (*A Raisin in the Sun*: one presses while the other evades), Indirect (*The Great Gatsby*: resentment and manipulation masked through politeness), Reflexive (*Fräulein Else*: speaking self against silent self), Micro-boundary (*Lost in Translation*: confession replacing argument, pauses opening doors to reflection).
5. Build the story before writing dialogue: Inciting Incident -> core value -> five dimensions of desire (object / super-intention / motivation / scene intention / background desire) -> four levels of antagonism -> spine of action -> scene turning points (action or revelation) -> beats -> **five steps of behavior** (desire -> awareness of antagonism -> choice of action -> action -> expression). "A scene does not live in the act of speaking, but in the action a character takes through speech."
6. High-context (Mafia: "an offer he can't refuse," understood instantly by insiders) vs. low-context cultures dictate the text/Subtext ratio.

## 9. Diagnostic Checklist

1. What is the action beneath this line (gerund)? What does the character want, and what strategy are they using? If unanswered, cut or rewrite.
2. Covering character names, can you tell who is speaking? Do nouns and verbs show their scope of knowledge, and do modifiers reveal personality?
3. Is any character telling another facts both already know? Is it ammunition? If not, cut it.
4. Is it on-the-nose (Subtext made text)? Can a candlelit "I love you" be acted? Exceptions: only "What do you really want?", "Are you okay?", and non-realistic genres.
5. Is the two-hander stiff? Find a third element to convert it into a trialogue.
6. How many times has the same beat repeated? A third crying scene turns into comedy.
7. Does the final word of every speech carry the operative punch? Is meaning timed neither too early nor too late?
8. Are there "I think," "you know," "listen," "hi," "oh," phonetic dialect, underlining, exclamation marks, more than two ellipses, or parenthetical acting directions?
9. Does every line of a long monologue deliver fresh information? Can it be broken into action/reaction beats?
10. Do two characters agree anywhere? (Agreement is boring.)
11. Can this scene dispense with dialogue entirely? Replace it with a deep breath or the removal of a ring?
12. Cliché: Was this your first choice? Brainstorm wild alternatives before choosing.
13. Does the scene feel melodramatic due to inadequate motivation? Would an understated version cut deeper?
14. Opera lyrics: Does the rhyme fit the character? Is the key line established first? Are line lengths stuck in uniform seven- or ten-character blocks? Are arias concentrated in high-stakes scenes?

## 10. Exercises

- Eavesdrop on text and Subtext (Isaac Bashevis Singer listening to kitchen maids through floor cracks), note new vocabulary, coin new phrases; read great screenplays, **rewrite bad dialogue** ("the fastest, most effective way to train the craft").
- **Write from inside the character**: Stanislavski's "Magic If"—"What would I do if I were this character in this situation?" (not "What would I do?" nor "What would he do?"); act it out before a mirror like Dickens; take improvisation classes if necessary; write from the inside out, evaluate from the outside in, then view it as an audience member for the first time.
- Hicks's five-step exercise: Write a two-page dialogue between a man and a woman ending in "...okay?" -> rewrite using **zero pronouns** except in the opening and closing lines -> rewrite again **cutting all negative assertions** -> record ten minutes of real conversation, transcribe ten pages verbatim, and condense to two pages -> hand-copy two pages of master dialogue to absorb cadence.
- Hoxter exercise: Delay (three strategies to avoid directly asking for time, met with three opposing strategies); power of silence; two characters each wanting something from the other across three beats, with at least one polarity reversal and a clear winner, driven purely by dialogue without physical action.
- McKee's key question checklist (background desire / object of desire / super-intention / scene intention / motivation / scene driver / antagonism / values / Subtext / beats / progression / strategy / turning point / deep character / scene progression -> text / exposition / characterization): Ask each question from every character's perspective—before, during, and after writing.

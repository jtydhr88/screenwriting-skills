# Korean & French Screenwriting Practice (韩国与法国编剧方法)

From three masters in the first four lectures of Liu Dapeng, editor, *Story Creation Masterclass, International Volume (Chinese original, no English edition)*: Oh Seung-uk (Korea; *Christmas in August*, *Green Fish*, *The Shameless*), Choi Seok-hwan (Korea; *King and the Clown*, *Lost in Hong Kong*), and Frédéric Auburtin (France; *Paris, je t'aime*, *Envoyés très spéciaux*, *The Bridge*). For Arai Haruhiko and the ten Japanese directors, see `sw-japanese-screenwriting`; for the four Hollywood lectures, see `sw-american-case-studies` and `sw-industry-business`.

Shared by all three: **field research before writing; read the script aloud to a hostile audience; and with producers, "convince them if you can; if you can't, accept defeat and grit your teeth to carry on."** Differences: Oh Seung-uk puts emotion and physical space first; Choi Seok-hwan the genre contract and the crisis/desire double helix; Auburtin dialogue last and involvement through post-production.

## I. Oh Seung-uk: Screenwriting Is a Profession That Demands Endurance

### Depicting the Emotion
- Producers first dismissed *Christmas in August* as "a novice script with zero commercial viability": action beats read like short fiction (washing hands: a red basin, a crisp clatter, dark specks of raindrops soaking into a white dress shirt), after Lee Chang-dong's remark that "what 1990s Korean cinema lacks most is a specific emotion." Self-critique: too many adjectives and adverbs blur focus; clinical objectivity leaves producers and audiences cold.
- Subverting the date trope ("come chase me"): Hur Jin-ho had the male lead tell a ghost story instead—real warmth, no sentimentality.
- A terminal-illness film without weeping: the protagonist's limited days announced at the start, no tears manufactured after the romance: "Given how much genuine tragedy exists in the world, exploiting it for commercial gain is simply unethical."
- Fieldwork for *Christmas in August* with terminally ill patients: cooking instant noodles late at night, riding bicycles, eating watermelon and horsing around with a younger sister, weeping under the blankets—"being able to manage one's own daily life does not mean living in perpetual gloom" -> cleaning a fountain pen, writing VCR instructions for his father.

### Research in the Field Before Writing
- Park Kwang-su studied an isolated island's soil color, tree shapes, and local conflicts before writing a word; Lee Chang-dong scouted on foot (reservoir flood discharges displacing villagers -> a farmer who lost his land selling chicken soup; "the moment you drive a car, you stop thinking like a writer").
- **Undercover fieldwork**: a handsome young assistant sent into a detective squad for a week to document arrests; Oh Seung-uk as a bag-carrying, floor-sweeping secretary for gangsters (squabbles over cut shares "like immature teenage standoffs"); interviewing hostesses about clothes, makeup brands, vocal inflections, and cigarette burns on their hands; male nightclub patrons gave fresher insights; "if interviewees resist speaking to a screenwriter, delegate someone else or claim to be an apprentice production assistant; when subjects feel superior, they boast and spill details"; "Korean screenwriters reject interviews where their identities are exposed, choosing instead to blend in under disguise."
- **Imagination alone cannot shape authentic characters** (the female lead in *The Shameless*): a follow-up interview yielded two real details (renting luxury garments, collecting debts in person) -> "a nightclub hostess marching into a corporate office to demand unpaid escort fees from a boss is brilliant dramatic conflict," then imagine the male lead trailing her.
- **The truth-telling liar**: Lee Chang-dong tells personal experiences in detached third person; for what is too humiliating to confess, become a third-party observer and perfect liar: "Conveying truth through lies is the ultimate goal of the screenwriter."

### Structure and Physical Space
- **Design the first and last acts first**: nothing moves until Acts One and Three are pinned down; *Kilimanjaro* began with an image in the snows of Gangwon Province—"a dying man lying flat while another kneels before him in absolute silence"—and built backward; the final scene of *The Shameless*—"from afar, a lover's embrace; up close, a woman plunging a knife into a man's gut"; three months trying to fit an investor's mandatory opening (dumping a corpse from an old van on a rainy night) before admitting it would not fit.
- **For 120 scenes, write 60 first and leave the rest blank**; driving aimlessly with the production designer through rundown Incheon, improvising: "A woman walking out of that apartment building over there is the target we've been tailing."
- **Space as the film's skeleton**: the female lead moves three times (an upscale white-collar apartment waiting for her lover -> chased out to a cheap rental -> a suburban slum marking total degradation, "the floors getting higher and more decrepit"); slaughterhouse dormitories partitioned like wasp nests, twenty-odd pairs of shoes at the door.
- Divide acts by emotional arc, not page count (*Christmas in August* spends roughly 30 scenes each on Acts One and Two, only 10 on Act Three); check your watch at films; favor endings where the audience thinks the film is over yet the story pushes on (in *The Shameless*, the pursuit of the female lead after the arrest is Act Three).
- **Transition point fatigue**: audiences check their watches at minute 60 of a 90-minute film, minute 70 of a 120-minute film, or scene 70 of a 120-scene script; "the most boring phase of any film is the transition point," yet masterworks place emotional entanglements exactly there; many directors wedge in mismatched set pieces or steal tropes.
- **The climax is hardest to write**: where Act Two stakes detonate (the male lead decides to betray the female lead, using her to execute the criminal) still needs two to three bridge scenes before the physical climax.
- Transition with visceral impact: while writing one scene, visualize the next scene's opening image (a gale at the back, a full moon); the opening minute of *The Shameless* tracks a figure from behind -> the face emerges in a mirror.
- Undercover premise: every word from the male lead is a lie; the female lead never lies—a nightclub manager remarks, "The only counterattack we have left is telling the truth in every single sentence."

### Finding an Angle You Love
- First principle: logical coherence; second: entertainment (subjective; bringing the audience to your perspective takes persistent effort); "Only Oh Seung-uk can write an Oh Seung-uk script."
- Unconventional perspectives from classical literature: *Water Margin* (Li Kui's and Wu Dalang's viewpoints), Chang Cheh's *Delightful Forest* (Wu Song as a bloodthirsty brute); Nie Zheng in *The Assassin* ("a mediocre man who must repay the master who recognized his worth" as nuance); *Water Margin*'s minor characters inspired a film built solely around a detective and a criminal's lover.
- Subverting the hardboiled archetype: the male lead gets an androgynous, even feminine softness, opening listless and drained; to investors complaining about shots tracking him from behind ("Why are we seeing so little of his face?"): "When acting is masterful, the way a character walks is the character."
- Care more for the antagonist than the protagonist; put in your worst deeds and the worst cruelties done to you; disguise observations of friends and family, or "you will end up without a single friend left."
- "The day I call my own film an art film is the day I should retire" — commit fully to a perfected commercial product.

### Working with Producers and Directors
- **Read the script aloud to a hostile audience**: five or six peers who know screenwriting and hold grudges from your past critiques, read to from morning to night; pitching an exposed back in a bathhouse to convey exhaustion and being told "You've lost your mind" -> working ten times harder on setups; assigning attendees roles to stage the romantic subplot.
- **"Take my name off the credits"** is your ultimate weapon; handling conflicting demands from several financiers and indecisive lead actors; convincing the director to revert to the first draft before approaching talent (the shared understanding over spitting watermelon seeds) -> investors trickling back.
- Loved scenes always get cut: plant them at critical junctures (moving a French priest teaching a girl to waltz on his shoes into the desperate climax of a besieged city) so they "become structurally indispensable"; one or two deleted scenes often hold the work's artistic soul and need salvage.
- The darkest hour: a director, at first ecstatic, reversing 180 degrees after several financier meetings, leaving the revised script "completely soulless"; when rewriting, "never lose your original premise; try your best to convince them to minimize changes, as the statistical probability of a producer pitching a great idea is vanishingly low."
- Passing the draft opens the gates of hell: a boutique production outfit demanding, "Turn every character into an ultra-wealthy elite and move the story to Gangnam to show glittering luxury" — "I have never experienced a rich person's life; I cannot write a single word of that"; turning a boss's improvised hotel anecdote into an opening of three characters staring awkwardly at each other; compromising to expand the criminal's screen time; pressure to put martial arts in the finale, yielding "a fight scene directed with zero sincerity."
- On-set rewrites: unable to solve the leads' first meeting on the page, the writer left it to the set, the actors telling the director, "Trust us, we will play it right"; "Give thanks to the air and breeze of that day" — the screenplay is only the skeleton.
- The greatest motivator is the deadline (enjoy the first two weeks of a three-week crunch, then weep through the final days); "Writing a screenplay is like staying up all night writing a love letter to someone you adore, only to tear it to shreds in shame the next morning."
- Lee Chang-dong asked daily: "Is this scene useful, entertaining, and essential to this film's existence?" If doubts remained after seven or eight rounds, he conceded: "Let it be, then."
- Rewriting *The Shameless* across ten years, rejected by six production companies and seven or eight A-list actors: "Endurance is the single defining keyword."

## II. Choi Seok-hwan: How Genre Cinema Tells a Story

### The Industry and the Trio Model
- Korean cinema history: translated Japanese novels -> indigenous pulp -> 1980s erotic cinema (every four domestic features earned one foreign import quota) -> 1990s new wave genre films -> full market liberalization in 2006, still delivering hits; "Only China, Korea, and India boast domestic industries capable of standing toe-to-toe with Hollywood."
- **Close as family**: the producer stands in the audience's shoes; the director is a vampire thirsting for narrative material; the screenwriter is "treated like a streetwalker," forced to cater to the producer; the three live, eat, and brainstorm under one roof — "the hallmark of Korean film development, and the secret edge over Hollywood."
- Master liars: "Lying must be executed with vivid entertainment"; the caveman returning empty-handed pantomimes wrestling a bear; scamming book money from mother by claiming it went to a freezing beggar brings her to tears: "Your deceit has to operate on a higher moral plane." The three-act structure is "the supreme truth of the screenwriter."
- Choosing your real estate: mega-mall spectacles ($100M+ budgets) vs. boutique department stores; the author opts for the family-friendly crowd-pleaser; "If luck strikes and you sell the script, cash out immediately and move on to write something better."

### Fidelity to Genre Is a Promise to the Audience
- Structure is invisible to audiences but vital for screenwriters (like a skyscraper's structural safety); genre follows set rules, like department store floor plans; **in a comedy the laughs never stop (even if the male lead dies tragically at the end, the death must still get a laugh); in a mystery, however many jokes you crack, never forget the puzzle**; enrich the ingredients but keep one spine; "Genre is a contractual promise made the moment the poster is viewed and the ticket bought; you cannot cheat the audience; innovation should sit within a modest 5–10% window"; when mashing genres, "each individual genre component must score 100%, unified under a single cohesive tone."
- Details are interior decorating: never build a gold palace to house a solid gold toilet; if the architecture holds, flaws in detail can be polished later.

### Make the Audience Wait, Rejoice, and Savor
- An overlong, sluggish opening act is common and survivable if the middle and ending grip; inverted, the film is doomed.
- **Deconstructing Grandmother's Folk Tale** (the tiger and the rice cakes): brief introductions -> Inciting Incident -> **qualitative shift, not mere quantitative escalation** (the rice cake was an offering -> the mother becomes the next offering; "an army of ten million following an army of one million" is only quantitative) -> suspense and velocity -> the resolution demands a reversal (a rope dropped from heaven) -> **a second rope drops for a second reversal** — "a single reversal is never enough; exhaust your imagination to force two reversals into the finale" -> **bittersweet** (grace into tragedy; sorrow into the happy ending); reversals must never feel forced; "a rope dropping from heaven works in a fairy tale; pulling that off in a movie will ruin you."

### Colliding with the Real World
- The original premise of *Lost in Hong Kong* (a father with three months to live helping his family fulfill their dreams) was questioned in China; Xu Zheng pivoted to broader comedy: "The further you cross boundaries to tap into primal human desires, the more intoxicating the film is to audiences" — cinema permits what reality forbids.
- Self-interrogation: What makes me anxious, joyful, enraged, introspective, or ashamed? What unresolved past interrogates my present? Great screenplays come in only two kinds: virgin subject matter, or a fresh retelling of an existing myth.
- No great screenwriter lacks messy real-life entanglements; male writers gravitate to fathers and sons, female writers to mothers and daughters; write what fascinates you, not trends; subject matter that provokes moral outrage often succeeds; in period drama, ask: "Why this story now, and what insight does it offer the world today?"

### Desire and Crisis
- **Deepening crisis in the story environment paired with intensifying desire in the protagonist**; the protagonist needs clear motivation and an enduring, specific terror; audiences reject flawless protagonists and mirror images of themselves; they want vicarious escape; the pursuit must be fierce, the tempo relentless.
- The tycoon example: external conditions worsen + internal greed grows monstrous; a supporting character asking, "Did your family really want you making money like this?" is enough to register family love as the emotional core.
- **The protagonist must undergo a qualitative transformation, even across a single night** (like a traveler back from a five-year voyage).
- Supporting characters do not serve the lead; they are the absolute protagonists of their own lives, often carrying the story's deepest thematic values; define a petty, short-statured character by objective and trigger (exploding when mocked for his height vs. walking away in icy silence only when his height is mocked).
- Always ask: "What price does the protagonist pay, and what must they surrender?" Making another $1M with $100M in hand is dull; risking $100M to chase $1M is riveting; the man devoted to his Persian cat collapses the moment the cat vanishes.
- **Failing to lock the ending in advance is suicidal**: "You can drive toward Beijing or you can drive toward Tianjin, but you must choose"; resolving the external crisis at the climax does not finish the story; the psyche at the climax must contrast with the final resolution to spark a secondary emotional climax; never let characters mouth the theme—convey it visually (leaping off a train bound for glory to sprint toward an impoverished lover).
- "If a story is written well, no one asks about the theme" — being asked "What were you trying to say?" signals poor execution; minimize expository voiceover, worst of all the protagonist recapping yesterday's plot over morning coffee; unavoidable exposition goes into a fierce argument; television tolerates mundane banter, cinema demands spectacular conflict.
- Two keys to crime cinema: intense criminal motivations rarely seen in real life; plausible criminal realities captured with meticulous authenticity. "Scripts written with the hand rank lower; scripts written with the feet rank supreme." Tsui Hark: "If there is one great screenwriter left on this earth, I would drag him out of hell itself."

## III. Auburtin: The Writer-Director Hybrid Model

### The French System
- The auteur tradition (Marcel Carné and Jacques Prévert; the Nouvelle Vague made the director primary author, with final cut); CNC (Centre national du cinéma) levies a box-office tax redistributed to domestic filmmakers (even Hollywood releases pay in); nearly every project pre-sells to television, whose prime-time demand for broad appeal is "an ongoing wound to creative freedom," with literary adaptation the main escape hatch.
- Gérard Brach (Polanski and Annaud's long-time collaborator), an agoraphobic recluse in bed with his cat watching muted television: "A screenwriter doesn't even need to leave the house; pure imagination matters most"; "Your mind must bubble like boiling water without pause."
- Adapting plays offers ready-made structure, but avoid filmed theater; cut original characters and subplots freely, then invent new ones; expand three-dimensional physical space; give the audience breathing room after every burst of emotion.

### Writing Dialogue Last
- Too many French screenplays shoot before the writing is resolved, guaranteeing failure; "All structural problem-solving occurs in the mind; putting words on paper is merely the final step"; a novice who writes "INT. DAY - LOCATION" then dives into dialogue makes a fatal error.
- Jean-Paul Rappeneau (*Cyrano de Bergerac*, veteran script doctor, office walls covered in index cards) works a strict pipeline: treatment -> structure -> dialogue last; for every sequence, ask why it is handled this way and what makes it structurally necessary.
- Rhythm is comedy's lifeblood: Billy Wilder's *Some Like It Hot* works because characters wriggle out of impossible predicaments through situation, not punchlines; in a master comedy the very first shot gets a laugh; "Laughing after three hundred viewings" is the benchmark.
- **Manufacture catastrophes for your characters**: "Pain and misfortune are the true springs of comedy"; "Happy people have no story to tell"; *La Grande Vadrouille* mined comedy from Nazi occupation. Francis Veber's script for *Les Compères* ran 13 dense pages with every punctuation mark deliberate; **read the script aloud** (to an assistant, a spouse, or a goldfish); every read-through filters, like rainwater seeping through bedrock into groundwater.
- **Twelve lines of dialogue, not thirteen** (Clint Eastwood's mandate to emerging writers): silence outweighs speech, as pauses carry more tension than notes; great dialogue carries Subtext beneath surface exposition, "unlocking an entirely different door"; build character biographies before writing dialogue (in *Quartier Latin*, "Sorry I'm late" means "I didn't want to come, so I made you wait on purpose"; "You look well" means "I have too much breeding to insult you to your face" — verbal boxing matches); **show what can be seen**; never use dialogue for what can be shown (television talks endlessly).

### Perspective and Hidden Agendas
- Dramatic setups need a hidden truth: the audience knows what the characters do not (the aggressor is having an affair with the victim's wife), or the characters know far more than the audience; be your own first reader, judging the draft through three lenses: omniscient auteur/director, experiencing audience, immediate protagonist; "Trust the audience's intelligence, and you can construct intricate plots"; cinema assumes the audience is brilliant, television assumes it is slow.
- Reality is funnier than fiction: Truffaut clipped bizarre gossip from daily newspapers for screenplays; journalist-screenwriters bring institutional authenticity.
- **Afford your characters dignity**: never humiliate immoral figures in their downfall (Chaplin kicking a police officer's backside, then pausing to straighten his tattered suit); the Hollywood-style ending a famous screenwriter had scripted for *The Bridge* in fidelity to the novel (the wife jumps from the bridge, the husband rescues her under exploding fireworks) was filmed and cut but felt "stylistically grotesque"; reshot as the two parting quietly—"You go your way, I'll go mine"—exiting with tenderness and dignity: **the ending's tone must harmonize with the whole film**.

### The Screenwriter's Total Involvement
- Four phases (development, pre-production, production, post-production); "I have never once encountered a script ready to shoot straight out of the envelope"; all screenplays run too long, and "we can trim it in post" is an illusion—read aloud to find cuts early; an on-set writer answers 100-plus questions a day ("Why is he wearing this tie?"); "we'll fix it in the edit" is malpractice, so anticipate pitfalls early; the jazz metaphor: filmmaking needs at least a three-piece ensemble in sync.
- **The Italian-style table read**: map every beat on a structural chart first, knowing the narrative well enough to shuffle scene order at will; hold a two-to-three-day table read where actors read every word aloud—scene headings, stage directions, and timestamps included—without acting; the sound recordist logs every scene; listening back before production lets the whole architecture, even the score, coalesce.
- Share the script with producers and talent as late as possible (first impressions cannot be erased); never seek early feedback from industry acquaintances you hardly know; friends and colleagues offer twin pitfalls (polite praise / bitter fights from blunt honesty) — find a trusted co-writer or write alongside the director.
- "The single hard metric of a script worth making is the sheer volume of time invested; rushing the cycle never yields great work"; start by fetching coffee as an assistant director to learn every crew department's craft; the screenwriter is the composer, the director the conductor.

### Commissioned Work and Collective Creation
- ***United Passions*** (FIFA centennial commission, 18-month crunch, written amid corruption scandals): the first draft was rejected as "far too hagiographic"; FIFA held final script approval — **there is always an ingenious way to protect your vision**: Sepp Blatter consulting his predecessor was staged with visual motifs evoking Vito Corleone in *The Godfather*, against a massive Swiss flag behind a boat, in long takes to resist cuts to "this tiny shred of artistic control"; an African delegate receives an envelope (the audience assumes a bribe) -> next scene he holds it to camera: "Thank you for gifting me this World Cup Final ticket"; the Adidas CEO holds an ominous briefcase -> next scene it opens, empty except for soccer shoe sketches. "When confronted with brick walls, never surrender easily."
- ***Envoyés très spéciaux***: two radio correspondents hide in an imam friend's Paris apartment, faking sound effects to convince listeners they are reporting live from war-torn Iraq; the Act Two climax turns on a faked hostage video; reviewing real decapitation footage forced a reckoning with ethical limits: "Comedy rejects the mundane and demands extreme scenarios, but it must never recklessly broadcast cruel reality."
- ***Paris, je t'aime***: twenty directors across twenty arrondissements, each with two shooting days and a five-minute limit, Auburtin directing the transitions; directors delivered rogue storylines that broke cohesion (the first assembly ran toward four hours with little narrative logic); early test screenings: bloated, disjointed, Paris invisible, themes adrift -> with the editor he distilled an "emotional spine" into a clean three-act arc (curiosity and humor / mortality, displacement, and isolation / transcendence and resolution); testing **82 distinct cuts, ignoring narrative logic to honor pure emotional flow** (moving the mime couple from bookends into the midsection, cutting straight to a grieving mother, then to a boy with a backpack — pure Kuleshov effect); weaving character portraits into a mosaic showing that "every Parisian shares one collective story"; "You must remain confident and experiment without ceasing"; "Every rule has its exceptions."
- Gérard Depardieu: "Find a partner to split your anxieties with; anxiety is indispensable to an artist"; Woody Allen and Stanley Kubrick had the clout to scrap footage and recast mid-production, but normally the screenwriter must settle questions upfront to avoid reshoots.

## IV. Directly Actionable Rules

1. Research in the field before writing: inspect the soil, trees, and local tensions on location; interview in disguise or send proxies undercover; "Screenplays written with the feet rank supreme."
2. Depict emotion through concrete sensory detail (the color of a basin, dark specks of rain), but beware adjective-heavy prose that blurs focus; transition between scenes with jarring visual collisions.
3. Lock Act One and Act Three first (or anchor to a definitive closing image); for 120 scenes, write 60 and leave deliberate negative space; use physical space (three successive residences) as the skeleton.
4. Divide the three acts by emotional arc, not page count; watch the clock while screening films; get past the dangerous "transition point" at minute 60 or 70 with interpersonal friction.
5. Once the first draft is done, read it aloud to hostile peers or assign parts; reading aloud is progressive filtration; use the Italian-style table read.
6. Wedge cherished scenes into structurally vital turning points so they cannot be cut; stay anchored to your premise in rewrites—"the odds of a producer offering a great idea are low"—convince them if you can, accept defeat if you cannot.
7. The screenwriter is a truth-telling liar: disguise raw autobiographical trauma in detached third person.
8. Genre is an ironclad contract with the audience (comedies sustain laughs; mysteries honor the puzzle), driven by a single spine with innovation capped at 5–10%; genre-bending requires every component to score 100%.
9. Couple a deepening crisis with intensifying protagonist desire; give clear motivation backed by an enduring vulnerability; make the price paid outweigh the prize; force a qualitative character shift even across a single night; lock climax and resolution upfront; engineer two reversals and a bittersweet landing.
10. Theme emerges through dramatic action, never preaching; carry exposition in fierce arguments, not coffee-table chats or car conversations.
11. Write dialogue last: treatment, structure, then dialogue; challenge every scene with "Why this way?"; build character biographies before drafting lines for layered Subtext; silence beats chatter (twelve lines of dialogue); never use dialogue for what can be shown.
12. Manipulate perspective: let the audience know what the characters do not, or let characters withhold what the audience craves; read your draft through three alternating lenses: authorial god, objective audience, subjective protagonist.
13. Pain and misfortune are comedy's true springs—inflict disasters on your characters; drive comedy through physical predicaments and rhythm, not quips.
14. Stay embedded through pre-production, shooting, and post, expecting 100 daily on-set questions; hold scripts back from producers and talent until polished; seek development partners, not casual friendly feedback.
15. Commissioned and collective projects: every rule has its exceptions; against censorship or studio interference, defend your vision with visual metaphors and retroactive scene justifications; in the edit, rebuild the sequence around emotional logic.
16. Afford your characters dignity: no cheap humiliation when punishing flawed figures; keep the ending's tone faithful to the whole film.

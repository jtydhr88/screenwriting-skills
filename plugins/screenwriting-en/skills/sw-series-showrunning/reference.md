# Series design reference (剧集设计参考)

Detailed grids and examples live here. Grace's full storyline document template lives in `sw-format-adaptation`. Douglas four-act plan is a planning tool. Five-act and six-act notes are separate. Do not convert one show's timings onto another. Do not treat A/B/C beat ranges or ~28 scenes as quotas. Yorke Criminal Justice is one season reading, not a mandatory episode map.

## I. Blank four-act planning grid (Douglas)

Douglas’s blank four-act grid is four act columns with seven optional dramatic scene slots in each. A teaser and a tag are separate optional units you add only when the actual series format uses them.

Douglas first-draft page hints if the show has a teaser: Act One ~17–18, Act Two ~30, Act Three ~45, Act Four ~50–60. Commercial rhythm she describes is roughly 10–15 minute curtains on traditional network hours; the produced hour is under 50 minutes after ads. Use the show's real pages.

Tent-poles, same order as the skill: opening, ending, Act Three worst case, then Act One and Act Two cliffhangers. Reverse-engineer beats into the jeopardy. Cliffhangers need not be A-story. Checkerboard order has no required look.

## II. Five-act and six-act variants (Douglas)

Do not merge these timings into one table.

- Observation: many five-act hours are Act Four of a four-act plan split in half; look for the two shortest acts.
- Smallville (Kelly Souders to Douglas): used to be four acts with a long Act One; they split Act One (three scenes and four; it used to be six to seven). Became teaser plus two small acts because Act One ended on an action beat.
- Douglas suggestion if you start from four-act and the show needs more breaks: lengthen what was the teaser to around ten pages as a new Act One, open on jeopardy or a provocative issue; later acts roughly ten pages. Suggested re-plan, not a mandate.
- Six-act: acts around eight pages; Act Six may be a tag, last shoe, or twist, sometimes as short as about five minutes.
- Nip/Tuck: writers completed dramatic arcs at end of Act Five and used a short sixth act as a twist that incited the next episode rather than concluding the current line (six-act or teaser plus five).
- House (Peter Blake to Douglas): write four-act, then add two breaks. Medicine path, diagnosis, theory/treatment, end-of-act it fails. Beat medicine, then personal; divide the longest later act.
- Dexter (Wendy West to Douglas): no formal aired breaks. Break by character beginning/middle/end, weave. Big reversals in the Dexter story, often ~page 40–45 of a ~55-page script. Character-first, not act-first.
- Premium cable examples she names (HBO/Showtime) may lack act breaks.
- ABC once mandated six acts for new shows in the period she discusses; Grey's Anatomy and Lost are her 2006 five-act examples. First drafts of some hours (House, Breaking Bad, The Wire) still showed four-act planning.

Teaser examples (Douglas, study the show): detective crime discovered or enacted; Miami Vice lush crime without main cast; House guest vignette before doctors; Law & Order cast arriving after; Good Wife / Grey's ensemble morning launch; Six Feet Under thematic death; Oz monologue; Dexter psychological world. Prefer story tension over theme lectures when new to the form.

## III. Beats, outlines, A/B/C scale (Douglas)

Dramatic beat: motivated want versus opposition. Slug lines number locations for production; they are not automatically scenes. Outline beats numbered per act, short like scene log lines, headed by slugs. Same location plus new conflict = new beat. Fragment of another arc at a convergence may be noted after the scene.

Beat sheet (some producers): turns by act, not every scene. Beat outline: skeletal list of what will be written. Color cards per story, then assemble. If A balloons past a usable hour, condense fragments or cut side motion. If too thin, split combined cards or marry a larger line. ~28 scenes and A12–16 / B8–12 / C3–4 up to 8 are illustration of unevenness, not required totals.

Log line test: Jane/Sally must own the ledge, not Portia the guest burglar.

## IV. Season optional chart (Douglas) and fractal season (Yorke / Katims)

Horizontal color-coded lines for five or so main roles across episodes 1–22, then vertical slice of intersections. Alternative: accumulate bible facts as scripts invent them.

Yorke: Criminal Justice five episodes as one example of series-level inciting incident / midpoint / worst point / resolution mirroring episode shape. Mini-series: each episode can function as an act of the whole (six-parter midpoint in episode three; two-parter cliff as midpoint). Homeland cabin midpoint illustrates fractal pattern. Doctor Who Dalek then army; penultimate deaths in Forbrydelsen, Walking Dead, Treme, The Wire, Game of Thrones are examples of late-series cost.

Katims (Landau interview): vague season journeys, then 4–6 episode movements around a question (Friday Night Lights football calendar; Parenthood bigger season lines such as diagnosis or wedding).

## V. Grace distances, rank, and soap concurrency (Writing for Television)

Three distances (Grace, pp. 59–60; length dictated by the series):

| Distance | Span | Example shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short | 2–3 episodes | Blast from the past that ends with no rekindling |
| Longer | 3–4 episodes | Grace: a broke character wagers a month’s wages on one lottery ticket, does not win, gets fighting drunk, hits rock bottom, then seeks help and starts recovery. |
| Full | Length the series will bear | Missing child with the mother as prime suspect |

A storyline may occupy about a third of a script; another the first two thirds and resolve by the last act; another the full span. Rank before weave: A central to this episode; B most impact/resonance; C undercuts, contrasts, or augments. Four or five concurrent soap lines over a week is normal. Process: identify the through-line; mark the broad episodes it occupies; list affected characters; chart each journey; then build connections and parallels. Series: open and return around standing cast/precinct. Serial: overarching resolution. Soap: no definable ending.

## VI. Engine, mystery, continuity notes

Rabkin's imagined dial compares interest in the initial hook with interest in the ongoing weekly series. Outlet pacing is a separate consideration: some specs hide the series-defining event at act four; he often says move it up and sustain. Streaming vs weekly: House-like identical machinery can numb in a binge; closed resets can feel finished; serialization can pull the next chapter. Not a single correct speed.

Landau: mythology = rules; mystery = past; question = future. Sweet spot = weekly promise. Pilot aha = earned discovery. Six loglines, brief, not plots of the whole season.

Curry’s method: one writer’s sulks become the next’s open grievance, then despair, then showdown across four neighboring episodes. Documented history, set furniture, and each character’s emotional state travel with the pickup; the start accounts for the prior hook and the destination is this episode’s agreed cliff.

Sources: Pamela Douglas, Writing the TV Drama Series; William Rabkin, Writing the Pilot: Creating the Series; Neil Landau, The TV Showrunner's Roadmap; Yvonne Grace, Writing for Television; Chris Curry, Writing for Soaps; John Yorke, Into the Woods.

# Chekhov Dramaturgy (契诃夫戏剧法)

This skill is distilled from an act-by-act breakdown of the four-volume *The Complete Plays of Chekhov* (translated into Chinese by Jiao Juyin, Li Jianwu, and Tong Daoming): *Fatherlessness* (*Platonov*), *Ivanov*, *The Wood Demon* and its revision *Uncle Vanya*, *The Seagull*, *Three Sisters*, *The Cherry Orchard*, nine one-act plays, as well as Chekhov's letters, Stanislavsky's and Nemirovich-Danchenko's rehearsal notes, and critical writings by Jiao Juyin and Tong Daoming.

Core credo (Chekhov's own words): **"Start powerfully, end weakly. Defy all laws of drama. Write like a novel." "Neither an angel nor a devil."** The conflict is not between human and human, but between a group of people and the environment that surrounds them.

For act-by-act structure tables and source-text examples of each play, see [reference.md](reference.md) in the same directory.

## 0. Medium Boundary: Which Layers Travel and Which Do Not

This is a method for the **stage**. When it is invoked on a film or series project, take it layer by layer:

| Layer | Does it travel to film / series? |
|---|---|
| **Structure** (no central climax, even-numbered act division, the event off-stage, the announced event that never happens, the correct answer stated in Act 1 and refused, the three-layer ending) | **Yes.** But "act" here is a theatrical unit; on a feature it becomes a sequence, on a series an in-episode movement — **do not treat "four acts" as four acts of a film or four acts of an episode** |
| **Character** (neither angels nor devils, a group of people against their environment, a character = a composite of two or three real people) | **Yes**, directly usable |
| **Dialogue** (pauses, answering beside the question, the thematic line interrupted by the mundane, the same speech spoken once at the start and once at the end) | **Yes**, directly usable |
| **Stage directions and sound discipline** (one sourced set of ambient sounds per act, "—Curtain", "[Pause." on its own line) | **The notation does not travel.** The use of ambient sound does; the way it is written down is theatrical. For film and series format see `sw-format-adaptation` |
| **The nine one-act methods** | Valid only for stage one-acts and short films; not applicable to features or series |

**In one line**: take the patterns, not the units, not the notation.

## I. Comparison and Evolutionary Arc of the Seven Multi-Act Plays

| Play | Year | Self-designated Genre | Time Span | Central Event (Off-stage / On-stage) | Act Ending Mode | Final Line |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fatherlessness | ~1880 | Untitled early work | One summer | Sofya shoots Platonov dead (on-stage) | Incident | "Give him three rubles!" "Life is worth one kopeck!" |
| Ivanov | 1887 | Drama | 1 yr between Acts 3 and 4 | Ivanov commits suicide at wedding (on-stage) | Caught / Truth-telling / Gunshot | "My old self has revived! (Draws revolver)" |
| The Wood Demon | 1889 | Comedy | One summer + 2 weeks | Voynitsky commits suicide (off-stage); happy ending | Courtship / Forbidden to play / Gunshot / Applause | "This is magnificent! This is magnificent!" |
| The Seagull | 1896 | Comedy | 2 yrs between Acts 3 and 4 | Treplev commits suicide (off-stage, masked by "ether bottle burst") | Confession / Monologue / Kiss / Lie | "Find some way to get Irina away. Konstantin has just shot himself..." |
| Uncle Vanya | 1896 | Scenes from country life | Late summer -> Autumn | Vanya misses two shots (on-stage); everything resets | Courtship / Refusal / Gunshot / Accounts | "Ah, to rest!" |
| Three Sisters | 1900 | Drama | 3.5 yrs (~1 yr between acts) | Baron dies in duel (distant gunshot off-stage) | Proposal walked in on / To Moscow / Marry him / Military music | "If only we could know!" "It's all the same." |
| The Cherry Orchard | 1903 | Comedy | May -> October | Cherry orchard auctioned (off-stage, buyer reports it himself) | Spring / Moon / Sold / Axe | "You good-for-nothing!" + Breaking string + Axe strokes |

**Evolutionary Arc** (follow late-period practice when writing):
- Events move from on-stage to off-stage (gunshot -> distant shot -> sound of axe).
- Act endings change from explosive peaks to pauses ("arrow on the bowstring" -> "forbidden to play" -> "he refuses" -> "What? Well, let us go then").
- Protagonist shifts from an individual to an ensemble.
- Genre labeling insists more and more on "comedy."

Chekhov assessed *Ivanov* as "the plan was on the right track, it was just written terribly"—every act ended with an incident (discovery, truth-telling, gunshot); from *The Seagull* onward, he switched to weak endings.

## II. Ten Reusable Structural Patterns

### S1 Four Acts, Even Numbers, No Central Climax
- Traditional European act division favored odd numbers (three or five acts) so the climax could sit in the middle; all of Chekhov's multi-act plays have four acts, aiming to "render the dramatic events on stage mundane and true to life" (Tong Daoming).
- Jiao Juyin: Chekhov **does not divide acts starting from the story, but first searches for an appropriate mood**—Act 1: vexing spring, morning light, family reunion, longing for ideals; Act 2: indolence, idle chatter, sentimentality, semi-hysteria, turbulence and contradiction; Act 3: desolate night, each harboring their own melancholy, selfishness, the impossibility of souls communicating; Act 4: collapse, despair, parting.
- **Instruction: First determine a mood keyword for each act, then decide what events to place inside it. Put the events inside the mood; do not paste the mood onto the events.**

### S2 Major Events Skipped Between Acts
- Two years pass between Acts 3 and 4 of *The Seagull* (Nina's ruin happens off-stage); one year in *Ivanov* (Anna's death); one year between each act of *Three Sisters* (marriage, childbirth, mortgage).
- Act openings use casual chatter for exposition: "The professor intends to stay until doomsday," "He hasn't paid rent for eight months," "Father died exactly one year ago today."
- **Instruction: The biggest changes occur between acts; the first line of an act establishes the time scale (anniversary, date, name day).**

### S3 Central Event Kept Off-Stage
- Gunshots sound backstage (*The Seagull*, *Three Sisters*, *The Wood Demon*); the auction takes place in town; the duel happens in the forest; news of death is whispered or announced by the most indifferent person (Dorn, Chebutykin).
- On-stage gunshots must miss (Vanya: "Bang! Missed? Missed again?!").
- *The Seagull*: Gunshot -> "Nothing. Something must have exploded in my medicine chest" -> Doctor goes out and returns -> Hums softly -> Flips magazine and mentions a letter from America -> (Low voice) "Find some way to get Irina away. Konstantin has just shot himself..."—**The most important event is masked by a character's lie, and the truth is passed in a whisper to another character in the final line; the audience hears it, but the mother does not.**
- *Three Sisters*: The duel is first evaded by everyone ("What happened yesterday near the theater?" "Nothing of consequence") -> when it happens, it is merely "a faint, distant gunshot," to which no one reacts, while Masha continues reciting poetry -> finally, the doctor whispers the announcement: "The Baron was just killed in a duel."
- **Instruction: Death is first avoided by everyone, registers only as a background gunshot when it happens, and is finally announced verbally by the most indifferent person. If violence occurs on-stage, it carries no consequences, and the subsequent scene is about tidying away the weapon (hiding the revolver, begging for morphine).**

### S4 Symmetrical Framing / Identical Dialogue Segments
- The nursery in *The Cherry Orchard* is filled in Act 1 (windows closed, blossoms open, crowd enters) and emptied in Act 4 (curtains and picture frames removed, furniture piled in corners, crowd exits)—the reduction of objects replaces exposition.
- The nurse's offers in *Uncle Vanya* ("Have some tea," "Or a drop of vodka") occur once at the beginning and once at the end; the only difference is that the protagonist accepts the drink the second time.
- The play-within-a-play monologue in *The Seagull* ("Men, lions, eagles, and partridges") is recited at both the beginning and the end: in Act 1, when Nina recites it, the audience whispers about the smell of sulfur; in Act 4, recited after living through ruin, Treplev falls silent—"a tragedy that can only be understood once tragedy has been tasted" (Jiao Juyin).
- **Instruction: Have the exact same passage of text recited at the beginning and end, separated by a character's entire downfall; or have the same room filled -> emptied.**

### S5 The Correct Answer Is Stated in Act 1 and Refused
- Lopakhin proposes in Act 1: "Parcel it out for summer cottages, twenty-five thousand"; in Act 2: "Just one word: yes or no"; in Act 3 he buys it; in Act 4: "Ideal weather for building." Lyubov's answers shift from "You understand nothing at all" to "How utterly vulgar" to silence.
- Lebedev: "The ceiling is white, boots are black, sugar is sweet." Sonya: "There is as much work as one could wish for."
- **Instruction: Place the correct answer on stage early and repeat it in every act; the drama lies in why the characters cannot hear it.**

### S6 Ensemble Scenes Backed by Mechanical Tempos
- Calling numbers during card games (*The Seagull*: "Twenty-two!" "Three!" "Eighty-one!" / *Ivanov*: "Two of spades," "Pass"), abacus clicks (*Uncle Vanya*: "Fifteen... twenty-five" / *The Anniversary*: "One... zero... zero... three"), prayers and choral chanting (*Tatyana Repina*), nautical commands (*The Wedding*: "Haul the mainsheet, furl the topgallant!"), quadrille calls (*The Cherry Orchard*), billiard terms (Gaev).
- **Instruction: Use mechanical counting or calling as a metronome for final scenes or ensemble sequences; major events (gunshots, entrances/exits, news of death) fall between two numbers.**

### S7 The Announced Event Does Not Happen at the End
- Everyone in the play talks about Lopakhin and Varya's impending match; left alone in Act 4, they discuss only luggage, the thermometer, and the weather: "I have no thermometer." "And the thermometer is broken anyway..." A shout from the yard: "Coming right away!" ("As if she had long been waiting for just such a call"). Lyubov enters: "Well?" [Pause.] "Well, let us go then!"
- Ivanov does not marry; in *On the High Road*, the axe is raised but never falls: "Did I kill her, or did I not?"; in *Uncle Vanya*, everything returns to the status quo.
- **Instruction: Have an event anticipated throughout the play fail to occur at the end, resolved with a "Well?" and a pause. The ending of a tragedy-mode one-act is "it did not happen."**

### S8 Three-Layer Ending: Hope Monologue + Nihilist Line + Household Action
- Sonya's "We shall rest" + night watchman tapping his board, Telegin strumming his guitar, grandmother writing margin notes, nurse knitting wool.
- Three Sisters' "We must live," "If only we could know" + Chebutykin reading the paper: "It's all the same, all the same" + Kulygin picking up his hat, Andrey pushing the pram, military march fading in the distance.
- Anya's "Hello, new life" + Firs's "You good-for-nothing" (his habitual catchphrase for others, now directed at himself) + string snapping + axe strokes.
- **Instruction: Juxtapose three layers at the end—a young person's or believer's hope monologue, a nihilist's dismissive line, and mundane household chores in the stage directions; save the very last line for the least important character, whom everyone assumed had already been taken care of.**

### S9 Ball / Banquet / Birthday Juxtaposed Against Disaster
- A ball on the night of the auction (dancing, conjuring tricks, recitations, borrowing money, punctuated by "The auction surely did not take place," "A passerby said it was sold," and finally brought back by the buyer himself: "Sold." "Who bought it?" "I did."); a wedding turns into an ideological dispute (*Ivanov*); thirteen seated at a name-day table; birthday breakfast announced with "They have forgotten us."
- **Instruction: Stage celebratory scenes at the exact hour disaster strikes; let disaster filter in as rumors before being confirmed by an eyewitness; have the victor articulate the loser's lamentation (Lopakhin's monologue is simultaneously ecstatic, humiliated, reproving, and exhausted, knocking over a candelabra: "I can pay for everything").**

### S10 An All-Night Lantern Slide
- A single night on the railway embankment in *Fatherlessness*: Osip -> Sasha -> Platonov -> Vengerovich Jr. -> Anna (horse hooves) -> Triletsky climbing through window -> Drunkard -> Sasha looking out window -> Osip spying -> Letter delivery -> Voynitsev drunk -> Gunfire -> Sasha lying on rails -> Train.
- Thunderstorm night in *Uncle Vanya*: every pair of characters attempts intimacy once, and every attempt fails; fire night in *Three Sisters* = confession night for the entire household, where everyone speaks the truth between screens and firelight.
- **Instruction: Choose one fixed location and an entire night; have characters pass through in succession, each leaving behind a decision or a confession.**

## III. Ten Reusable Character Patterns

### C1 Neither Angel Nor Devil; Conflict Is Between Humans and Environment
- No villains. Natasha's triumph is charted room by room (green sash corrected -> checking candles for fire hazards -> "Irina's room is just right for the baby" -> "I will not permit you to sit in my presence" -> "Chop down the fir trees," "Why is this fork lying here?"), without a single villainous line.
- The professor's sin is being a "famous nobody"; Lopakhin weeps as he buys the estate.
- **Instruction: Convey a villain's conquest entirely through commands over spaces and physical objects.**

### C2 Supporting Cast Offers Simple Explanations; the Protagonist "Does Not Know"
- *Ivanov*: Lvov = Tartuffe/scoundrel; Zyuzjushka = married a Jewish woman for money; Kosykh = plays cards like a cobbler; Sasha = a second Hamlet. Ivanov himself: "I do not understand you, you do not understand me, and we do not understand ourselves."
- Platonov is defined as "the finest embodiment of modern uncertainty... the hero of an unwritten modern novel"; Chebutykin: "That I don't remember."
- **Instruction: Let every supporting character hold a reductive explanation for the protagonist, while the protagonist's own explanation is "I don't know." The protagonist's action is merely responding to callers—send everyone to his doorstep.**

### C3 The Righteous Man as Persecutor
- Lvov declares his honesty every time he enters, only to be mocked from three different angles ("Make way for the honest man," "Speaking of one's own virtue to women is bad manners," "No matter how violently you act, you always believe yourself pure and noble"); his monologues follow a fixed pattern: missed chance to speak -> "My wretched weakness."
- **Instruction: Write a morally irreproachable yet repellent character; derive disgust from the fact that "he is never wrong."**

### C4 Every Character Gets a Portable Object + an Eternal Catchphrase
- Kosykh's cards ("Ace of diamonds, king, queen..." x4), Solyony's perfume bottle, Chebutykin's newspaper, Gaev's hard candies and billiard slang, Pishchik's "Fancy that, just look at this!", Telegin's "This is magnificent!", Chubukov's "and so on and so forth" (thirty-odd times), Shipuchin's "as sure as my name is Shipuchin," Yepikhodov's "You see how it is!", Firs's "good-for-nothing," Sorin's "and all that sort of thing, and that's all," Ferapont's "What?".
- Catchphrases are turned against oneself at the end (Firs) or recited incorrectly (Masha's oak tree: "A cat, green leaves... I've got it mixed up"); portable objects shift meaning across the four acts (camera burned, beard shaved and replaced with a false one, mother's clock smashed).
- **Instruction: Assign each character a portable object and a situation-independent catchphrase to use at moments of heaviest emotion; invert or misremember the catchphrase at the end.**

### C5 The Lucid Character Equates Themselves with the Protagonist's Failure
- Astrov: "In this whole district there were once only two decent, cultivated people: you and me. But in the space of ten short years, we have been dragged down day by day into this damned vulgar life." "Like your Uncle Vanya, I too am dissatisfied with life."
- Dorn and Chebutykin operate the same way. The doctor's diagnosis of himself: "When I am drunk, I take on the most difficult operations... and convince myself that I am indeed a great benefactor to humanity!"
- **Instruction: Have the most lucid character (often a doctor) align themselves with the protagonist as sharing the same failure, and treat the newcomer's influence as a contagious disease ("Laziness and idleness are infectious").**

### C6 Marginal Figures Articulate the Theme
- Kuzma the churchwarden: "This afternoon we buried a gentleman, just now we had a wedding, and tomorrow morning we have a christening. On and on it goes, never an end." The prompter, Nurse Marina ("Let the turkeys go on gobbling and squabbling"), deaf Ferapont, Firs.
- **Instruction: Let the theme be voiced by sweepers, the deaf, or the forgotten. Force the protagonist to speak the truth only to someone who cannot hear (Andrey to the deaf man: "If you could hear properly, perhaps I wouldn't be speaking to you"), while the listener returns absurd rumors ("A merchant in Moscow ate pancakes until he died," "In Petersburg it was two hundred degrees below zero").**

### C7 Figures of Unknown Origin Handle the Performance
- Charlotta: "I have no passport, I do not know my real age... Who am I?" crunches a cucumber; performs conjuring, ventriloquism, holds a bundle like an infant: "Find me another post, won't you." Shamraev specializes in telling irrelevant old jokes at high-tension moments (before departure: "The horses are harnessed... Once upon a time... 'We've fallen into a trap!'"). Sorin's "L'homme qui a voulu."
- **Instruction: Include a figure of mysterious origins in the ensemble who performs rather than confides; include a character dedicated to telling ill-timed jokes.**

### C8 One-Way Love Chains
- Medvedenko -> Masha -> Treplev -> Nina -> Trigorin <-> Arkadina; Sonya -> Astrov -> Yelena <- Vanya; Solyony -> Irina <- Tuzenbach. Not a single pair is mutual.
- *Fatherlessness*: The same desire is summoned through four different media (Sasha's food / Anna's horse / Sofya's telegraphic letter / Grekova's court summons).
- **Instruction: Map out love chains and ensure every arrow is unidirectional; the pursued party's denial functions as an admission ("So you don't detest me after all, you were merely frightened," and in the next scene she writes: "I am resurrected").**

### C9 Characters as Real-Life Hybrids + Actor Feedback
- Trigorin = Potapenko + Chekhov; Charlotta = an English governess next door; Yepikhodov "turned entirely into what Moskvin made of him"; Firs grew out of Artyom's pantomime of fishing.
- *The Cherry Orchard* started from an actor's physical gesture (fishing, slapping water in a bathhouse) and gradually grew into a house, characters, and relationships; in the end, the atmosphere and catchphrases were kept (windows and blossoming branches, the old servant, the borrowing mistress, the billiard enthusiast, "put on the wrong trousers again"), while the gimmicks (one arm, fishing, the bathhouse) were discarded.
- **Instruction: Build characters by blending two or three real people; allow an actor's improvisation to flow back into the text; begin conception from a single physical movement.**

### C10 A Character's Decency Is Part of the Theme
- Chekhov objected to provincial productions dressing Vanya in greasy boots like a bankrupt squire: "Squires dress far better than you or I," "wearing an exceedingly handsome necktie"—"The point is not the necktie, but the main idea of the play: that naturally gifted Astrov and elegant, cultivated Uncle Vanya decay in this desolate backwater, while that idiot of a professor enjoys life in Petersburg."
- **Instruction: Do not depict bankruptcy as slovenliness; give the idealist a pair of worn galoshes (Trofimov, the "balding gentleman," tumbling down the stairs).**

## IV. Twelve Reusable Dialogue Patterns

### D1 First Line States the Theme, Promptly Crushed by Trivia
- "Why do you always wear black?" "I am in mourning for my life. I am unhappy." -> "My salary is only twenty-three rubles a month." "Father died exactly one year ago today" -> Clock strikes twelve. "How long have we known each other?" "Eleven years."
- **Instruction: Make the first line of dialogue the character's summary judgment on their entire existence, then immediately crush it beneath money, clocks, or calendar years.**

### D2 Answering Off-Topic, Half a Step Askew
- Jiao Juyin: "Person A asks about Person A's concerns, while Person B answers about Person B's... Any dialogue determined by logical form is not realist drama." While everyone discusses the pain of parting, Charlotta says: "My little dog eats walnuts"; during a grave conversation, Gaev murmurs: "Red ball into the middle pocket!"
- Latching onto only a single word: Dorn discusses the crowds of Genoa and the world soul -> "Where is Zarechnaya?"; Fedya speaks of owls and when asked "What on earth are you talking about?" replies only: "About owls."
- **Instruction: Keep responses half a step off—catch a single word from the preceding line, never its meaning.**

### D3 Philosophy Cut by Physical Need or Newspaper Snippets
- Vershinin's long speech on "two or three hundred years from now" -> "I am thirsty. I should like some tea"; Chebutykin interjects: "Balzac was married in Berdichev"; forest monologue -> vodka: "In short... (Drinks) I must go. Of course, none of this really matters very much."
- **Instruction: Follow every philosophical outburst with a physical craving or a newspaper item; have a character's conviction first relayed by an admirer, then half-spoken by the character, cut short by a physical prop, and immediately self-deprecatingly dismissed.**

### D4 The Deepest Pain in the Shortest or Most Irrelevant Words
- "When did he say that?" (Anna upon hearing "You are going to die"); "I have no thermometer"; "About owls"; "A wife is just a wife" (two pages of monologue trimmed to nine characters); "A mistress is just a mistress!"
- Jiao Juyin: "The vast majority of people, when expressing their most excruciating, agonizing thoughts, tend to use the fewest words, or even utter something utterly irrelevant." Chekhov to an actor: "The broader masses... when the agony is too great, merely whistle."
- **Instruction: Have the wounded party react with a technical question; have someone whistle during a tragic scene.**

### D5 Long Monologues Limited to Professional Obsessions or a Past Woman
- Trigorin's monologue is entirely the self-portrait of a writing machine (clouds shaped like grand pianos, classifying herbs under "flowers admired by widows," "not as good as Turgenev"); love appears only at the tail end as "jotting down notes"; Svetlovidov: "Throw the stage away!"; Astrov's three maps; Vanya: "Ten years ago she was seventeen and I was thirty-seven."
- **Instruction: Confine long monologues to professional obsessions, never romantic declarations; turn the theme into a visible prop (maps/charts), have the character lecture on it, and make the listener completely indifferent ("I can see from your face that you have no interest in this").**

### D6 The Confessor's Listener Refuses to Listen
- Masha: "I love Vershinin" -> Olga steps behind the screen: "Whatever you say, I will not listen," and Masha finishes speaking anyway; Lyubov covers her ears; Vanya: "Leave me in peace"; Anna: "Pray for me."
- **Instruction: Have the listener hide or refuse to hear the confession, while the confession proceeds to completion regardless.**

### D7 Talk About the Weather When Awkwardness Peaks
- Vanya enters carrying roses and catches the doctor kissing Yelena; the doctor: "The weather today is truly delightful. It was a bit overcast this morning... To tell the truth, this autumn has been extraordinary... only the days are growing shorter... (Exits)"; Lopakhin: "Three degrees below zero"; the Tramp: "Delightful weather today"; "Ideal weather for hanging oneself."
- **Instruction: Discuss the weather after an awkward discovery; let the final observation about the weather (the days growing shorter) conceal the true subtext; have the discoverer holding a gift intended for the discovered.**

### D8 Arguments Start with Gentle Gestures, Escalate to Labels, End in Shared Tears
- *The Seagull* mother and son: changing the bandage (childhood memories) -> "You don't understand him" -> "Envy!" -> "Decadent!" "Miser!" "Ragpicker!" "Nonentity!" -> He weeps silently -> She weeps too -> "Forgive your wretched mother."
- *The Bear*: "Bear! Brute! Bourbon!" -> Duel -> "You must first show me how to fire" -> Kiss.
- **Instruction: Begin an argument with the tenderest physical action, escalate to trading social labels, and collapse into weeping or kissing together; seal reconciliation with a shared object (drinking from one glass), followed by each repeating an opposing line ("How happy I am!" "How unhappy I am.").**

### D9 Literary Quotes Substituted for Declarations
- Shakespearean fragments (*Swan Song* uses *King Lear*, *Hamlet*, and *Othello* to prove his genius, followed by "At best I might play a follower to Fortinbras"), Pushkin's oak tree (Masha), Lermontov (Solyony), *Hamlet* ("Hamlet feared dreams; I fear life"; Lopakhin's "Okhmelia, get thee to a nunnery"), Latin tags (Kulygin's ut consecutivum, Professor's manet omnes una nox).
- **Instruction: Let characters label each other with canonical names, which the recipient rejects; use citations in place of direct declarations, affirming oneself only to immediately retract the claim.**

### D10 A Chilling Aside Following an Ardent Declaration
- Arkadina falls to her knees clutching Trigorin's knees: "You are Russia's only hope" -> He surrenders: "I have no will of my own" -> She (aside): "Now he is mine" -> Composed: "Of course, stay if you wish."
- Yasha: "Pretending he has just stepped out of a swim in the river."
- **Instruction: Deliver a cold aside immediately following an impassioned declaration, exposing the performance.**

### D11 Final Parting Words Are Mundane Requests
- Tuzenbach departing for the duel: "Say something to me." "What shall I say?" "Anything at all" -> Talks of trees: "That tree is dead, yet it sways in the wind with the rest" -> "I haven't had my coffee today. Tell them to brew me some."
- Platonov before dying: "Give him three rubles!" (paying Marko for tea); Popova: "Tell them Toby gets no oats today" (the opening/closing cipher marking a change in heart); "Fetch my coat."
- **Instruction: At parting, have one character beg the other to speak, while the other can find nothing to say; make the departing character's final line a trivial domestic request or a small debt; leave the protagonist's debt to the most peripheral character to be settled in the final breath.**

### D12 Whistling, Pauses, and Swallowing Half-Sentences
- "(Whistles)" is Masha's language; "[Pause." is the most frequent stage direction ("The closer one gets to life, the more one realizes that in real life, the deepest and most powerful element is the pause"—Stanislavsky); "If she only knew... never mind... I'll tell you about it later"; Sonya at the doorway withdraws her consent: "No, it is better left in uncertainty..." "What did you say?" "Nothing, nothing at all. (Exits)."
- Lovers' whispers using nonsensical syllables: "Tra-ta-ta!" "Ta-ta!" "Tra-ta-ta?" "Tra-ta-ta!"
- **Instruction: Have a character retract their agreement at the doorway before exiting; the other party fails to catch it, and fate runs its course; have a betrayed husband repeat self-affirmations to show he knows (Kulygin: "I am satisfied, I am satisfied, I am satisfied").**

## V. Stage Directions and Sound Discipline

- Write only what can be seen and heard; do not describe psychology. Jiao Juyin: "If we delete a word or two from Chekhov's stage directions, several of his characters will drop dead."
- **One set of source-grounded environmental sounds per act**: night watchman's knocker, owl, accordion, cello, guitar, waltz, Jewish band, fire alarm, military band, snapping string, door lock, carriage bells, axe. The final act preserves only two (the lock, the axe).
- An inexplicable sound occurs twice: the first time everyone offers an explanation ("A bucket broke in a distant mineshaft," "A heron," "An owl," "It sounded just like that before the great disaster"), the second time no one is present.
- Measure time via clocks and train schedules: Lopakhin checks his watch more than five times ("The 4:30 train," "August 22nd," "Only forty-six minutes until departure"); "Your clock is seven minutes fast"; the doctor's chiming watch: "The first, second, and fifth batteries depart precisely at one."
- Physical props as themes: three maps (fifty years ago / twenty-five years ago / today—"a chart of degeneration"), map of Africa (stage directions note "evidently of no earthly use here," discussed at the darkest moment: "I imagine it must be frightfully hot in Africa just now"), stuffed seagull ("I don't remember!"), century-old bookcase (Gaev delivers an address to furniture that ought to have been spoken to people), smashed clock, mother's photograph, gold medallion, ring of keys (Varya hurls them to the floor).
- The anti-sound effect: "I must write another play that begins like this: 'How lovely, how silent! Not a bird singing, not a dog barking, not a cuckoo...'"
- Format (following Jiao's translation style): Character Name + space + dialogue; actions in parentheses; freestanding directions begin with "["; pauses on a line of their own "[Pause."; act ends with "—Curtain".

## VI. One-Act Dramaturgy (Nine Modes)

| Mode | Single Action | Ending | Technique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tragedy: *On the High Road* | Ruined landowner begs for vodka in roadside inn on rainy night | Vagrant raises axe but does not strike: "Did I kill her, or did I not?" | Descending value of pawned items (cap "full of holes like a sieve" -> coat "naked flesh" -> gold medallion "don't touch that face with your fingers"); third party (coachman) narrates protagonist's past; coincidence (carriage spring breaks, wife enters inn) |
| Monologue: *On the Harmful Effects of Tobacco* | Henpecked husband forced to lecture | Wife appears in wings; he resumes lecture tone | Public assignment constantly derailed by private life (number 13, seven daughters, boarding school advertisement, pamphlets for sixty kopecks); 1886 -> 1902: identical line "Thirty-three years were the best of my life" shifts from truth to hypocrisy; stomping on his tailcoat, then putting it back on |
| Tragedy: *Swan Song* | Aging actor awakens late at night in empty theater | Recites Shakespeare with prompter: "I am no genius... let us go" | Empty theater = tomb; "I have no family" x3; literary quotes replace monologues, proved then denied; two-hander |
| Farce: *The Bear* | Debt collecting -> Duel -> Marriage proposal | "Tell them Toby gets no oats today" | Three-part structure; chair cracked twice; misogyny/misandry mirror each other with matching statistics; servants burst in with axes and pitchforks to find them kissing |
| Farce: *The Proposal* | Proposal -> Fight over meadows -> Fight over dogs | "Yes!" "No!" "Champagne!" | Proposal derailed three times; escalating physical symptoms (palpitations, numb left foot, twitching eyebrow, seeing stars) serve as tempo; catchphrase counts; subject of fight has nothing to do with objective |
| Tragedy: *Tatyana Repina* | Entire church wedding service | Woman in black swallows poison: "They must all take poison!" | Public ceremony staged in full; private collapse occurs only in the cracks; sound layering of litany / choir / crowd / groomsmen / "a groan" x7; theme voiced by churchwarden; outsider takes poison |
| Farce: *A Reluctant Tragic Hero* | Suburban husband borrows a revolver | "I want blood! Blood!" | Shopping lists serve as dialogue (lampshade, pound of ham, castor oil, twenty bottles of beer, size 82 corset); sympathizer immediately piles on chores (asks to carry sewing machine and canary cage) |
| Farce: *The Wedding* | Hired "general" lectures on seamanship at wedding banquet | Best man's "On such a grand occasion..." cut short three times | Ensemble = each character speaks an isolated vocabulary (naval commands / telegraph taps / "Greece has everything"); hired dignified man discovers the truth: "Where is the door?" |
| Farce: *The Anniversary* | Bank director rushes annual report while besieged by wife and old woman | Deputation reads eulogy "doth exist" while two people groan | Three disconnected plotlines stacked in one room; mistaken identity (clerk mistakes wife for old woman and throws her out); two people faint simultaneously; eulogy juxtaposed with catastrophe |

Cross-cutting principles: single space in real time; one unifying object (medallion, oats, meadows/dogs, bridal crowns, sewing machine, abacus); physical symptoms as tempo; catchphrase counts; farce three-beat structure (assignment -> off-topic brawl -> physical collapse -> unexpected reconciliation or outburst); tragedy ending is "it did not happen"; ensemble scenes use ritual/counting as skeleton; marginal characters voice the theme.

## VII. "Fixing the Flawed": *The Wood Demon* -> *Uncle Vanya*

| Modification | The Wood Demon (1889) | Uncle Vanya (1896) | Principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Characters | Zheltukhin siblings, Orlovsky father and son | Cut | Remove characters serving only matchmaking functions |
| Central Event | Voynitsky commits suicide at end of Act 3 | Two shots miss: "Bang! Missed?" | Violence without consequences |
| Resolution | Three couples engaged, one reunited; characters hide, watch, and applaud: "Bravo! Bravo!" | Everything resets; Vanya returns to ledger: "February 2nd, oil, twenty pounds..." | Never dissolve the tragedy of humans vs. environment easily |
| Forest Monologue | Recited in full up to "helping God create the world" | Sonya relays it first; doctor gets halfway through, cut by vodka, dismisses it: "None of this matters" | Belief interrupted |
| Professor | Complaining, selling estate | Add: "He has not left behind a single page of writing, he is a nonentity! A soap bubble!" | Intensify "youth squandered on an idol" |
| Doctor | In love with Sonya, successfully courted at the end | Knows Sonya's feelings but does not respond: "I must make her understand I cannot love her" | One-way love chains |
| Act Ending | "Forbidden to play!" | "He refuses." | Petty refused desires relayed by a third party—retained verbatim |

Other revision cases:
- *The Seagull* Act 1 originally ended with a shock reveal (Masha and Dorn alone, revealing Masha is the doctor's daughter, never mentioned again in subsequent acts). Chekhov argued "the audience likes to see an arrow on the bowstring at the end of each act," to which Nemirovich-Danchenko replied: "Quite true, but such endings belong at the very end, not in the middle!" Revised to Masha confiding: "I love Konstantin." **Suspense at an act break must pay off later; explosive twists with no follow-up must be cut.**
- *Three Sisters*: Andrey's two-page monologue to Ferapont was trimmed to nine characters: "A wife is just a wife!"
- A director suggested cutting an entire scene at the end of Act 2 of *The Cherry Orchard*: "His face went pale, but he considered it for a moment... 'Cut it then!'"
- Phonetic title shift: not a commercial *vishnevyi sad* ("cherry orchard"), but the lyric, self-contained *vishnyovyi sad* ("cherry [blossom] orchard")—a single vowel shift in the title transforms its entire meaning.

## IX. Workflow: Writing a Chekhovian Script

1. **Fix the theme (the pulse of the era) first, cultivate characters second, divide acts last** (Jiao Juyin: Chekhov collected material in thick notebooks for each character, dividing acts only once there was enough material to build a complete persona).
2. **Assign an emotional keyword to each of the four acts** (reunion/longing -> indolence/turbulence -> desolate night/private grief -> collapse/departure), fixing the season, time of day, and environmental soundscape for each.
3. **Determine the central event and move it off-stage**; establish who masks it with a lie and who delivers the final whispered announcement.
4. **State the correct answer in Act 1** and have the characters reject it; repeat it once every act.
5. **Chart a unidirectional love chain**; assign each character a portable object, a catchphrase, and a real-life prototype.
6. **Include a righteous persecutor, a lucid doctor, a performer of unknown origin, an ill-timed joke-teller, and a deaf person or servant who voices the theme.**
7. **End each act with a pause, a refusal ("forbidden to play," "he refuses"), or a monologue**, never an incident; if using an "arrow on the bowstring," it must be picked up in later acts.
8. **Write an all-night lantern slide** (thunderstorm night / fire night / night on the rails) as Act 2 or Act 3.
9. **Juxtapose celebration against catastrophe**: ball / wedding / name-day set against auction / news of death at the same hour.
10. **Announce an event and ensure it does not occur at the end** (proposal, wedding, falling axe).
11. **Three-layer ending**: hope monologue + nihilist line + domestic chore; grant the final line to the forgotten character; turn the catchphrase back on its speaker.
12. **Review all dialogue**: first lines state themes and get buried in trivia; responses shift half a step askew; philosophy is cut short by tea or newspapers; deepest pain takes the fewest words; confessors' listeners refuse to listen; discuss the weather after being caught; arguments escalate from tender gestures to social labels to shared tears; final farewell is a mundane request.
13. **Cut**: turn two-page monologues into nine characters; cut entire scenes; allow a single vowel in the title to alter the work's entire meaning.
14. **Insist that it is a comedy.**

## X. "Chekhov-ization" Checklist

1. Is the act division even-numbered, and does each act have a distinct mood keyword?
2. Does the largest event occur off-stage? Does any on-stage gunshot miss?
3. Does each act break on a pause / refusal / monologue rather than an explosive plot twist?
4. Does every character possess a portable object and a catchphrase? Is the catchphrase inverted at the end?
5. Is there a "righteous persecutor," or at least an absence of villains?
6. Is the correct answer spoken in Act 1 and refused?
7. Is the dialogue half a step askew? Is philosophy cut short by tea / vodka / newspaper items?
8. Are the most painful lines the briefest? Does someone whistle?
9. Is every act's environmental sound realistically sourced? Are only two sounds left in the final act?
10. Does an event anticipated throughout the play fail to occur at the end?
11. Does the ending follow "hope monologue + nihilist line + household action"?
12. Do you insist that it is a comedy?

# Story Structure (故事结构)

This skill merges six structural systems: Syd Field, *Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting* (Paradigm / Plot Points); Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (BS2 Save the Cat 15 Beats, The Board); Robert McKee, *Story* (structural hierarchy, story triangle, Inciting Incident, Progressive Complications, crisis-climax-resolution); Julian Hoxter, *The Creative Screenwriter: 12 Rules to Follow and Break to Unlock Your Screenwriting Potential* (nine beats, A/B stories); Neill D. Hicks, *Screenwriting 101: The Essential Craft of Feature Film Writing* (three acts = attraction / anticipation / satisfaction, ten elements); Lu Jun, *Screenwriting Theory and Technique: Starting from Short-Form Drama (Chinese original, no English edition)* (Introduction, Elaboration, Turn, Resolution; eight opening methods, eight ending methods, climax flaws, eight pitfalls of transformation, six types of change). They are mutually compatible: **Field provides the skeleton, Snyder provides the page counts, McKee provides the principles, Hoxter provides the beat names, Hicks provides audience psychology, and Lu Jun provides practical craft techniques from Chinese drama.**

For comparison tables, case studies, and film-by-film plot points, see [reference.md](reference.md) in the same directory.

## I. The Hierarchy of Structure (McKee)

- **Structure** = a selection of events from the characters' life stories organized into a strategic sequence to arouse specific emotions and to express a specific view of life.
- **Event** = change, which must be expressed through **value** (life/death, love/hate, freedom/slavery, truth/lie... binaries that can turn positive or negative at any time). Rain falling in East Africa is a coincidence, not a story event; rain wrestled out of conflict by a rainmaker is.
- Hierarchy: **Beat** (an exchange of action/reaction) -> **Scene** (an action through conflict in more or less continuous time and space that turns the value-charged condition of a character's life on at least one level; subtle change) -> **Sequence** (2–5 scenes with escalating impact culminating in a peak; moderate change; give each sequence a title) -> **Act** (a series of sequences peaking in a climactic scene of major reversal) -> **Story** (a series of acts, where the final act climax = story climax, producing change that is **absolute and irreversible**; changes across scenes/sequences/acts can be reversible, like E.T. dying and reviving in Act Two).
- **No scene without a turn**: note the value polarity (+ or -) at the opening, note it again at the ending; if they are the same, ask "why is this scene here?"—it is almost always for exposition -> cut it and weave the information elsewhere.
- Plot = "navigating through dangerous terrain"; all stories have plot.

### The Story Triangle (Deciding Which Structure to Write)
- Top: **Archplot** (classical design): active protagonist, external conflict, continuous time, causal coherence, closed ending—"a mirror of the human mind," drawing the widest audience.
- Bottom-left: **Miniplot** (minimalism): open ending, internal conflict, passive protagonist, multiple protagonists (*The Accidental Tourist*, *Parenthood*, *Pelle the Conqueror*).
- Bottom-right: **Antiplot**: non-linear time, coincidence, inconsistent realities (*After Hours*, *Weekend*; Roger Rabbit turning two-dimensional once establishes a rule).
- Below the base line: **Nonplot** (stasis).
- Commandments: a writer must write to live, must master classical form first (Bergman, Fellini, Godard, Altman all mastered the Archplot first), and must believe in what they write—"to be different for the sake of being different is as empty an effort as to blindly follow commercial rules."

## II. The Three-Act Paradigm and Plot Points (Field)

```
Act I: Setup           Act II: Confrontation           Act III: Resolution
Pages 1–30             Pages 30–90                     Pages 90–120
           Plot Point I (pp. 25–27)        Plot Point II (pp. 85–90)
```
- One page = one minute. **Plot Point** = "an incident, or event, that hooks into the action and spins it around into another direction"; a completed screenplay may have fifteen plot points, but the two at act endings are the "anchors."
- **Before writing, you must know four things: ending, beginning, Plot Point I, and Plot Point II.** Know the ending first: "You don't have to know the specific details, but you have to know what happens."
- Each of the three acts also has a beginning, middle, and end; each act possesses a "direction."
- **The first ten pages must establish three things**: who the main character is; the dramatic premise (what the story is about); the dramatic situation (the circumstances surrounding the action). The setup is completed within thirty pages. Openings fall into two types: a strong visual action sequence (*Star Wars*) or a rapid expository character introduction (the moans in bed interrupted by the telephone in *Shampoo*).
- Beginning links to ending: starts on a highway, ends on a highway; starts with a poker game, ends with a poker game; boxing match to boxing match; "Will they cover it?"
- **Sequence** = "a series of scenes tied together by a single idea," serving as the backbone of the screenplay (a wedding, a chase, a homecoming, a bank robbery). Determine the sequence idea first (the cup) before pouring in the content (the coffee). Frank Pierson wrote *Dog Day Afternoon* using only twelve sequences, writing four first (beginning, two plot points, ending) before adding the remaining eight.
- Theme = action + character: "A screenplay is like a noun: a specific person in a specific place doing their thing." Action divides into physical and emotional.
- Variations: *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* runs 40/40/40; in *Nashville*, the city itself is the protagonist—"follow the protagonist and you will find the plot points."

## III. BS2 Save the Cat 15 Beats (Snyder, 110 Pages)

| # | Beat | Page | Core Points |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening Image | 1 | Tone, genre, scale; snapshot of protagonist's "before"; the opposite of the Final Image |
| 2 | Theme Stated | 5 | Usually spoken to the protagonist by someone else; the script = an argument surrounding this |
| 3 | Set-Up | 1–10 | Introduce or mention all A-story characters; "the six things that need fixing" (ticking time bombs to detonate and heal later); storm is brewing, stasis equals death |
| 4 | Catalyst | 12 | Telegram, firing, catching an affair, three days left; good news disguised as bad news; if it drifts to page 20, cut back |
| 5 | Debate | 12–25 | Last chance to say "this is crazy"; poses a question |
| 6 | Break into Two | 25 | No later than page 25; protagonist **decides on their own** to enter, cannot be lured or wake up on a spaceship |
| 7 | B Story | 30 | Usually the love story, carries the theme; new characters are the mirror-image opposite of Act One characters |
| 8 | Fun and Games | 30–55 | **Delivering the promise of the premise**; core of the poster, trailer material |
| 9 | Midpoint | 55 | False victory or false defeat; "stakes are raised"; the inverse mirror of "All Is Lost" |
| 10 | Bad Guys Close In | 55–75 | Hardest to write; external villains regroup + internal team splinters |
| 11 | All Is Lost | 75 | Worse than the beginning; **whiff of death** (Obi-Wan dies; if nobody dies, use a goldfish or a flower) |
| 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | 75–85 | 5 seconds to 5 minutes; only by being defeated first can the lesson be learned |
| 13 | Break into Three | 85 | Solution found via B-story character / thematic discussion; A-story and B-story merge |
| 14 | Finale | 85–110 | Dispatch villains from lowest to highest (minions die first); protagonist must change the world; "synthesis" |
| 15 | Final Image | 110 | Opposite of the opening; absence means Act Two missed something |

Three acts = thesis / antithesis / synthesis. **Setup running past page 25 = audience wanting their money back** (*Minority Report* taking 40 minutes to set its hook).

### The Board (40 Cards)
- The Board divides into four rows: 1–25 / 25–55 (to Midpoint) / 55–85 / 85–110, each ending in a major turn.
- Pin up the most exciting scenes first -> identify blanks -> pin the three major turning points (Midpoint first, then All Is Lost as its opposite, then Break into Three).
- Common ailment: stuffing 20 cards into Act One (all backstory, ultimately compressed into one "Protagonist Enters" card); **Act Three perpetually having only two cards** ("Protagonist knows what to do" and "The Big Showdown")—go back and check the six flaws, B-story, and bad guy roster.
- On each card write **+/-** (emotional shift across the scene; if you cannot identify one, toss the card) and **><** (who has the goal, who is in the way, who wins; write only one conflict per scene).
- Field's card method shares the same root: 3x5 cards with a few words each, color-coded by act; workflow is Act One in one day, Act Two in two days, Act Three in one day, followed by weeks of shuffling and moving scenes. "Trust your story." Once writing starts, the board can be abandoned.

## IV. Nine Beats (Hoxter) and Linear Progression

- Act I (<30 mins): **Basic Exposition** (protagonist has a problem/desire; Inciting Incident) -> **Debate** (wants it but does not know how to get it; first commitment is weak and unrealistic—*Jojo Rabbit* throwing the grenade and injuring himself = "a long way to go").
- Act II (≈45 mins in two halves): **First Steps** -> **Raising the Stakes** (wolf chases into cave where mother bear waits; ex-girlfriend is dating the ex-boyfriend who hates you and tomorrow becomes your department manager) -> **Midpoint** (second commitment, point of no return, protagonist becomes hero) -> **Commitment** (assistance) -> **Crisis** (lowest point + revelation; opening the heart for the first time).
- Act III (<30 mins): **Confrontation** (plan meets counterattack) -> **Resolution** (goal achieved, theme demonstrated, world transformed; "give us a moment to feel it").
- Different stories emphasize different beats: *Winter's Bone* has the longest debate (rejected house by house); *How to Train Your Dragon* spends 20+ minutes on first steps.
- **A Story** = main plotline, what the protagonist wants, surface objective; **B Story** = key interpersonal relationship, "the person you tell your heart to," strongest in Act Two; at the end of Act Two, the B-story character helps forge the Act Three plan, and executing that plan resolves the A-story. Once the theme is set, it never changes; what changes is the attitude toward it.
- Six questions for "Is three-act right for me?": Popular genre? Strong goal-driven? Plot-driven? Clear ending? Major protagonist arc? Mainstream audience target?
- Tarantino's three-act information gap: Act One audience does not understand, characters know more; Act Two reaches parity; Act Three audience is far ahead.
- William Goldman's Ten Commandments: Thou shalt not take the crisis out of the protagonist's hands; thou shalt not make life easy for the protagonist; thou shalt not sermonize for the sake of sermonizing; thou shalt not fabricate cheap shocks; thou shalt respect the audience; thou shalt know thy world as God knows the world; thou shalt not artificially complicate what is already rich; thou shalt never stop exploring to push characters into deepest conflict; thou shalt not write on-the-nose dialogue, every line must carry Subtext; thou shalt rewrite repeatedly.

## V. Three Acts = Attraction / Anticipation / Satisfaction (Hicks)

- Act One must generate **attraction**: making the audience "willing to follow along"; Act Two manages **anticipation**: keeping the audience constantly guessing what happens next and guessing wrong; Act Three delivers **satisfaction**: fulfilling all expectations, but never in the expected manner.
- **Drama is conflict**: arranging conflict, compressing time, breaking down events—*Bonnie and Clyde* breaks "stealing a car" into parking spot, keys, ignition into a chain of micro-actions where every step can go wrong.
- **Ten-Element Checklist** (fill out before outlining): prune backstory (keep only what serves the objective); ban flashbacks; inner need; Inciting Incident / catalytic spark; external goal; preparation; opposing forces must be stronger; self-revelation; obsessive entanglement; protagonist must win the struggle directly; problem resolved.
- Every scene does two things: advances the external goal + reveals the inner need. "Less is more."
- Five premise questions: Who? Wants what? Why now? Who prevents it? What is the outcome?

## VI. Structural Techniques of Chinese Short-Form Drama (Lu Jun)

### Introduction, Elaboration, Turn, Resolution = Phoenix Head, Pig Belly, Leopard Tail
- **Introduction (Qi)**: the onset of contradiction and the initial conflict, lighting the fuse; clearly reveals the disposition, forces, and trajectories of both sides; generally brief, embedding exposition in action—steep, sharp, and arresting.
- **Elaboration (Cheng)**: occupies roughly two-thirds, interlocking and climbing step by step, winding and turning, alternating fast and slow; Leigh Hunt: "spur the horse's flanks to make it run forward, while pulling the reins to keep its stride natural."
- **Turn (Zhuan)**: the climactic peak, where character and theme reach their absolute depth, "a crash of thunder."
- **Resolution (He)**: following immediately upon the turn, concise with lingering resonance.
- The introduction must arouse anticipation, the elaboration must hold attention, the turn must shake the viewer, and the resolution must leave an aftertaste—even a one-thousand-word sketch requires all four movements complete.

### Fast Entry: Three Dos, Three Don'ts, and Eight Opening Methods
- Better near than far (make the first cut close to the climax, deleting retrospective exposition prior to conflict), better simple than complicated (a single small incident driving straight in through twists and turns), better dynamic than static (open with dramatic situation; even one person can generate a commotion). Avoid starting too far back, scattering focus, or multiplying doubts—**drama dreads nothing more than explanation**.
- Eight opening methods: direct conflict (conflict right as the curtain rises: "The current township head will not be coming"); disposition layout (setting up the conflict situation immediately); atmosphere buildup (direct, lateral, or inverted atmosphere surrounding the central event); unexpected incident (a blind girl arriving to audition); cross-purposes (the slacker saying "if you had notified me I would have picked it up myself"); sudden blow (father decreeing three flower baskets must be woven every night); self-introduction (chorus, narrator, or phone call to reduce exposition); driven to the brink (an old spouse caught astride the wall stealing a breeding rabbit and interrogated).
- William Archer: an opening must "clearly point in that direction on the horizon where the dark clouds are gathering."

### Clear Progression: Finding the Dramatic Core + Four Operations
- Rodin chopping off the "perfect hands" of Balzac's statue—four words for balancing detail and brevity: **highlight the core**. Lu Xun's *Kong Yiji* spends one hundred and fifty words on broken legs and five hundred words on fennel beans—identifying the Dramatic Core of the "pure child's heart" as the standard for selection.
- Write in full detail wherever character is delineated (lingering repetition, winding twists, step-by-step hesitation); write concisely at beginnings and endings, in broad situational descriptions, and wherever irrelevant to the theme.
- Clarifying progression: **subtraction** (pruning people and incidents to clear room for the shining spark, daring to let go of darlings), **addition** (supplying social conditions and emotional grounds for anomalous behavior), **multiplication** (magnifying a comical element manifold into extreme exaggeration), **division** (if the point of entry is wrong, scrapping it to start anew elsewhere).

### The Climax Must Be Towering
- "Never put pen to paper lightly without finding the climax"—Alexandre Dumas fils: "Do not begin until you have completely settled the action and dialogue of the final scene"; Ibsen: "I could almost say I wrote this play for the sake of the very last scene"; Bai Yushuang's example: the red bridal dress turning into a black funeral banner; once the climax was set, eight scenes were dashed off in one breath.
- Three paradigms: **driven by character** (Zhang Guirong shifting from endurance to snapping the whip and seizing the land deed: "I am a person! I belong to the state! Not a beast of burden you feed"); **driven by environment** (a new institutional order providing the macro-environment; *Cops and Robbers* where the fake policeman steps into role under a patrolman's guidance, finally crying "caught one," with the other being himself); **unexpected event** (the "dead" son suddenly walking through the door).
- Four flaws: absent climax (three vignettes given equal weight like a peep-show); premature climax (sobbing, cursing, and threatening suicide within five minutes); flat climax (ferocious shouting while the plot skids without rising action); artificial climax (resorting casually to kneeling, divorce, dividing property, slapping faces—kneeling is permissible only when it becomes a character inevitability and produces a new relationship, as in *Bao Gong's Apology* where the sister-in-law kneels: "I kneel before your loyalty to country and people, third brother").
- **Drama precedes the climax as well**: construct obligatory scenes, master delaying tactics, suppress before elevating (the magic mirror flipping to a bronze mirror before flipping back for "the final reflection").

### Transformations Must Be Smooth (Untying the Knot)
- Eight pitfalls: kicking contradictions upstairs (superior officers arriving, calling, or issuing decrees); mild remedies for severe ailments (instant epiphany after a single test); off-point resolution (saving the girlfriend's father has nothing to do with "reckless scientific research"); lengthy moralizing monologues; offstage handling (Secretary Zhang criticized him); calling in reinforcements; accidental discovery of blood kinship; the tired trap of walking into the lion's den.
- Three methods: **power of environment** (surrendering an unreformable villain to irresistible social forces); **power of preparation** (a folk song progressing layer by layer from love song to oppressed ballad to underground pass-phrase); **power of action—ideally a signature masterstroke** (*A'er Receives His Wife* weaving the basket: both proof of transformation, fitted to the stage, and echoing the opening).

### Endings Must Be Striking
- Conceive the ending first (Cao Yu). Four cautions: too neatly rounded (imposing an idealized halo); dragging on ("end when it ought to end," *The Necklace* losing resonance with a single word added); jarring leaps (sudden strokes of genius leaping into limitless moralizing); collapsing weakly (four lines of offstage singing forcing a hard close).
- Eight methods: **thunderclap** (climax and ending merge as one: "those typewriters," "I am the inspector, I have stayed next door for three nights," "he would rather confess," "the engagement banquet was eaten last month," towel hits the floor); **unresolved question** ("Should I forgive him?" spoken to the audience); **extended suspension** (*Oppression*: "What is your surname?" "I—ah—I—"); **rebound** (singer leaving a suicidal lover: "I still have one minute and ten seconds" to catch the train; a gunshot sounds offstage); **illuminating line** (a single speech by the fiancée illuminating the entire play); **echoing frame** (three gongs at the opening answered by three gongs at the ending, contrasting heavy and light); **cyclical return** (reunion -> memory -> back to reunion: "Beware the day of the people's wrath"; Anne begins writing letters again); **conclusive verdict** ("a drawn game," choral summary).

### Changes Must Be Plentiful
- "Where there is change, there is drama." Six kinds: relationship changes (rotation between deceiver and deceived); character changes; fortune changes (three major rises and falls); conflict situation changes (a single gun changing hands four times); atmosphere changes (duel turning into courtship); narrative mode changes. Priority of sources: **character-driven > screenwriting craft > accidental events > external forces**. Five or six significant changes mark a passing grade for a short play; the fifteen in *Striking the Brass Gong* stand as a textbook example.

## VII. Inciting Incident and Progressive Complications (McKee)

### Inciting Incident
- Five-part design: Inciting Incident -> Progressive Complications -> Crisis -> Climax -> Resolution.
- Must be **dynamically and fully developed** (moving to Los Angeles is not; wallpapering parking tickets plus escaping down the fire escape on a felony warrant is), radically upsetting the balance of life to positive or negative; **the protagonist must react** (refusal is also a reaction); arouses the desire to restore balance -> object of desire -> quest; a complex protagonist simultaneously conceives an unconscious desire.
- **Must happen on screen**, posing the central dramatic question and projecting the **obligatory scene** (the shark and the police chief facing off).
- Placement: within the first quarter; can occur in the first second (*On the Waterfront*) or at minutes 27–32 (*Rocky*, *Casablanca*); if delayed, subplots must bridge the gap; **introduce as early as possible, but only when the time is ripe**—how much does the audience need to know to react fully? (*Rocky* needs half an hour before "Him? That bum?!").
- Creative strategy: **how the worst thing can become the best thing** (*Kramer vs. Kramer*), **how the best can become the worst** (*Death in Venice*); "if a story is to become art, there is no middle ground."
- **Spine of the story**: if there is no unconscious desire, the conscious goal is the spine; if present, the unconscious desire is the spine, allowing the conscious goal to shift continuously.
- Hoxter: two categories of catalytic events—character-driven (friend suggests substitute teaching) or coincidence (mistaken identity; distress signal); hook the reader within ten pages.

### Progressive Complications
- Facing ever-stronger opposition generates ever-greater conflict, crossing a succession of **points of no return**: each gap eliminates actions of equal magnitude. A "soft paunch sagging over the belt" = the writer repeating actions of Act One scale. Requires inventing hundreds of scenes to quarry out 40–60.
- **Law of Conflict**: "Nothing in a story moves forward except through conflict"; the volume of conflict in life is a constant (squeezing a balloon).
- **The Gap**: the character always takes the most conservative action from their subjective point of view -> the world responds differently or far more powerfully than expected -> a second action carrying greater risk. Risk test: "If the protagonist does not get what they want, what do they stand to lose?" If the answer is "return to normal," it is not worth telling.
- Complication-based (single level: action films having only extra-personal conflict, soap operas having only personal conflict) vs. complex (inner, personal, and extra-personal levels turning simultaneously—the French toast in *Kramer vs. Kramer* turning across three values and three levels). Counsel: **do not proliferate characters, do not multiply locations; keep it relatively simple yet complex.**
- Four techniques for progression: social progression (widening the radius of action's impact outward); personal progression (driving deeply into intimate relationships and the inner psyche); symbolic ascension (imagery moving from specific to universal, remaining invisible); ironic ascension (six types: finally getting it too late; driven away from the goal only to be guided toward it; discarding what is later recognized as essential; working toward a goal while unwittingly moving in reverse; seeking destruction only to shoot oneself in the foot; receiving what seemed a curse and trying to shed it, only for it to prove a blessing).

### Act Design
- Aristotle: the longer the work, the more major reversals required. Feature length requires at least three acts (good -> bad -> ?). Proportions: Act One ≈ 25% (peaking around minutes 20–30); **the final act is the shortest, ≤ 20 minutes** (accelerating sensation); Act Two's 70-minute swamp -> two solutions: **subplots** (each carrying its own act structure) or **multiplying acts** (mid-act climax -> four acts; five acts for *Four Weddings and a Funeral*; seven acts for *Raiders of the Lost Ark*). The cost of multi-act design: proliferating climaxes breeds cliché and dilutes impact. A three-act central plot + subplots is standard.
- **False endings** (E.T. dying, blowing up the ship in *Alien*) do not suit most films; the penultimate act climax should intensify the question: "At this point, what more could possibly happen?"
- **Act rhythm**: the climaxes of the last two acts cannot carry the same charge (never build up from an up, never build down from a down); an ironic ending requires establishing a direction first so the penultimate act can move in reverse.
- **Four subplot relationships**: **contradicting** the Controlling Idea (sweet romance A-story + betrayal subplot); **resonating** with it (multiple plots rooted in this, "parenting can never be won"); **foreshadowing** to fill the vacuum of a delayed main plot; **generating complications for the main plot** (most important: a love interest in a crime film). Neither contradicting, resonating, foreshadowing, nor complicating -> cleaving the story in two. Focus must be maintained (*Rocky* handling the love story carefully to preserve the sports genre).
- **Break a rule for only one reason: to replace it with something more important** (*Kiss of the Spider Woman* delaying the Inciting Incident to the mid-act climax, building empathy first before revealing the deal).

## VIII. Crisis, Climax, and Resolution (McKee)

- **Crisis** = decision; the protagonist has exhausted all actions except the very last; the obligatory scene; must be a true dilemma (choosing between two irreconcilable goods or the lesser of two evils; choosing between good and evil is no choice at all); **the crisis decision must be an intentional, static moment**, never placed offscreen or treated offhandedly (*Thelma & Louise*: "Go? What do you mean, go?"). Crisis and climax can take place in different locations, but must be cut together without cutting away to a subplot.
- **Crisis within the climax**: prying open another gap in the midst of climactic action (the spiral of three crisis decisions in *The Empire Strikes Back*).
- **Climax**: the crowning reversal need not be noisy violence but **must be filled with meaning**—"meaning generates emotion"; action must be pure, clean, and self-evident; Aristotle's "inevitable and unexpected"; Goldman's "give the audience what they want, but not in the way they expect"; audiences want emotional satisfaction, not neat happy endings (downbeat endings also profit); **key image**: a climax written for the eye + a single image encapsulating the Controlling Idea (the handcuffs in *Greed*; tearing up the apartment in *The Conversation*).
- Once the climax is reached, proceed to **rewrite from back to front**, assessing every scene against the climax to justify its existence, trimming anything whose removal changes nothing; subplots climaxing inside the main plot's climax are best (Rick's single action resolving love + politics + Renault + Strasser), otherwise the least important climaxes first.
- "Movies are all about the last twenty minutes"; 75% of effort goes into design, and 75% of that goes into the final act climax.
- **Resolution** has three purposes: giving scenes to subplots unresolved inside the climax (can draw the main plot into the resolution to create a false complication); displaying the spread of the climax's fallout; showing courtesy to the audience—adding a line on the final page for a slow pulling-away shot.
- Hoxter's three climax questions: Does the villain's defeat express the theme? Does the protagonist do what they could not do in Act One? Does it conclude the plotline while wrapping up the whole story?
- Coincidence: introduce early to allow time to build meaning; never use coincidence after the midpoint; **never use coincidence to turn the ending** (a *deus ex machina* is a lie and an insult, anti-structure excepted; comedy can tolerate it only if the protagonist has endured immense suffering and never despaired).

## IX. Non-Linear, Multi-Strand, and Ensemble

- Non-chronological (*Reservoir Dogs* flashback reveals; *500 Days of Summer* evoking memory; *Pulp Fiction* circularity; *Lost Highway* circular puzzle) — Hoxter: still maintain sequence coherence; framing narratives can be circular (*La Jetee*, *12 Monkeys*).
- Multi-strand plots (*Much Ado About Nothing*, *Contagion*); anthology films (*Sin City*); ensemble films (with a central protagonist like *The Dirty Dozen* / thematic correlation like *Love Actually*, *Babel*, where a single Inciting Incident impacts all threads); minimalist plots (*Stranger Than Paradise* across three locations and three acts where "nothing happens").
- McKee: multiple protagonists each carrying desires -> multi-plot, held together by **resonance** (variations on the same Controlling Idea) rather than causality (Altman and Jarmusch films where "the mesh depends on the degree of correspondence among the threads").
- Flashbacks: Tony Bill: "When I see a flashback, I know the story is in trouble"; McKee: dramatize them (mini-dramas possessing an Inciting Incident, progression, and turning point) and **create the need and desire to know before deploying them**; Trottier: "If the reader doesn't care about what's happening now, we shouldn't tell them what happened in the past"; Hicks: ban flashbacks (compress backstory into present action using present-tense active voice).
- Chekhovian anti-climax (even-numbered act divisions, no central climax, events occurring offstage) see `chekhov-dramaturgy`; Ozu's "events offscreen, reactions onscreen" see `ozu-screenplay-style`.

## X. Structural Diagnostic Checklist

1. **Can you describe what it is about in one or two sentences** (noun-based: who, where, doing what)? If not, you are not ready to write.
2. **Do you know the ending, beginning, Plot Point I, and Plot Point II**? Is the midpoint a false victory or a false defeat, serving as the inverse mirror of "All Is Lost"?
3. Do the first ten pages establish protagonist, premise, and situation? Does the setup exceed 25 pages? Has the Catalyst drifted past page 20?
4. Does the protagonist **decide on their own** to enter Act Two? Does the protagonist take action or merely react to the environment? Is the protagonist asking questions (many question marks in dialogue = structural trouble)?
5. Does the value change between the beginning and end of every scene? Is a scene without a value shift merely serving exposition?
6. Does every gap in Act Two eliminate actions of equal magnitude? Is there a "soft paunch" (repeating Act One scale)? Does the pace accelerate after the midpoint?
7. Is the sum of opposing forces stronger than the protagonist at the Inciting Incident (the protagonist should be an "underdog")? Are the bad guys bad enough ("make the bad guys worse")?
8. Around page 75, is there a whiff of death? Is the crisis a static dilemma? Is the climax "inevitable and unexpected"? Is there a key image?
9. Does the subplot contradict, resonate with, foreshadow, or complicate the Controlling Idea? If none of these, it is cleaving the story.
10. Is the final act the shortest? Do the climaxes of the last two acts carry opposite polarities? Does the ending attend to subplots and grant the audience a breath?
11. Lu Jun's four questions: Is the entry fast (start close, single focus, few doubts)? Is the climax towering (not premature, not flat, not artificial)? Is the transformation smooth (not kicked upstairs, not trivialized, not pushed offstage, not bailed out)? Is the ending striking (not rounded, not dragging, not jumping, not collapsing)?
12. Count the changes: a short piece needs at least five or six significant changes, originating primarily from character.
13. Was the correct answer stated and rejected in Act One (the Chekhov / Lopakhin technique), and is what was foreshadowed fulfilled or deliberately unfulfilled at the ending?

## XI. Workflow: From Premise to Script

1. **One-sentence logline** (Snyder's four elements: ironic hook + mental picture + audience and cost + title; add protagonist adjective, antagonist adjective, primal goal); test by pitching to strangers. Hoxter's logline formula: protagonist + Inciting Incident + conflict/challenge + stakes + theme, highlighting the hook; not a tagline.
2. **Determine genre and position on the Story Triangle**; compile a list of comparable films to identify conventions and clichés.
3. **Fill out the ten elements** (Hicks) and five premise questions; define protagonist needs and obstacles.
4. **Determine the ending first**, then the beginning and the two plot points; find the "key image" of the climax.
5. **BS2 One-Sheet**: fill out the Save the Cat 15 Beats matching target page counts; Theme Stated at page 5, Catalyst at page 12, Break into Two no later than page 25, Midpoint at 55, All Is Lost at 75, Break into Three at 85.
6. **The Board with 40 cards**, marking +/- and >< on each; Act Three cannot have only two cards.
7. **Step outline** (McKee): one or two sentences per scene ("He enters the room expecting to see her, only to find a note saying she has left forever"), noting structural position on the back; the purpose is to **destroy your own work**—sketching a scene a dozen ways before discarding; rewrite from back to front once the climax is locked.
8. **Pitch the story for ten minutes** while watching the listener's eyes, without showing the outline; an enthusiastic reaction = a quiet "wow" followed by silence.
9. **Treatment** (60–90 pages, present-tense moment-by-moment description, **no dialogue**, writing out Subtext) -> prune, expand, and reorder scenes at this stage.
10. **Screenplay**: five to ten pages a day, where dialogue emerges at its best; when flaws appear, return to the treatment to fix setups and payoffs. Skipping steps to write the screenplay directly produces a counterfeit, uninspected treatment and is the slowest method of all.
11. **Just get the first draft down** (Field: err on the side of length, 180–200 pages is better than 90) -> coolly compress -> polish. Kick off the next scene before wrapping up each day (Hoxter).
12. **Revision by objective targets**: examine Act Two first ("generally speaking, yes"); Snyder's nine rewrite checks: protagonist active without asking questions; no narrative hand-holding; villain made worse; turning points accelerated; emotional color wheel; cover dialogue test; give supporting characters a limp and an eyepatch; pull back the starting point; make it primal enough.

# Premise & Theme (前提·主题·立意·戏核)

This skill answers four questions: **what to write** (subject selection), **why write it** (premise / Controlling Idea / the third rail), **from where to cut into it** (point of view / Dramatic Core), and **how to say it in a single sentence** (logline / one-sentence story). Sources: Lajos Egri's *The Art of Dramatic Writing*, Robert McKee's *Story*, Neill D. Hicks' *Screenwriting 101: The Essential Craft of Feature Film Writing*, Lisa Cron's *Story Genius*, Julian Hoxter's *The Creative Screenwriter: 12 Rules to Follow and Break to Unlock Your Screenwriting Potential*, Blake Snyder's *Save the Cat!*, and Lu Jun's *Screenwriting Theory and Technique: Starting from Short-Form Drama (Chinese original, no English edition)*, chapters 1–6. How they complement one another: Egri provides the dialectic and tyrannical nature of the "premise"; McKee provides "Controlling Idea = value + cause" and reverse-engineering from the climax; Cron provides concrete steps to construct the protagonist's inner conflict (desire vs. misbelief); Lu Jun provides the tools for subject selection, deepening concept, point of view, and Dramatic Core from Chinese dramatic craft; Hicks, Hoxter, and Snyder provide commercial validation in a single sentence. Rabkin connects thematic argument to concrete conflict.

For example libraries and worksheets, see [reference.md](reference.md).

## I. The Premise Is a Tyrant (Egri)

- Every second has a premise. An inventor who does not know the purpose of a device, or a runner who does not know where to go, is insane; a screenplay can be packed with frenzied action while no one knows the motivation.
- **Three parts of a premise**: "Frugality leads to waste"—frugality = character, leads to = conflict, waste = ending. "A good premise is a thumbnail synopsis of your play." Premises of famous plays: *Romeo and Juliet* = great love defies even death; *King Lear* = blind trust leads to destruction; *Macbeth* = ruthless ambition leads to its own destruction; *Othello* = jealousy destroys itself and the object of its love; *Ghosts* = the sins of the fathers are visited upon the children; *A Doll's House* = inequality between the sexes in marriage breeds misery.
- What premise checklists lack is **the author's conviction**: "Which side are you on?" "Any person with strong convictions is a storehouse of premises." A premise borrowed from outside does not belong to you; "You must not write anything you do not believe." The premise is never spoken in dialogue, but the entire play proves it.
- **A play can have only one premise**; two premises = pulling in two directions (*Shadow and Substance*, where the first three and three-quarters acts argue "wisdom conquers superstition," only to pivot abruptly at the end). Sub-premises merely supplement the master premise.
- The premise need not be universal truth; within your work, it is absolute truth. Rodin chiseled off the hands of the Balzac statue: "No part can be more important than the whole."
- You may start from character, an incident, or an idea, but you must find the premise before you begin writing; if you change your mind, choose a new premise and start over. Deducing a premise from an idea: writing about love → great love → "love conquers all" is too vague → "can love transcend death? Yes" → from this deduce the mandatory character traits (the girl cannot be foolish or callous, the boy cannot be shallow) and the ladder of struggle.
- **The premise drives the only choice** (the *A Doll's House* workshop): need a married couple → Helmer cares about social standing → Nora's action must threaten his standing and be done for Helmer's sake → involves money → theft will not do (it turns the premise into a condemnation of stealing) → emergency funds → Helmer would rather die than borrow → only her father can guarantee the loan → **"Nora's father must die"** → forging a signature. "The premise is a tyrant; it allows you to walk down only one road."
- The proof of the premise is the "obligatory scene": highest expectation is aroused only when the premise is being proved; obligatory scenes in many plays fall flat because their premise is muddled.
- Dialectics creates character: thesis (external demand) / antithesis (character desire) / synthesis (resolution); the Irene case—you must prove "why she specifically had to end up a prostitute," ruling out all other possibilities.
- The ending must be caused by the character's actions, never by accident or dying of old age (the Aunt Clara case).

## II. Controlling Idea = Value + Cause (McKee)

- The **premise** is "what would happen if..." (*Jaws*, *Kramer vs. Kramer*), and need not remain in the finished work; the **Controlling Idea** is a single sentence: "Justice is restored because the protagonist is more violent (*Dirty Harry*) / more cunning (*Columbo*) than the criminal"; "Dictatorship is sustained by a corrupt CIA" (*Missing*); "One learns to love unconditionally" (*Groundhog Day*). It functions as a creative commandment: ski-cap deductive reasoning does not suit Harry, and a .44 Magnum does not suit Columbo.
- Storytelling is a creative demonstration of truth, the conversion of idea into action, **never relying on expository preachiness**; "crime does not pay" must be acted out.
- **Write the climax first and look backward**: What value does the climax produce? What is the primary cause? "The story tells you its meaning"; "If the plot develops exactly as you originally planned, your writing method is far too rigid."
- **Idea vs. Counter-Idea**: Sequences alternately express positive and negative dimensions, granting equal firepower to both warring sides; if the antagonist is a fool, victory is worthless; preachiness = treating art like a scalpel; Stanley Kubrick's three anti-war films all concede humanity's lust for war. Classics offer no pat answers; they offer "a medicine to maintain sobriety."
- Three types of Controlling Ideas: **Idealistic** (upward turn); **Pessimistic** (downward turn; *Chinatown*: "evil prevails because it is part of human nature"); **Ironic**—positive irony = redemption plot ("relentless pursuit of success, wealth, and acclaim will destroy you, but if you recognize this in time and renounce obsession, you find salvation"—*Kramer vs. Kramer*, *Out of Africa*, *North Dallas Forty* opening his arms to let the ball drop); negative irony = punitive plot ("obsessively and ruthlessly pursuing desire will destroy you"—*Wall Street*, *Nixon*). Irony is the hardest to write: a single action must be both positive and negative simultaneously; it must be clear, un-ambiguous, and motivated; positive and negative must not cancel each other out.
- The more tightly you construct around a single clear idea, the more meaning audiences will discover; the more you cram in, the faster the structure collapses. Persevere: great writers have a single passion-igniting theme (Hemingway with death, Dickens with the search for the father, Moliere with human folly and vice).
- Ask yourself: "Do I truly believe the meaning of this story?" The artist's sole responsibility = tell the truth.

### From thematic argument to concrete conflict (Rabkin)

William Rabkin, *Writing the Pilot: Creating the Series*, treats theme as a specific argument: a thematic question or a contradictory statement, not a one-word topic. Opposition of thesis and counter-thesis organizes the work.

- Rabkin turns a keyword into a specific opposition, then into interpersonal conflict that can carry the argument in story. Abstract opposition is not enough until people enact it.
- Rabkin says discovery may begin with concept or character. Theme, conflict, and concept must connect before writing.
- For Rabkin's evil-in-pursuit-of-good example, include unsuccessful conventional allies so the protagonist's harmful methods in pursuit of good remain intelligible.

Examples in [reference.md](reference.md).

## III. The Third Rail: Desire vs. Misbelief (Cron)

- **Story does not equal plot**. A story is "how what happens affects someone trying to achieve an arduous goal, and how it transforms their internal self"; writing a series of events without a story is why 96% of manuscripts get rejected.
- **The Third Rail** = the protagonist's internal struggle: a long-desired goal vs. the **misbelief** blocking them. Every action, plot turn, and sensory detail must sit directly on this rail.
- Five fallacies: prose style reigns supreme; free-writing; "the first draft is always shit"; outlining external events first; shoehorning into external structural templates ("first you need a good story, only then do you get structure").
- **From flash of inspiration to what-if question** in four steps: 1. Capture the moment that strikes you. 2. Ask why you care. 3. Identify the main theme (cliches are fine; "almost every story begins as a cliche"). 4. Formulate the what-if question: include context, surprise, conflict, and subtext; can run several lines.
- **The three Why steps**: 1. What does the protagonist want before the story opens (from abstract to something you can visualize with eyes closed)? 2. Why do they want it (what does it mean to them)? 3. **The misbelief**—neither a fatal flaw nor just trauma: something the protagonist believes to be correct, because at a crucial moment in life it once "worked" ("intimacy will only hurt you").
- **The Origin Scene**: the exact moment the misbelief took root; write it in full detail, in the first person and chronological order, answering four questions: old belief before entering / why / scene goal / how the character expected it to be fulfilled—even if the wish is granted, it can collapse due to opposite underlying reasons. Add **three turning scenes**: each time stepping toward the goal only to retreat out of misbelief, simultaneously intensifying both desire and belief.
- Theme springs from internal contradiction; external what-if questions trigger that inner contradiction to forge the theme (*Romeo and Juliet*: feuding parents → yearning to be together vs. family loyalty).
- Ending does not equal the external events of the final act; it is **the moment the misbelief is defeated and the world is seen through a new lens**; write the epiphany scene first; the opening can already hint at this trajectory.
- Two tests for the primary external problem: external three-column check (can escalate to the final page, has concrete and immediate consequences, has a ticking clock) + internal two-question check (forces a rebellion against the misbelief, inflicts concrete emotional damage); surface incidents (stealing a dog, fixing plumbing) are merely devices forcing the character to confront their inner self.

## IV. Five Premise Questions and Ten Elements (Hicks)

- **Premise** = a concise statement of the central conflict, answering five questions: Who is the protagonist? Who is the antagonist? Why are they in conflict? What change does the outcome of the conflict bring? Why must the protagonist take action? Only when answered does it form a gestalt. State it in three sentences: a protagonist opposes another; why they must oppose each other to reach their respective goals; what will change in the protagonist's life.
- Drama = conflict that brings **palpable change** to the protagonist's life while simultaneously altering the surrounding environment; select events with direct causal links around "palpable transformation X."
- **Internal Need**: a quality the protagonist should possess but lacks, assumes they already have, or treats as trivial (compassion, forgiveness, courage)—the primary reason audiences keep watching is that the protagonist must realize this quality to resolve the conflict. Two tests for the **External Goal**: the audience must know where the goal is (need not approve of it); the audience must feel the protagonist is threatened. Use Maslow's hierarchy of needs to verify goal plausibility (competing for glory makes no sense when basic sustenance is in jeopardy).
- Theme is not a textbook: "The worst torture is making a film that plays like a textbook"; audiences expect to make the world more intelligible through shared experience.

## V. Theme Stays Constant, Attitude Shifts (Hoxter)

- Almost all films are guided by theme; three questions: What does the protagonist want? What are they struggling against? Is the theme love, betrayal, loyalty, coming-of-age, friendship, revenge, or survival? A film about romance is not necessarily about love as its theme.
- **The sweet spot: once the theme is set, it never changes; what changes is the attitude toward it**; what is the protagonist's attitude toward the theme at the beginning versus the end? An innocent friend vs. a cynical friend, plus their respective partners = the four-way interaction "contains the value system of the entire screenplay." Overly broad or passive themes are useless; ask "how to actively drive this theme forward."
- The thematic test bench: take the same raw material (a widowed father "carried away" by a spaceship captain) and run it through coming-of-age / love / sacrifice / obsession / family / friendship / revenge / justice, seeing which theme brings the characters to life best.
- Conflict: both protagonist and antagonist should have goals connected to the theme (in *How to Train Your Dragon*, father and son both want to hunt dragons, but in different ways); **obstacle does not equal antagonist** (the Red Death dragon is an obstacle; the father is the antagonist, because only the father articulates the theme).
- Climax three-question check: Does the antagonist's defeat express the theme? Does the protagonist do something they could not do in Act One? Does it conclude the plotline while resolving the overall story?
- Character: protagonist holds distinct views on six things (ranging from atheism down to how to cook fettuccine) → four relationship characters each take a stance → generates plotlines driven by thematic clash.

## VI. One-Sentence Story (Snyder) and Logline (Hoxter)

- **Snyder's four elements**: ironic hook ("A cop comes to LA to visit his estranged wife, only for her office building to be seized by terrorists"); a vivid mental picture (including time frame); target audience and budget; a killer title (*Legally Blonde* is great; *For Love or Money* is terrible). Enhanced version: add protagonist adjective + antagonist adjective + primal goal. Inability to write a viable one-sentence story indicates a fundamental flaw in the story itself; pitch it to a stranger waiting in line at Starbucks and watch whether their eyes wander.
- **Ten Story Genres** (Monster in the House / Golden Fleece / Out of the Bottle / Dude with a Problem / Rites of Passage / Buddy Love / Whydunit / Fool Triumphant / Institutionalized / Superhero): categorize by "what it is about"; master the genre rules before breaking them; never pitch using "X meets Y."
- **Make it primal**: survival, hunger, sex, protecting loved ones, fear of death; frame relationships as husband, wife, father, daughter, mother, son, ex.
- **Hoxter's logline**: one or two sentences that convey the core concept; checklist—protagonist, antagonist, genre, conflict, challenge, stakes, theme, twist, **hook**, Inciting Incident; formula: "When his partner is murdered by a ruthless alien (Inciting Incident), a hard-bitten Brooklyn beat cop Lauren Brown (protagonist) must track down the alien's homeworld (challenge) to avenge his partner (theme)." **Logline does not equal tagline** (a tagline is post-release marketing); "X meets Y" is not a logline. Plot vs. story: plot = on-screen events; story = subtext, why, emotional journey; loglines emphasize plot.
- McKee: title = name, designating character / setting / theme / genre (*Jaws* captures all four); literary, abstract, or non-descriptive titles are counterproductive.

## VII. Subject Selection Must Be Rigorous (Lu Jun)

- Lu Xun: "Subject selection must be rigorous, excavation deep; never stitch together an entire piece from petty, meaningless incidents."
- **Four Pitfalls to Avoid**: Chasing scale and solemnity (a grand subject does not equal grand significance; high character awareness does not equal deep theme; most enduring short masterpieces draw from mundane life); chasing fads (bandwagon subjects: "having one is worth the same as having a thousand"); lacking dramatic potential (mismatch between material and form); improper scale (compressing multi-act material into a one-act play leaves only a withered skeleton).
- **Four Methods to Employ**: **Choose with the heart**—speak from personal feeling; choose what moves you deeply and lingers for decades (Li Zhun's works always carry "those two baked flatbreads"; Ding Xilin's *Oppression* grew from a friend dying of landlord harassment, yet he shaped it into joint resistance and victory); **Choose with the body**—write what you know (Lao She's "trunk": only with plenty of clothes can you change outfits); historical subjects must tie into living experience; if unfamiliar, go immerse yourself in the cheap inns—"novel on the surface, familiar at the core"; **Choose with the eye**—artistic discovery; Rodin: "The mediocre artist always wears someone else's spectacles"; Flaubert stared at a campfire until he could see how it differed from every other fire; **Choose with dramatic instincts**—select only what can be dramatized: conflict, action, emotion, plus musicality for traditional Chinese theatre.
- **Six-character maxim: Small, simple, and possessing a Dramatic Core**. The incident must be small (borrowing a pair of boots, sending a basket of eggs, breaking up a single quarrel; in *Hitting the Gold Bough*, even the emperor merely steps in to settle a household spat; the character for "sharp" consists of "small" over "large"—a truth in itself); the plot must be simple (**if you cannot lay out the thread in five minutes, it is definitively not a good piece**; Li Yu: "pare away loose ends, establish the master spine"; simple does not equal thin; **you must make simple matters complex**: begging for a watermelon → vine → counting melons → seeds); have a show-stopping trump card.
- Three maxims from foreign authors: I have a good story; I have the passion to tell it to the end; **this story can only be told by me and no one else—the third is the most vital.**

## VIII. Deepening Concept (Lu Jun)

- Goethe: "One of the finest hallmarks of originality lies in taking a chosen subject and developing it to its fullest potential." Deepening concept is like chess: Player A thinks three moves ahead, Player B thinks five. Three revisions of *Women's Representative*: bolster the public hygiene campaign (move one) → stage old vs. new ideology between mother-in-law and daughter-in-law (move two, conventional) → connect family reform with social reform by staging the fight for political and economic rights, where Zhang Guirong produces the land deed (move three). Deepening through adaptation: *Chuncao Barges into the Court* transforms "a righteous man gets two wives" into "the lowliest can shake the feudal order"; *After the Reunion* completely upends the theme of *Shi Tianwen*, which merely preached filial piety.
- **Four Techniques**: 1. **Grand backdrop, small story**—place a small incident against the backdrop of the era (in *The Green Cockatoo*, actors shift from staged murder to real killing, entirely powered by the French Revolution; in *Reassurance Pill*, Changgen's rise from a spineless weakling to a self-respecting man is due to the new policy restoring his dignity); counter-example: *Migrant Workers' Shed* reduces a societal dilemma to personal spite. 2. **Reflect deeply on character**—when inspiration stalls, study a real person with idiosyncratic traits (Liu Bao: poverty forced her to discard pride, prosperity allowed her to reclaim dignity; Xiao Ding: a walking societal comic strip); *Fifteen Strings of Cash* grew by deeply pondering Kuang Zhong, generating the added scene *Suspecting the Rat*. 3. **Study the ending**—"deepening the concept is an ongoing process of interrogating the ending" (three endings for visiting the poor on a rainy night: political fairy tale → leaving the roof repair unassigned → the brick-and-tile magnate declaring "a Party township head cannot merely drop by to console the poor"). 4. **The power of symbolism**—the park bench in *The Zoo Story* = middle-class status.
- **Three Does-Nots**: Does not force unearned elevation (hunting for class struggle in a simple black bench; scoring a bird-fighting finale with the *Yellow River Cantata*); does not preach or illustrate abstract points (Engels: "the tendency must spring forth naturally from the situation and action"; writing a script that boils down to an advertising slogan is mere illustration); does not feign profundity (if the piece stands after stripping away "good guy / bad guy" labels, never paste them on).

## IX. Point of View Must Be Fresh (Lu Jun)

- Overlapping subject matter is not fatal; the five hundred Arhats all display distinct postures because their angles of vision differ. The same unfaithful lover framework: *Sicilian Limes* "squeezes" the betrayer into view solely through the gaze of the abandoned; *The Return* mirrors the abandoned through the eyes of the unfaithful lover, contrasting the family's passionate waiting against Wang Biao's chilling detachment. Elder abuse can be told simply through an old couple picnicking while hiding a chicken leg.
- The charm of angle: painting "an ancient temple hidden deep in the mountains" by showing only a monk drawing water; painting "returning from trotting through flowers, horse hooves smell sweet" by showing only butterflies chasing the hooves—find that oblique cut that captures everything without tackling it head-on.
- Three methods: **Transforming the ordinary into the wondrous** (*My Father*, where parents claim student essays; *Little Darling and Little Ancestor*, which explores single-child upbringing without a single adult taking the stage or a single word of preaching); **Compressing time and space** (*Altruism*, where a crowd reveals their inner lives around a single leap into a river); **Condensing emotion** (*Riders to the Sea*, which shows the family's agonizing vigil rather than the sea voyage; *The Price of Coal*, where the son survives injured yet no one can smile—"Ellen today is Mary tomorrow"; the single chair in *Together Forever*).
- Three angle requirements for short drama: **Keyhole view** (a "girl admiring a qipao" rather than a panoramic expo); **Focusing** (differentiating characters within the same situation through personality: Cui Yingying vs. Zhu Yingtai, Liu Cuiping vs. Wang Baochuan); **Defamiliarization / deformation** (a saint arrested, a living person's obituary, a barber partnering with a nurse—focus the main energy on the social theme, holding back trivial comedy; beware of "digging wherever there is cheap theatricality").

## X. Dramatic Core Must Be Solid (Lu Jun)

- Tolstoy: "The most important thing in a work of art is that it has a focus... this focus can never be fully expressed in words."
- **Dramatic Core**: possesses reproductive vitality in plot, explosive counter-force in conflict, and penetrating depth in thematic substance—**without it, there is no play; without it, it cannot become *this specific* play** (in *Three Dollars National Currency*, the university student deliberately smashes another vase and pays three dollars according to the wealthy lady's own logic).
- The Dramatic Core is the root; the **dramatic nerve center (vital organ)** is the branch (a prop or pivotal plot turn carrying special weight: the peach blossom fan, Chuncao altering the letter); the **dramatic focal point (the eye)** is the leaf (a pivotal character or arresting set-piece: the prefect yielding his sedan chair). The test: strip it away and see if the play still exists—if it does, it was merely branch or leaf.
- Four modes of presentation: a singular action (building an ancestral shrine for a "dead son"; a couple setting mutual infidelity traps; a love letter penned by three elementary schoolers); a carefully chosen prop (a magic chair that forces truth-telling; a gong mallet; a single penny); a twist of human fortune (stealing money for a five-dollar stroller); a song or a line of dialogue (the ballad in *The Rising of the Moon*; "those typewriters").
- Once the Dramatic Core is fixed, theme (*Infanticide* generates its contrast from this: two children, one dying surrounded by care, the other smothered by kin, stemming from the identical root cause), structure (mistaken death → inheritance dispute → revival), and character (a quack writing obituaries for the living) fall into place.
- Three sources: everyday detail (a husband fined; paying off a street vendor's debt); character psychology (a master stealing his servant's cigarettes); imaginative constructs (taking a badger to court; a pound of flesh).

## XI. Conflict Must Be Genuine, Plot Must Be Striking (Lu Jun)

- **Three layers of genuine conflict**: authentic (not fabricated), meaningful (not pointless), dramatic (not rhetorical). Eight structural ailments self-check: lacking social significance; fabricated (a soldier breaking an engagement without cause, a mother-in-law refusing to recognize a daughter-in-law); inaccurate (donating bonus money, a foreign merchant "testing" a factory director); lacking dramatic character (a battle of viewpoints rather than personality—"people with identical viewpoints can still clash," Zhou Yu vs. Zhuge Liang); arbitrary misunderstandings; dodging the hard issue (a fresh conflict detouring into worn tropes halfway through); feigned conflict; lacking dramatic tension. Belinsky: only when both disputants strive for the upper hand, wound a specific aspect of the other's character, and produce a new relationship as a result do you have drama.
- **Plot must be striking, but must first be authentic**: "In a one-act play, one should write about preposterous things" (Chekhov); Hu Ke: "What audiences want to see is a hundred-year-old taking command, not a hundred-year-old in a nursing home; Wu Song beating a tiger, not beating a dog"; abandon striking elements that lack social meaning (reaching out of a coffin to check for rain). When the plot lacks authenticity, prop up the characters first (four revisions of *The Honorary Wife*: establish solid motivation for the marriage, provide plausible backstory and carnal desire).
- **Finding the extraordinary within the ordinary is fundamental craft**: refining "borrowing boots" into "borrowing, boasting, taking, offering, testing," full of twists and turns; choose rich theatrical flavor—like medicinal herb names concealing secret messages—over flat mediocrity that merely conforms to dry rules: "meaning is the raw grain, prose is cooking it into rice, poetry is fermenting it into wine."
- Five Shakespearean methods for adapting source material: elevate secondary characters' intellect; reposition the antagonist; sharpen the protagonist's wit while stripping away passive submissiveness; patch plausibility holes while sketching character; re-engineer the ending to line up all characters for a swift, decisive curtain.

## XII. From Idea to Premise: Workflow

1. **Capture the flash of inspiration** and write down "why do I care" (Cron); confirm that it is a story "only I can tell" (Lu Jun).
2. **Run through the four methods and four pitfalls of subject selection**: Is it born from personal feeling? Is it familiar or tied to personal experience? Have you spotted an angle no one else has seen? Can it be dramatized? Is the incident small enough, the plot simple enough?
3. **Draft a one-sentence theme** (cliches are fine) → deduce the **what-if question** (including context, surprise, conflict, and subtext).
4. **Establish the premise** (Egri's tripartite formula: character trait + leads to + ending); confirm you believe in it; only one premise per play.
5. **Construct the Third Rail**: What does the protagonist desire (concrete and visual), why, and what misbelief stands in the way? Write the Origin Scene and three turning scenes.
6. **Answer Hicks' five questions** and Hoxter's three questions (What do they want? What do they fight against? What is the thematic keyword?); What attitude do the protagonist and antagonist each take toward the theme? Map out the attitudes of four relationship characters.
7. **Deepen the concept by thinking two moves ahead**: list three conceptual levels for the same material and pick the highest; employ grand backdrop/small story, study character, interrogate the ending, or use symbolism.
8. **Locate the Dramatic Core**: If you strip it away, does the play still stand? Is it an action, a prop, a predicament, or a line of dialogue?
9. **Determine the point of view**: Tackle it head-on or squeeze it from an angle? Which approach: keyhole, focus, or deformation?
10. **Write the climax / epiphany moment**, reverse-engineer the Controlling Idea (value + cause) from the climax, and label it Idealistic, Pessimistic, or Ironic; design alternating sequences of Idea vs. Counter-Idea.
11. **Draft the one-sentence story** (Snyder's four elements + adjectives + primal goal) and **logline** (protagonist + Inciting Incident + challenge + stakes + theme, highlighting the hook); pitch it out loud to a stranger.
12. **Self-check**: Run through the eight conflict ailments; verify against the three Does-Nots; are two premises pulling in two directions; does the ending stem from character action rather than accident; does the thematic keyword stay fixed while attitudes transform across the script?

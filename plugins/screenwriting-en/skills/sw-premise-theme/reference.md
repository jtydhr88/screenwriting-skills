## I. Egri's Premise Checklist and Classic Play Premises

**Classic Play Premises**: Romeo and Juliet = Great love defies even death; King Lear = Blind trust leads to destruction; Macbeth = Ruthless ambition leads to its own destruction; Othello = Jealousy destroys itself and the object of its love; Ghosts = The sins of the fathers are visited upon the children; A Doll's House = Inequality in marriage breeds unhappiness; Dead End = Poverty breeds crime; Sweet Bird of Youth = Callous ambition leads to ruin; Juno and the Paycock = Lack of purpose leads to ruin (though the son's death is unrelated to the premise, causing act two to stall); Shadow and Substance = Faith conquers pride (actually two premises); Tartuffe = He who digs a pit for others falls into it himself; The Necklace = Escaping reality exacts a bitter price; A Lion Is in the Streets = Ruthless ambition leads to self-destruction; The Pride of the Marines = The dedication of love overcomes despair; Earth and High Heaven = Narrow-mindedness breeds isolation.

**Checklist of Usable Premises** (what is missing is your conviction): Misfortune leads to easy contentment / Foolish generosity leads to poverty / Honesty defeats deception / Haste destroys friendship / Bad temper leads to isolation / Boasting leads to self-humiliation / Cunning digs its own grave / Dissipation leads to self-destruction / Egocentricity leads to alienation / Frugality leads to waste.

**Deduction Examples**
- Crime drama: A perfect crime bores a friend -> lacks premise -> character's "sole motive is your command" -> reveal the why (the process of a daughter killing her mother) -> pick ambition as murder motive -> why is the victim unyielding? -> ruthless ambition carries the seed of self-destruction -> *Macbeth*.
- Juvenile robbery: Why rob -> alcoholic father -> Great Depression -> "poverty breeds crime" -> Johnny is 16, personal premise "as long as you are clever enough you can get away with anything" vs. the law's "long arm of justice"; seeing only roughness without causes mistakenly yields "lack of policing breeds crime" - one must explain why a millionaire's son does not steal bread.
- Aunt Clara: Dissipation -> rival's suicide -> husband departs -> religious fanaticism -> meddling in others' lives -> "extreme behavior leads to destruction"; the climax must stem from her own actions, not an accident.
- Origins of jealousy (frivolity / inferiority / acquaintances / boredom / lovers / selling oneself to save a husband / misunderstanding) each correspond to a different premise; "there can only be one driving force generating jealousy."
- *Tartuffe* first third: Madame Pernelle "if he ruled the house it would benefit everyone" (first hint of entrusting the estate); Dorine "out of his jealousy toward Madame" (foreshadowing); Orgon "And Tartuffe? - Managed two partridges and half a leg of mutton - Poor man!" across four rounds.

## III. Cron: The Construction Process of the Ruby Case

- **Spark**: Jenny's "woman who hates dogs." **Why care**: Love and death are separated by a razor's edge. **Theme**: "Love is worth everything; better to have loved and lost than never to have loved at all" (cliches are fine).
- **Hypothetical Question in Three Drafts**: Abstract -> add dog -> "A woman who considers herself successful by keeping love at arm's length stands on the verge of losing everything... steals a dog for an hour or two to prove her mental stability... unable to part with the dog, she is forced to accept that the very human connection she avoided is the only thing that can withstand loss."
- **Who**: Ruby is a television writer on "The Silent Partner," Henry's creative partner of ten years, deeply in love but refusing to cohabit or marry, "believing herself free from the risk of heartbreak."
- **Why**: Want - to write alongside Henry day in and day out; Why - a "hall pass" to the room of romantic love; Misbelief - sincere intimacy will destroy you (eroding the self -> vulnerable -> power imbalance).
- **Origin Scene** (after six drafts): Her best friend Beth's father passes away; at a soccer match, Beth falls to her knees before an empty chair weeping inconsolably -> Ruby concludes, "Thank God I will never feel such grief"; spaghetti night, "Ruby is writing a play," imagining her own father dying with "a trace of relief," begging Beth to play soccer, "Please."
- **Three Turning-Point Scenes**: Age 13 skating rink - hanging out with boys to win Beth back, genuinely liking a boy and being kissed, "suddenly feeling for no reason that she had lost something" -> breaks away; meeting Henry and falling in love -> realizing dependency -> convincing herself it is not love -> breaking up; proposal after ten years as partners -> reluctant yes -> falling ill as the wedding approaches -> returning to the past, "proving that one need not commit completely to reap love."
- **Primary External Dilemma** (passing three external filters and two internal questions): Henry takes a solo feature assignment and announces the final season -> producer demands a rewritten finale within ten days -> an argument -> Henry's bike crash leaves his fate hanging -> Ruby faces the deadline alone. Stealing the dog cannot be included - that is plot, not story.
- **Ticking Clock Tracking**: Collaborative split -> studio demands rewrite -> argument and crash -> under a week -> fan script as backup -> **The Sixth Tick**: Nora, the veterinarian sister, forces Ruby to move in together -> decides to "borrow" a dog.
- **Scene Card One**: Alpha Point = Ruby is about to start writing the script; What Happens = Sharon's phone call + Nora's visit; Consequence = complete the rewrite, but appease Nora first; Significance = after losing her greatest love, the only thing she can control is the final say over the show; Realization = the biggest obstacle is Nora; Therefore -> find a way to divert Nora's attention.
- **Epiphany Moment**: Celebrity dog Rufus and owner Tony; "People love dogs because dogs cannot hurt you" "They will die one day"; Ruby devises an ending for Tony; "I dodged the pain, but sacrificed the joy" -> let Juliet live.
- Threefold Test of the Plot Point (What right does Nora have to kick Ruby out -> Occam's razor: Nora owns the house; from internal contradiction: when their parents died, Nora wanted to rebuild connection, and giving Ruby the house to live in was her only familial warmth).

## IV. Henson's Premise Statement Template and Examples

- Theme (one emotional noun + <= 2 qualifiers) -> Basic Action (present continuous transitive verb) + Purpose Sentence (Protagonist name + wants to do what + despite Opponent name + opposition).
- *Hamlet*: Justice -> Murdering, Hamlet wants to kill Claudius; *E.T.*: Separation -> Seeking help, E.T. wants a boy to help him get home; *My Fair Lady*: Acceptance -> Teaching, Higgins wants to teach Eliza proper speech to gain societal acceptance; *Citizen Kane*: Success -> Investigating, a reporter wants to find the meaning of "Rosebud" despite Kane leaving no clues at death; *Sleepless in Seattle*: A woman dies; father-son estrangement; a child writes to a radio station to find a partner for his father; the father refuses to meet the responding woman; the child persuades the father to meet.
- Avoid: Two actions (murdering -> killing); intransitive/stative verbs ("Eliza wants to become happy"); unidirectional verbs ("learning"); template blanks: "The emotion prompting (protagonist) to act is (theme)" "The need for (theme) drives (protagonist) to (transitive verb)" "(Protagonist) wants (desire), despite (opponent) (opposing)."
- Dramatic Premise in Three Sentences (Disturbing Incident / Act I Hook / Act II Hook, each with a core question): *Hamlet* encounters father's ghost who names the uncle as killer (will he kill the king?); hires actors to stage murder before the king (same question); Claudius feels guilt and Hamlet readies his revenge -> yes. *City of Angels* Seth falls for Maggie (can they be together?); appears before her and is rejected; learns he can fall to Earth -> no. *Scent of a Woman* student cares for blind officer (can he help him face reality?); wild journey; student faces trouble prompting the officer to help him -> yes.

## V. Hoxter's Theme Proving Ground

Test the same material (young Jimmy, a widowed father "taken away" by a spaceship captain, actually a romance) through different themes: Coming of Age / Love (captain's niece teaches space dating) / Sacrifice / Obsession / Family (alien crew = new family) / Friendship (a father can also be a friend) / Revenge (family are refugees) / Justice (bringing to justice) - see which theme brings the characters most to life. Four-way dynamic: naive vs. cynical friends plus their respective lovers "embodies the values of the entire script."

Logline formula: "His partner killed by a vicious alien (Inciting Incident), a tenacious Brooklyn beat cop Lauren Brown (protagonist) must track down the alien homeworld (challenge) to avenge her partner (theme)"; *Winter's Bone* "An unflinching teenage girl (protagonist), facing violent retribution (stakes), breaks her drug-dealing clan's code of silence (conflict) to find her missing father (challenge) and save her family home (theme)" + a second sentence establishing character; *Alien* ensemble protagonist + Inciting Incident + conflict + antagonist, hook = haunted house in a spaceship.

## VI. Bork: Eight Story Problems and Eight Levels of Stakes

**Eight Story Problems**: 1. I (we) face mortal danger 2. My life will likely be ruined 3. A once-in-a-lifetime opportunity to permanently transform self-worth 4. Must rescue someone from a horrifying doom 5. Must achieve a distant, life-altering, seemingly impossible goal 6. Must defeat a bully who harms and threatens the innocent 7. Must break free from a terrible condition that prevents freedom and happiness 8. Must win the ideal partner against overwhelming odds.

**Stakes from Highest to Lowest**: Life (humanity or individual) -> Upholding justice and preventing disaster -> Freedom and democracy (escape/release) -> Preserving family and personal life (returning home / threatened way of life) -> Uniting with the ideal partner (massive external obstacles resolved only at the end) -> Better career and prospects (must place everything at risk while remaining morally superior yet disadvantaged) -> Life-changing prize (only the underlying meaning matters) -> A chance at happiness (must be concretized).

**Sub-par Stakes**: An achievement that does not alter destiny; success in job or career; learning something new or changing an inner attitude; generic military conflict; joy and health issues lacking concrete massive change; isolated "must make a decision" moments; an unsympathetic person evolving into a good one.

**Three Elements of a Logline**: Who the protagonist is and connection / Catalyst event / Nature, mission, difficulty, and importance of the challenge. Example *Schindler's List*: A cunning industrialist amasses a war fortune but is horrified by the plight of Jewish people, employing them against an amiable yet sadistic Nazi commander.

## VII. Snyder: Loglines and Genres

- The Ironic Hook: "A cop comes to L.A. to visit his estranged wife and her office building is taken over by terrorists"; "A businessman falls in love with a prostitute he hired to pose as his girlfriend"; "She is the perfect woman - until she has a drink."
- Enhanced Version: "A mild-mannered teacher... an overprotective cop brother-in-law... a journey through hell."
- Titles: *Legally Blonde* is great, *For Love or Money* is terrible; *Nuclear Family* sold for 1 million dollars on the pun.
- Six Creative Games: Comedic Version / Serious Version / Fish-out-of-Water FBI / ___ School / Versus!!! / My ___ Is a Serial Killer.
- Quick Reference to the Ten Genres: Monster in the House (monster + enclosed space + a sin); Golden Fleece (journey seeking X only to find oneself); Out of the Bottle (Cinderella gains magic -> learns to be ordinary); Dude with a Problem (ordinary person + extraordinary situation); Rites of Passage (the assailant is invisible "life"); Buddy Love (a love story in disguise); Whodunit (the focus is why); Fool Triumphant (an underdog against the system); Institutionalized (group story, one breakout individual); Superhero (extraordinary person in an ordinary world, give them pain).
- Hollywood's Secret: *Point Break* = *The Fast and the Furious*; *The Matrix* = *Monsters, Inc.*; *Who Framed Roger Rabbit* = *Chinatown*.

## VIII. Lu Jun: Theme Deepening and Dramatic Core Examples

**Three Revisions of *Women's Representative***: Intensifying midwife reform to depict the hygiene campaign (too narrow) -> emphasizing mother-in-law and daughter-in-law clash over old vs. new ideology (stale, Mother Wang turns into the mother-in-law from *The Virtuous Sister-in-Law*) -> linking domestic transformation to social transformation: Zhang Guirong pulls out the land deeds: "Out of two deeds, one is mine; out of three rooms, one end is mine... I take my deed, and if I cannot live in the house, I will tear it down"; Aunt Niu goes to the district to learn modern midwifery and open a clinic, shifting to Zhang's side only after tasting its benefits.

**Three Endings for A Rainy Night Visit to the Poor**: 1. Granddaughter begs a stranger to impersonate the township head -> grandfather discovers the truth -> the thatched hut stops leaking, as the new county head leads the township head and village chief on the roof plugging leaks (political fairy tale); 2. Grandfather learns the truth and blames only his own old age; the hut stops leaking without explaining who fixed it; 3. The woman in the raincoat is a brick-and-tile magnate, and the township head is riding in her car en route to handle disaster relief at the brick factory - "A Communist township head cannot merely know how to visit the poor and comfort the suffering, nor should it forever follow a single template"; in the end, the township head still does not arrive.

**Sources of Dramatic Core**: Daily details (a husband's fine; a Hangzhou aunt paying a debt for a street vendor); mysteries of character psychology (*The Air of Beijing*, a master stealing his servant's cigarettes); imaginary worlds (*The Badger's Trial*, bringing a badger to court; *The Merchant of Venice*, a pound of flesh).

**Four Revisions of *Honorary Wife*** (when the plot feels unreal, steady the characters first): Provide sufficient motivation for marriage (mother critically ill; uncle "buys" a poor girl from the countryside; knowing of an injury but not that it is fatal; honest confession on wedding night pleading for temporary endurance); Xiao had a lover in the countryside and gave herself to him on their farewell night, becoming pregnant after marriage; Yinghu urges divorce while mother and uncle are utterly baffled; Yinghu collapses, takes his own life by poison, and entrusts his wife on his deathbed; Xiao resolves to stay by her mother-in-law to hold the home together.

**Eight Conflict Case Studies**: *Golden Brick Turmoil* lacks social meaning; the "injured soldier renounces engagement" formula is false; *Acknowledging the Mother-in-Law* feels like "swallowing a decrepit fly"; foreign investor "testing" the factory director is inaccurate; *Old Lovers* spins its wheels over a three-hundred-line argument of viewpoints; *Red Apricot* and *How Much Yearning* rely on homophones/misjudgments/associative misunderstandings; *The Antelope Carving* should have gifted the carving to Person A's father to please everyone (evading the hard truth); a demobilized soldier walks away and returns out of guilt (a feint); *Bridal Chamber Night* pivots conflict direction mid-stride (lacks tension).

# Scene Craft (场景与段落)

The scene is "the smallest expressive unit of drama" (Lu Jun) and a "story in miniature" (McKee). This skill handles all decisions from location selection to the blank page for a single scene: **why here and now, what value is at stake, who wants what through what tactic, how beats build to a turning point, how props and details do their dramatic work, how suspense is planted and delayed, and how to enter and exit the scene**. For case analyses and toolkits, see [reference.md](reference.md); for the dialogue layer, see `sw-dialogue`; for full-script architecture, see `sw-story-structure`.

---

## 1. What a Scene Is

- **McKee's definition**: An action through conflict in more or less continuous time and space that turns the value-charged condition of a character's life on at least one perceptible level. **No scene is without a turn**: note the value polarity at the beginning and again at the end; if identical, ask "Why is this scene here?"—almost always an exposition dump → cut it and weave the information elsewhere. A lovers' breakup scene moving across four locations (bedroom/kitchen/garage/highway) remains a single scene (tested by whether it can be written as "one unified whole").
- **Mei Feng's value test**: The only thing to establish when staging a scene is **what value is laid on the table in this scene, and whether by the end it has flipped or gained a new layer**; subplot values can be braided in, but both polarities of the primary and secondary values across the script must be rendered accurately. Change occurs only through conflict; conflict occurs only through character choice. The restaurant scene in *The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance*: the identical value pair (civilization vs. savagery) circulates multiple times within a single scene—law book / frontier code / literacy / black suit / trip-up / drawn gun / picking up the steak / hurling the whiskey bottle.
- **Field**: A scene is "a place where something happens," built on two elements: place and time; changing place or time creates a new scene (costing roughly $10,000 per screen minute); first identify the **purpose** (the single piece of information driving the story forward), then determine place and time, then find the **components within the scene** (the waiter in the diner, neighboring tables, an old acquaintance).
- **Hicks**: Every scene must accomplish two things simultaneously—give the reader new information to push the plot forward; reveal more about the protagonist. Scenes where nothing happens (shaving in a hotel room) must be cut.
- **Henson**: A scene = a complete dramatic action occurring in a specific place, unified in time, objective, and character; triggers for scene changes: a shift in place or time (most frequent), adding or subtracting characters, or changing the dramatic objective.
- **Lu Jun**: A scene is far more than two lines of text declaring "time and place"—"Why does the story take place here and now rather than there and then?" Lao She: "To portray a company cook, placing him merely in a kitchen boiling water and cooking rice makes dramatic spark hard to come by; station him laying track in the great desert or in the heat of fierce combat right when everyone desperately needs water and food, and his role expands immensely."

## 2. Five-Step Scene Design (McKee)

1. **Identify conflict**: Who drives it, what do they want (expressed as an objective statement); what does the opposing force want, which must stand in direct opposition rather than at a tangent.
2. **Note the opening value** polarity (+/-).
3. **Break into beats**: A beat = a unit of action/reaction; label subtextual action and reaction using emotionally colored present-continuous phrases ("prostrating himself at her feet" / "ignoring her pleas"); only an overt shift in behavior constitutes a new beat.
4. **Note the closing value** and compare (identical = a non-event).
5. **Examine beats** for the arc and turning point: each beat stacks on the preceding one until the value charge shifts at the turning point; turning points can be created only by **action or revelation**, yielding a fourfold effect: surprise → increased curiosity → insight → new direction.

Supplements:
- **Scene objective and super-objective**: What each character wants right now (the scene ends once the scene objective is met) + the unconscious super-objective (often contradicting outer characterization); background desires (the web of constraints) limit what may and may not be said.
- **Five steps of behavior**: Desire → sensing opposition → choosing an action → action → expression. "The scene does not reside in the act of speaking, but in the action the character executes through speaking."
- **The earliest symptom of a poorly written scene is excessive beat repetition** (the same tactic in different words).
- **Four flaws of clumsy scenes**: Desires are not in opposition, causing a lack of conflict; circular repetition with zero progression; a turning point placed too early or too late, breaking balance; on-the-nose writing that strips credibility. Fixes: test beats against the scene objective; alter behavior to fit desire, or alter desire to fit behavior.
- Six conflict paradigms require distinct approaches (balanced, comic, asymmetric, indirect, reflexive, minimal), detailed in `sw-dialogue`.
- **Hoxter**: A scene beat = a moment of dramatic progression or shifting center of gravity (*Bonnie and Clyde* guessing professions → "I just got out of state prison"); every major character has an objective, and the actor selects a tactic (flirt, threaten, coax, reason); polarity reversal; John August's six questions and four drills: What needs to happen in this scene? What happens if it's cut? Who needs to be present? Where could it take place? What is the most surprising thing that could happen? Long or short? → brainstorm three openings → rehearse in the mind's eye → rough draft → full scene.

## 3. Entrances, Exits, and Pacing

- **Enter late, leave early**: William Goldman's maxim: enter the scene at the latest possible moment; *Chinatown* peroxide in the bathroom → cut directly to the bed post-coitus (Field). Hicks: **Leave the scene before the conflict is completely resolved**—the sense of lingering irresolution generates tension (*Little House*: "I came all this way and all I get is a raccoon!"); enter right as the scene ignites, cut loose as it nears its close, leaping with energy into the next scene. Walter's "start late, end early" applies to scenes and individual lines of dialogue alike (in a restaurant, cut straight to the entrée).
- **Rhythm and tempo** (McKee): 40–60 scenes averaging 2.5 minutes; pair a one-minute scene with a four-minute scene; a camera drains a location's expressive power within two to three minutes; *12 Angry Men* relies on over 80 French scenes (a major actor's entrance or exit creates a new scene); shorten scenes accelerating toward an act climax to **earn the right to pause**; scenes of major reversal are themselves long, measured, and taut.
- **Pace** = the level of activity within a scene (pillow-talk whispers vs. a street riot).
- **Principles of transition**: The third element linking two scenes = commonality or contrast—character traits (mischievous child → childish adult), action (foreplay → afterglow), object (greenhouse → jungle), a single spoken line, light texture (dawn shadow → sunset glow), sound (ocean waves → snoring), idea (blank canvas → dying old man).
- **Sequence**: Field's "series of scenes tied together with a single idea," forming the backbone of a screenplay (wedding, chase, homecoming, bank heist); first establish the sequence's idea (the cup), then pour in the content (the coffee). McKee: 2–5 scenes escalating in impact to a peak; assign a title to every sequence. Hoxter: A sequence = a group of scenes fulfilling one or more story goals, unified by time/place; decide which beat to weigh down (*Winter's Bone* argument takes the longest); two methods for rhythm: controlling scene duration (when to enter and exit); dividing sequences by beat.
- Mei Feng: Between two major dramatic scenes, insert a short scene featuring Peabody—lacking conflict, slowing tempo, separating heavyweight set-pieces, resolving time gaps—allowing the entire film to breathe and alternate between tension and ease.
- Henson: Action paragraphs resemble punchy lists rather than descriptive essays; incorporate the "given circumstances" (time, season, weather, environmental conditions shaping action, physical details offering clues)—"early morning, pale blue sky, sweltering heat waves rising, Stars and Stripes fluttering over the bleachers" = Fourth of July; alter it to "slate-gray sky, fresh snowfall" = Veterans Day; "changing a single image can alter the entire story."

## 4. Action Over Dialogue (Walter / Henson / Hicks)

- **Principle 30: Drama is physical behavior and action**; **Principle 31: Any action is better than inaction; an action seamlessly integrated with the story is the best action**. Tennessee Williams: "Have them stand on their heads, have them take off their pants, have them do something—anything at all, but never let them do nothing."
- Cinema's greatest gift is magnifying the very small: *Loves of a Blonde* wedding ring slips and rolls across the floor, the camera crawling under tables past rows of legs, grabbing the ring and standing up only to find the character stranded among a sea of women; *Kramer vs. Kramer* French toast (the first time looks like being lost in the Borneo jungle; by the end, pans and spatulas are wielded like an executive chef); *The Rose Tattoo* wringing stockings; *In Treatment* handmade hot pad; *Inglourious Basterds* fountain pen drawing up ink. "Tiny, mundane, everyday actions magnify the inner weight of cinema."
- **Static setups**: Restaurants, bars, coffee shops, cars, and dinner tables are best avoided unless inherently indispensable—phones (*Atlantic City* hunting for a package in a phone booth; *Hook* pitching the phone out the window), cars (*Close Encounters of the Third Kind* gesturing toward a "car" passing behind), restaurants (*Big Night* refusing to cook two starches = a metaphor for the clash between commerce and art), offices (police detective confronting his superior → brainstorm a dozen alternative locations → swimming pool: the superior swims laps under doctor's orders, the detective shouts along the pool deck, chlorinated water splashing across $600 alligator shoes). **Generate a dozen alternative locations for every scene before choosing the one that expands the plot**; "give actors something to do and bind story to character through physical detail."
- Hicks: Write action paragraphs with causal logic, clear beginnings, middles, and ends, giving the reader a **visceral sense of rhythm** rather than transcribing frame-by-frame shot orders (the snowmobile chase counter-example); sex, fights, and chases are the hardest to write and easiest to turn cliché, yet the writer must still supply a scene outline to sustain context (the lighthouse staircase fight sequence); keep setup descriptions minimal for phone, dining, and driving scenes.
- Henson's "story-doing": Turn passive state into physical action—"I feel miserable" → "I burst into tears" → son offers comfort or mocks; "Mary, you are so rebellious" → Mary hires an assassin to murder Elizabeth I; when a script stalls = character is trapped in an interior state instead of an action; use the "I want..." exercise to break the impasse; write reactions counter to expectation (Indiana Jones pulling a gun on the scimitar swordsman; Dunbar attempting suicide upon learning of amputation; Butch and Sundance leaping off the cliff).
- Counterpoint method (Field): Smile gently when playing rage; talk about flowers while falling in love; pitch romance in the presence of a shark; frame the decision to rob a bank inside a pool hall as a cop walks in and circles around. Comedy = build a situation where characters react with dead seriousness.
- McKee: The best advice on dialogue is not to write it; for every scene, ask first: "How could this be told purely visually?"

## 5. Richness of Wit: Suspense, Delay, and Comic Invention (Lu Jun)

- **Dramatic wit = fresh, curious, charming, and clever**, realized as **possessing suspense and dramatic charm** (charm also implies the anticipation of charm). Li Yu: "Wit and charm are indispensable to the playwright."
- **Planting and dismantling suspense**: Resolve one suspense point while introducing another in a continuous chain (*The Foolish Son-in-Law Sells Cloth* links seven suspense beats seamlessly); if starting with a massive mystery from page one (murder investigation), the climax must be striking enough to outweigh the opening hook; misdirect the audience at the start, hurling successive enigmas before pulling off the grand reveal (*The Unshakeable Daughter-in-Law*).
- **Revealing the answer vs. guarding the secret**: Chinese traditional drama routinely hands the master key to the audience (*Fifteen Strings of Cash* identifies the villain Lou Ashu at the outset; *Nine Copper Coins* yields the secret in three lines upfront); **William Archer: A playwright should never keep secrets from his audience—you cannot truly keep them anyway**; sheer curiosity is "a one-night delicacy," while drama's enduring pleasure lies in **foreknowledge**—the audience watching mortals fumble blindly like gods looking down. *The Women Generals of the Yang Family: Birthday Banquet* offers two paths: hide the tragedy from both characters and audience (the momentary effect of surprise) vs. let the audience, Mu Guiying, and Princess Chai learn the grim news before the banquet, forcing them to paste on smiling faces (the magnetic pull of true dramatic interest, infinitely more moving).
- **Suspense of character and destiny**: True master suspense lies in character and fate—handing the audience the secret of a father poisoning the food from frame one does not kill suspense (two bowls of untouched egg fried rice with resting chopsticks); short drama does not chase sweeping outward spectacle, but polishes local hinges to a brilliant sheen.
- **Delaying for release**: Crouch before leaping high, pull back the fist before punching; *A Night at an Inn* ratchets dread to its boiling point before the silent, looming idol appears "visible in form yet making no sound," only then delaying further by calling names one by one; *Mr. Leonidas Meets the Reactionaries* stacks three tiers of escalation to widen the chasm between panic and false alarm—"aesthetic satisfaction comes only when tension and seriousness peak, only to reveal the terror was nothing of the kind."
- **Four wellsprings of dramatic charm**: Inventive situations (Molière's formula: conflict introduced point-blank → humiliated party plots → target of satire takes the stage to perform → full-throated skewering → plotter pulls the rug); inventive characters (*Xiao Jiu and Lao Le* fearing one's wife is framed as "fear born of love," confused in small matters but razor-sharp in big ones, using a character hinge to make personality flare up three times); inventive language ("I told her to halt, and she—you can go ahead and walk as you please"; the two philosophers in *The Forced Marriage*); inventive details (*Gold Plating* stacks pretentious posturing across four layers: before meeting, after meeting, upon a newcomer's arrival, and backstage).
- McKee's interest = curiosity (intellectual) + concern (emotional, center of good); mystery / suspense / dramatic irony as three audience-character dynamics (three ways to write the man with the axe in the hallway); shun cheap shock and false mystery.

## 6. Exquisite Detail (Lu Jun)

- Detail = the smallest structural unit used to portray character, event, and environment; "detail decides victory or defeat" (three steps at a subway exit, Admiral Togo spotting laundry hung to dry on warship gun barrels); "the story may be invented, but details cannot be faked" (sparrows hopping across two rows of roof tiles, turkeys appearing in an anachronistic era).
- **Person A vs. Person B**: Person A says Jin Xiang is "putting on airs to conceal poverty," producing a generic, humorless impression; Person B says that after dining, he sits by the main gate picking his teeth with a matchstick because "his teeth are jammed with cured ham," wears a ten-yuan note clipped to his belt, and buys half a salted pig's head, parading it from the east side of the village to the west—**Person A uses concepts to express commonality; Person B uses concrete detail to delineate individuality**.
- Case examples: Ah Q signing his confession "drawing a circle shaped like a melon seed"; Hong Chengchou brushing off a spiderweb and smoothing his robes (betraying an underlying will to live); the young woman playing hopscotch under the setting sun in *A Legal Marriage* (revealing psychology / expressing theme / advancing the relationship); swatting moths at night in *The Overcoat*; Master Yan on his deathbed refusing to expire over two lit wicks; eight physical details tracing the daughter's return from military training in *Changing Melodies*.
- **Five functions of detail in *Striking the Brass Gong***: Setting the environment (entering carrying the gong with three strikes and four chanted lines), driving conflict (catching the duck), etching character (flattering Cai Jiu to swindle his mallet → producing a second mallet like a magic trick), triggering the climax (killing the duck to inspect the grain in its crop), highlighting the theme (bequeathing the mallet with parting words).
- **Five requirements**: Truthfulness; "flesh-close" relevance (fitting this exact piece, character, and scene); sense of weight and priority (favor details stamped with historical birthmarks and social imprints: a steamed bun soaked in human blood, a 620-yuan unpaid bill, an unexploded firecracker from the Criticize Lin Biao and Confucius campaign, searching for keys to slaughter an ox in celebration); **avoiding showiness** (saying "what a waste of expression" once is enough, seven or eight times turns stomach-turning; passing a "rusty sword" around in a full circle is pure indulgence; a prince's fine breeches make a great detail, but stopping the scene to lecture on their origin is fatal; Mother Snow paired with five minutes of superfluous dancing—**the sole test of necessary repetition is whether it drives dramatic progression, deepens conflict, and sharpens character**: a sack of rice appearing three times is necessary); **visuality** (tossing off the phrase "kill the duck to check the crop" pales before gripping a cleaver and slicing open the belly).
- There is only one source: observing life like a teahouse waiter reading chopsticks ("You thumped both chopsticks against your chest to align them before eating; that's how I knew you were a poor working man").
- Walter's "Conk": Shun "a small Midwestern town," write "Springfield, Ohio"; shun "wearing expensive clothes," write "a double-breasted chocolate-brown Armani suit"; shun "engaged in various activities," write "gluing popsicle sticks together and finger painting." McKee: concrete nouns, active verbs, banishing "is/are" throughout.

## 7. Precise Props (Lu Jun)

- A prop is an intimate object of emotional exchange (a paper fan, handkerchief, key, or button exchanged during matchmaking); "a single well-chosen prop often outweighs a torrent of dry, flat words"; precision matters, not quantity (Xu Xian's umbrella, Othello's handkerchief).
- **Six dramatic functions**: (1) Structural core (*Chasing the Eggs* with its two eggs, *A Single Penny*, the scarecrow in *Disciplining the Scarecrow*, *The Locked Chest*—where the wife flings the key to the floor rather than placing it in his palm, the promissory note in *A Doll's House*); (2) Catalyst for conflict (donkey-hide gelatin driving conflict one step forward with every handoff; the handkerchief passed like a relay baton); (3) Character delineation (Zhou Puyuan checking the grandfather clock against his pocket watch; *Pi Jiu and La Zi* using a softshell turtle to kill two birds with one stone; two mallets in *Striking the Brass Gong*; three wooden staves in *The Orphan of Zhao* representing being caught between two loyalties); (4) Symbol of soul and destiny (the magic chair: who gets seated + what they confess once seated; *The Arhat Coin*; a prize-winning savings bond); (5) Thematic window (a cradle, a calming pill, a nanmu coffin, a door panel scrawled with political slogans, an erratic alarm clock); (6) Vehicle for music and movement (a stone mill, an iron wok).
- **Six techniques**: Magnification (a foot-warmer sized like a giant cauldron), miniaturization (a shop signboard shrunken to the size of a playing card), repetition (a paper chain formed by pasting letters of introduction together, a dozen stools stacked with chicken heads and feet), distortion (jumping into a clay vat and waddling while hoisting it), virtualization (Mother Snow, a stone bodhisattva), living surrogates / animate props (actors playing bailiff ghosts chanting inner monologues). "Drama, in the end, is theater"—"A bright moon shines over Suzhou and other places" may be scientifically factual, but where is the poetry?
- Indick: The protagonist's weapon = an emblem of identity (lightsaber, Excalibur, or an automobile, boat, computer); let it play a decisive role at the climax. Campbell: Symbolic motifs (handkerchiefs, statuettes) recurring without descending into overuse. McKee: Image systems—a category of imagery recurring with subtle variation from beginning to end, which must remain subliminal (*Diabolique*'s water; *Chinatown*'s blindness / water / corruption); once consciously flagged, it degrades into neutral curiosity. Snyder's "running gag / reprise" (the flyer in *Back to the Future*); recurring actions must reverse polarity in the second half.

## 8. Appropriate Settings (Lu Jun)

- **Eugene O'Neill's calculated design**: *Beyond the Horizon* alternates outdoor scenes (horizon = desire, dreams) and indoor scenes (horizon vanishing) between acts to forge rhythm; *Desire Under the Elms* features two elms "like two exhausted women resting their sagging breasts, hands, and hair upon the roof"; *The Emperor Jones* pairs forest and drumbeat (the drum escalating from resting pulse rate to frantic frenzy, hallucination solidifying into physical scenes); *Long Day's Journey into Night* tracks the fog outside from brilliant sunlight to a wall of blinding murk.
- **Chekhov's atmospheric unity**: Night watchman's rattle, sound of felling axes, a breaking string, screech owls, raging fires, soft sighs, whistles, and pregnant pauses—rendering psychological states and entire ways of life (see `chekhov-dramaturgy`).
- **Dramatic contrast in traditional staging**: A birthday banquet hall flipped into a funeral parlor; the Monument to Chastity; Master Lu's mansion spring couplets vs. Xianglin's Wife collapsing in the snow; a manhunt under a moonlit wharf.
- **Cliches to avoid**: Rural stories set universally in courtyards; urban dramas set exclusively in living rooms. **Three criteria**: Serving the thematic premise (an alcove beside a railway tunnel holding only two people); serving characterization (a muddy mountain ditch drawing out historical, political, and economic contexts across three layers, letting Uncle Zhao vent his spleen → fight for face → lay bare his heart); serving conflict (an elevator becoming an active participant in conflict; construction scaffolding serving as the physical force resolving tension; *Zhang Gudong Borrows a Wife* echoing between Moon City and the in-laws' home; *Sisters Swap Marriages* crosscutting three vertical spaces, dust upstairs drifting into a wine cup downstairs).
- **Hardware and software**: Hardware = physical constraints and elements; software = literary and cultural elements enriching the scene's interior resonance (a recurring folk song, local legend, provenance of a location—*The Seventh Tree Is a Magnolia* weaving a theme song three times, the origin of Charity Square, the moral character of the tree-planter).
- Hicks's genre context: Relationship dramas with too much spatial breathing room bleed out the claustrophobic pressure of forced confrontation; comedies treat the entire world like a giant banana peel; horrors and thrillers twist spaces into labyrinthine corridors; detective noirs demand a foul, suffocating urban jungle. McKee: A small, knowable world (*Dr. Strangelove* built on three locations and eight characters).
- Egri on entrances and exits: Characters must enter and exit solely when plot necessity dictates and conflict is driven forward; dismissing a character on the pretext of "getting a glass of water" is inexcusably sloppy. Lu Jun: Stage major character entrances right as storm clouds gather overhead.

## 9. Diagnostic Checklist

1. Why here and now? Would shifting locations make conflict harder and reveal character more deeply? Have you generated a dozen alternative locations?
2. What value is at stake? What is the opening polarity (+/-), and what is the closing polarity? If unchanged, it is an exposition scene: cut it.
3. What does each character want, what tactic do they employ, and what is their unconscious super-objective? Is opposition head-on or tangential?
4. Track beats using gerunds: Are there repetitive beats? Does each beat edge closer to the turning point than the last? Is the turning point an action or a revelation? Does it land at the right moment without arriving prematurely or dragging late?
5. Did you enter late enough and exit early enough? Did you cut away before the conflict was fully tidied up?
6. Are there "nothing happens" dead zones (shaving, chit-chat, ordering food)? Are characters stuck in emotional states rather than executing physical actions?
7. Can dialogue be stripped entirely? What small physical gesture can be magnified (a ring, toast, stocking)?
8. Are phones, cars, restaurants, or offices truly unavoidable? If used, do they anchor the physical conflict?
9. Is the suspense a cheap "one-night delicacy" or the foreknowledge of fate? Was the proper key handed to the audience? Is the delay drawn out across enough layers (three tiers of escalation)?
10. Are details truthful, flesh-close, stamped with social imprints, and visible? Is there self-indulgent showiness (repeating the same detail seven or eight times, pausing to explain provenance)?
11. Does the prop serve as a structural core, conflict catalyst, character delineation, symbol, thematic window, or movement vehicle? Which of the six techniques was applied: magnification, miniaturization, repetition, distortion, virtualization, or animate surrogate?
12. Does the scene possess software (songs, lore, provenance)? Are entrances and exits dictated purely by plot demand?
13. Does the sequence possess a single unifying idea? What is the third element linking adjacent scenes (transition)? Do scenes shorten as they drive toward an act climax?

## 10. Workflow: Writing a Scene

1. In the sequence outline, verify this scene's **purpose** (the single piece of plot information + the specific revelation of the protagonist) and its placement within the act (pacing: long/short, fast/slow).
2. Write down the **value pair** at stake and its opening charge; note every character's scene objective, tactic, super-objective, and background desire.
3. **Choose the location**: Brainstorm a dozen possibilities and filter through the three criteria (premise / character / conflict); establish the software (song / story / provenance).
4. Settle on **props and details**: Assign a core prop its dramatic function; select two or three flesh-close, visual details; decide which detail recurs and confirm whether repetition drives dramatic progression.
5. Set the **suspense strategy**: Does the audience know more or less than the characters? Which key is placed in the audience's hand? How many layers of delay will be constructed?
6. Draft the **beat sheet** (gerund action/reaction units + value changes), pinpoint the turning point, and ensure no beat repeats; ask first: "How can this be told purely visually?"
7. Determine **entry and exit points**: Enter late, leave early; deliver the parting blow on the final word or action.
8. Draft the scene: Present-tense action paragraphs, concrete nouns, active verbs, zero camera direction, zero "we see"; for dialogue craft, refer to `sw-dialogue`.
9. **Self-check**: Run through the Diagnostic Checklist; apply Walter's "$?" test—is this line worth the audience's hard-earned dollar?; apply the "Three Strikes" rule—Does it have purpose? Does it turn value? Is it executed in the best possible way?

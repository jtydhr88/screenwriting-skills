# Writing a Succession-Style Series (39 episodes across four seasons, taken apart)

This skill is distilled from an episode-by-episode breakdown of Jesse Armstrong's *Succession: The Complete Scripts* in four volumes (Faber, 2023): 10 episodes in S1, 10 in S2, 9 in S3, 10 in S4 — **39 shooting scripts** — plus Armstrong's S1 and S4 introductions, the four Notes on the Text, all the author's footnotes, and the first-hand accounts of the London writers' room by Frank Rich (the S2 introduction) and Lucy Prebble (the S3 introduction, "One Last Pitch").

**The episode-by-episode structural parameter table for all 39 episodes (section 8) and the four-season arc grid (section 9)**, the comparison table of ten set pieces, the source quotations and evidence lists for each layer's patterns (numbered S/C/D), the footnote evidence table (eight rows summarized in section 5, the full version in section 10), the chain of evidence for the ending engineering, and the list of thematic aphorisms are all in [reference.md](reference.md) in the same directory.

The core credo (in Armstrong's own words): **"a script is only a recipe"** (a recipe – a set of instructions for action and suggested dialogue); **"I like to write long and I encourage my fellow writers to write long. I rewrite long"**; **"I have nothing to say, only things to show you"** (quoting Benjamin). The paradox of satire: with nothing to say it does not live, and saying it kills it — "like a leaflet scattered in the street."

General method is not repeated here: episode act structure and act-out taxonomy are in `sw-series-structure`; engine, bible, pilot and character web in `sw-series-engine-bible`; room process and taking notes in `sw-writers-room`; general dialogue method in `sw-dialogue`; the scene value turn in `sw-scene-craft`.

## Sources and a Note on the Texts

**The four volumes print the scripts as they stood when shooting began** ("as they stood when we started to film"), not the broadcast versions. The four Notes on the Text give the same list of differences, ordered by likelihood: **the edit** (chiefly) → **alts** (lines swapped in a day or two before shooting) → **late changes** (too late to typeset, written in by ballpoint) → **fog of war** (small changes of word order) → **improvisation** (the freebie take) → **ADR** → **the remembrance of things past** (reordering after the read-through; actors remember the old draft and stitch deleted fragments back in while shooting). Armstrong says publishing shooting scripts makes him feel exposed, because "the final draft of the show isn't the script. It's the version we air."

**Footnotes are added only where the reason for a change is not self-evident**, with research sources noted in passing. **The distribution of the footnotes is itself information** — the episodes that shot most smoothly have none (the S1 distribution and all the footnote evidence are in reference.md §5). A footnote is a reason for a change, not a statement of intent.

**Format characteristics**: **no ACT marks and no page numbers.** Scene headings read `Int./Ext. Location – Day/Night`. From S1E2 on, **DAY ONE / day two** story-day markers are used (S2E2 runs day one–seven); S4E05 and S4E10 print `day one / day two` as subheadings on the page; S4E08 prints **timestamps** (4:54 P.M. → 3:40 A.M.) with "On screen –" notes on the state-by-state count. `Intercut with:` is used heavily for phone cross-cutting. Stage directions often read "Maybe …?"; `(then)` marks a pause before changing subject; `[square brackets]` hold the unspoken second half of an interrupted line, written out so the actor understands it.

**The "invisible acts" are an inference, not text**: every invisible-act division in this skill and in reference.md comes from **a scene-by-scene inference over the scripts** in `_notes/`; the scripts carry no act marks, and the basis is stated each time. **Treat it as a checkable analysis, not as an Armstrong statement** — in four volumes he never discusses his own structure in terms of "acts."

## 1. The Four Seasons Compared, and the Line of Evolution

| Season | Eps | Engine question | Season shape | Written by Armstrong | How the finale redraws the alliances |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1 (2018) | 10 | Will Logan hand over power today? | **A symmetrical turnaround**: in E1 he takes Vaulter at $140 a share and loses the throne; in E10 he moves on his father's company at the same $140 and loses everything | E1, E9, E10 | Logan wins, and the way he wins is by **owning his son's manslaughter**; the show goes from "four children competing" to "the father holding everyone's debt" |
| S2 (2019) | 10 | Sell or not? And if we fight, who takes the hit? | **Choosing an heir → choosing a sacrifice**: the finale answers not "who inherits" but "whom do we hand over" | E1, E9, E10 | The father hands over his most obedient son as a blood sacrifice, and the son hands over the father on live television, and the father looks approving — **the engine resets to war** |
| S3 (2021) | **9** (the only nine-episode season) | The father wants to sell the kingdom; do the children hold any cards? | **Father vs. children → a sibling alliance**: they had one card, and the parents tore it up before the children came through the door | E1, E2, E8, E9 (two to open the season, two to close it) | Redrawn as "the children's alliance vs. the father plus Tom plus Matsson"; Tom gets a wink on the balcony |
| S4 (2023) | 10 (final) | Can succession be decided by the siblings' own vote? | **The protagonist dies 30% in**, and the other seven episodes are aftershocks; E10 hands the season's material goal to one procedural vote and puts the characters' endings in the twenty minutes after it | E1, E3, E4 (with Prebble), E8, E9, E10 | Not a redrawing but **switching the engine off**: the company keeps its name but takes Matsson's, and the CEO is the most replaceable man in it |

**The line of evolution** (described by the later practice): **scene counts go from many and short to few and long** (S1E2 has 84 scenes; the season finale S3E9 has only **24 scene headings**, four long pillars holding up an episode); **act marking goes from hidden to visible** (S1E1 has no DAY markers at all, and S4 prints subheadings and timestamps on the page — **streaming writers are still writing acts, they have just disguised the acts as a schedule**); **the containers become more fixed** (eight of S4's ten episodes compress into a public occasion with a fixed procedure); **answers come earlier and earlier** (S1's questions often span episodes — counting votes in E5, opening the ballot in E6; every S4 episode answers its question by its own ending); and **the stakes degrade from "who inherits" to "who gets disposed of"** (S1 asks about the throne, S2 about the sacrifice, S3 about whether there are votes at all, S4 about whether a dead man's handwriting counts as a voice). Per-item scene counts and symmetry are in reference.md §2, S5 and S6.

## 2. Reusable Patterns at the Structural Layer

**S1 The three-part test for invisible acts.** With no ACT marks, three things mark the acts: ① **story-day markers** (a new day is a new act); ② **a change of location or vehicle**; ③ **the moment the central question turns.** An act must end on a **transfer of information or power**, not necessarily on a suspense hook; the closing syntax is an event plus a new question, or a **loneliness-button stage direction** (S3E9, Roman scrolling his phone on the lake: "Gerri? Shiv?? Kendall??? **No one to call.**"). Every act position is inferred.
> First write out each episode's 2–7 story days, then find one transfer of power inside each day: that is your act. No more than seven days in an episode (S2E2's day one–seven is the ceiling).

**S2 The container episode and the ceremony beat sheet.** Compress a whole episode into **a public occasion with a fixed procedure**, and let the procedure's checkpoints replace the act breaks. Four uses: **clock cards on screen**, **the order of ceremony as the call sheet**, **an external event as the act-break caption**, and **leaving the head of the table empty first** (in S3E9 Logan does not come to the wedding, and **the ceremony becomes a search**). Also: **the seating plan is the relationship map.** The full inventory of 21 containers across four seasons, samples of the four uses, and the seating evidence are in reference.md §2, S2.
> List the fixed stages first (arrivals, gifts, toasts, speeches, the first dance, fireworks) and stuff one conflict into each. The stronger the ceremony's order, the greater the transgression of the hidden line, so the conspiracy can only advance through corridor asides, things held in a palm, bathrooms and car parks.

**S3 The pressure chamber plus severed communications.** Lock everyone into one building **and add a physical restriction on information** (the phone pouches at the S1E8 warehouse party: "Okay, boys, we're pouching."). Restrict the flow of information first, then make information travel by body. The advanced version is S3E7: phones are demanded and **everyone refuses** — the refusal is itself a declaration. Seven instances are in reference.md §2, S3.
> What a constraint creates is not difficulty but a demonstration of who can change the rules.

**S4 One core question an episode, answered inside that episode.** The syntax is fixed as "can X do Y today," with the answer given at the episode's end (the ten questions of S2 are listed in reference.md §2, S4).
> Cross-episode suspense is carried by the new configuration of power, not by an unanswered question.

**S5 The finale has fewer and longer scenes.** S3E9 has only 24 scenes (E5 of the same season has 60), and four long pillars: a negotiation, a ceremony, a private conversation, a confrontation.
> Compress a finale into 3–5 long pillars. If the finale's scene count is above the season's average, you are probably writing an event list rather than a settlement.

**S6 Season-shape symmetry.** In S1E1 Kendall takes Vaulter at $140 a share while his father collapses and the throne evaporates; in S1E10 he moves on his father's company at **the same $140** while he himself collapses and the father is restored. The line version and the music version are in reference.md §2, S6.
> Find a repeatable quantity for a season (a price, a line, a piece of music), use it once at each end, and never explain it in between.

**S7 A climax with the protagonist absent.** The S1E6 no-confidence vote: its instigator is stuck in traffic and speaks remotely over a Polycom, so the suspense shifts from "the count" to "can he get there." **Absence is the strongest device for sustaining suspense**, and its extreme form is S4E03 — the protagonist **dies off screen.** Four versions are in reference.md §2, S7.

> ⚠️ **Two preconditions, and without either this device becomes a defect** — and item 5 of the diagnostic checklist in `sw-chinese-series-practice` ("the protagonist is present and has enough screen time") will fail it outright. Both are right, and the difference is the preconditions:
> 1. **The absent person must still be exerting will** (speaking remotely over a Polycom, a phone call, a dispatch, a rule he laid down), and **this episode must be about his absence.** Someone who merely says nothing while absent is not a device, they are a hole.
> 2. **There must be a stock of presence.** Every absence in Succession happens after the audience has followed the show for dozens of episodes; on a show three episodes old, the protagonist's presence is not yet strong enough to be sustained by an empty chair. **Use it sparingly early on.**
> What to ask in diagnosis is not "are they present" but "**did the absence generate action.**"
> Move the climax's instigator out of the room, and the suspense turns from "how will it turn out" into "can he get there / will he be found out / will they believe it."

**S8 Decisions made in the gaps of an external clock.** S4E08 hands the (inferred) acts to a public event's clock, and the characters can only decide between two calls. Three samples are in reference.md §2, S8.
> Fix the external clock first (the election calls, the ceremony's running order, the board's roll call, the publication time of a story), and fit the characters' choices into the gaps — so the choices look forced out of them by time.

**S9 Dimming a plant.** S3E3 cut Tom's explicit line about "divorce" on the grounds that it "cast too heavy a shadow over subsequent events," leaving only the lawyer's name, **Rex Hendon**, for the attentive viewer, to be paid off in S3E9. The reverse example is in reference.md §2, S9.
> Cut the explicit line and leave only a name, a habit, an inconsistency. An attack need not aim at the vital spot — let the adversary reveal an inconsistency about something trivial, and hand it to a third party to detonate.

**S10 Migrating a cut scene.** **Do not throw away a cut scene; move it to a position with a lower resting heart rate.** The "We knew" argument at the S2E10 card table moved to the S3E2 bedroom, and the footnote says it is "more germane and sprung-in" there — **the value of the same scene depends on whether enough pressure precedes it.** Three cases are in reference.md §2, S10.
> When a scene is cut, record whether it was "in the wrong place" or "wrong"; the first waits for a structural gap, and only the second is thrown away.

**S11 Letting one occasion contaminate another.** S4E07–E09 use this throughout: put the deal line and the marriage line inside the same party, corridor or funeral, so a victory on one line automatically becomes the cause of defeat on the other — **kindness rewritten by structure into leverage, with the script never saying so.** The cleanest example (Shiv sends Tom home to sleep → Tom misses the funeral → Matsson reads it as a point in his favour) is in reference.md §2, S11.

**S12 A technical failure delaying an action.** In S1E10, "deliver the takeover letter" is a one-line action stretched into a whole passage of suspense by three small obstacles; a related case is in reference.md §2, S12 ("I am down to my last fucking touchscreen!").
> An absurd prop makes a grand decision look like an office accident — which is this show's basic tone for handling power.

## 3. Reusable Patterns at the Character Layer

**C1 Seven wants in one room.** An ensemble show needs no extra conflict designed; it needs only seven people's small wants for that week put into one room and made to block each other (the seven constant wants of S1 are in reference.md §3, C1).
> When you are done, check: which two people's wants do not collide this episode? That line is filler.

**C2 Humiliation conducted downward.** Anyone who receives a humiliation must pass it to someone weaker in the next scene. The whole of S1E8 is one chain (Logan → Roman → Tom → Greg, in full in reference.md §3, C2); the Boar on the Floor in S2E3 is the ritualized version of that chain.
> This rule generates scenes by itself. When you cannot write the next scene, ask "who was just humiliated, and who is the weakest person near them?"

**C3 Three versions of a family memory.** The Dog Pound in S1E8: one childhood episode in three versions (the accuser's, the brothers' flat denial, and a third party's third version). The third party then lets slip the father's dog-training theory, and Kendall, having heard it, "thinks, makes a calculation. And exits.", switching from selling his shares to launching a leveraged buyout. All three versions are in reference.md §3, C3.
> Let the version a third party lets slip be the trigger for the turn, not an annotation of the theme. The unreliability of memory is the engine of the reversal.

**C4 Send each character back once a season to a place that makes them smaller.** S2E7, "Return," is one episode built entirely on this rule; S2E8 is the intensified version: Logan returns to his old house in Dundee and **does not get out of the car** — "Nah. I can see it. I can see it." (The full roster is in reference.md §3, C4.)
> Once per character per season. The hardest version is to have them reach the door and not go in.

**C5 The heir is the most replaceable person.** The winner of S4 is Tom: the children lack application, and more importantly **their timing is wrong.** The theme is carried by two lines, **neither of them Tom's own** — in S4E10 Matsson says, giving him the job, "I don't need a partner, I need a **frontman**… a **pain sponge**." The introduction's own wording and Shiv's verdict are in reference.md §3, C5.
> If the theme is "the empire no longer belongs to the family," the person who ends on top must be someone with no agenda, a high pain threshold, and replaceable at any time; and the reason for the appointment must be spoken aloud by an enemy or by whoever despises him most.

**C6 The opponents are more logical than the protagonist, and each for a different reason.** The core of the S3E9 showdown is not the father but three old retainers refusing to help, each refusal more personal than the last (fiduciary duty / money / an old grudge); the final blow is written as a stage direction, "leaning in so only Roman can hear." The three speeches and comparable samples are in reference.md §3, C6.
> Not one of the reasons is "the villain is just bad." Compress the final blow into a whisper only one person can hear.

**C7 Mirror characters and external referees.** Four devices: **the mirror family** (the Pierces in S2E5 — the more orderly the family across the table, the louder the Roys' silence); **mirrored incapacity** (S3E5 juxtaposes two old men being spoken for); **the external referee** (Josh, Mencken, Matsson, whose function is **to force the protagonists to say what they would never normally say**, after which the truth of the lines is left suspended); and **the weakest person naming the game's logic** (Greg, Willa, Kira) — **the joke is the theme.** All four kinds with quotations are in reference.md §3, C7.

**C8 Physical decline as a hidden clock.** The motif of S3: from bending to pick up a phone, to UTI delirium, to reading his grandson a children's book about death. The S1 version: Logan practising walking inside a taped square on the floor, "Walking's not good. It's fucking **normal**." (The full scale is in reference.md §3, C8.)
> Write "there is not enough time" as a physical detail, not as dialogue. Three or four times a season, each more specific than the last.

## 4. The Dialogue and Stage-Direction Layer

Full quotations and examples for all thirteen patterns are in reference.md §4.

**D1 Write subtext and "maybe" into the stage directions.** Write the real intention as a note and let the line say the opposite: "True? Or passive-aggressive? Kendall sort of wants his dad to tell him what to do, but doesn't want to ask for direction." (S1E1). Also: characterize without forcing a binary, write interiority as a question, and keep the maybes (Prebble: "Now I always keep the maybes in.").
> Do not fix an expression; write the character's psychological question. Keep the maybe. Use square brackets for the unspoken second half of an interrupted line. The gaps you leave on the page are what the floor can give you back.

**D2 Turn a reversal on one word.** The S2 finale: at the press conference Kendall **delivers the compliant version in full**, then says a single word: **"But."**
> He must first say the compliant version without dropping a syllable, or neither the room nor the audience will believe he has capitulated.

**D3 The trigger must be a word established earlier.** The trigger in S2E10 is **NRPI** ("No Real Person Involved"): in S2E9 it is internal company jargon, and in S2E10 in the father's cabin it becomes a word of comfort to a son — the father thinks he is granting absolution, and is in fact classifying his son as "not a real person," so **the absolution and the insult are the same sentence.**
> The trigger must be a word established earlier, and the speaker's intention must change between the two uses.

**D4 Recycle a comic plant as evidence.** "Mo Lester" is an in-house joke in the early episodes of S2; read out word for word by a senator at the S2E9 hearing, it becomes an accusation; the more the defendant tries to escape via "it was a joke," the more it proves systemic acquiescence.
> For every joke you write, ask once how it reads in a courtroom. The antagonist's strongest weapon is an old joke.

**D5 Give the aphorisms about power to supporting characters.** At least one quotable aphorism an episode, preferably to a supporting character, with the protagonist failing to listen (Frank: "You are what you do.").
> The epic register is carried by characters and punctured by cost. Let an outsider translate your family epic into a few extra cents a share.

**D6 Four denials as four miniature biographies.** The cut card-table scene in S2E10 is written not as a confession but as four kinds of denial, and Shiv's closing "I did not know what I was laughing about" is the season's most precise character definition.
> Write an ensemble's complicity as several different ways of denying — four people's denials are four biographies.

**D7 Let the protagonist lie successfully.** In S1E9 Tom asks "Are you fucking around on me?", and the stake is written into the stage direction: "If she looks away, he'll know. But she doesn't." She lies successfully, and so Tom volunteers every detail of the cruise scandal to her.
> A successful lie drives plot better than being caught — the lie becomes the next episode's debt.

**D8 Turn a story on a polite question.** The season's biggest turn in S3E9 (Matsson proposing the acquisition) is written as a question that leaves the other party an exit, and Logan replies, "You're not fucking serious."
> Write a major turn as a question that leaves the other side a way out. Their "you can't be serious" carries all the shock for you.

**D9 Let the adversary deliver the protagonist's interior monologue.** In the next line of that scene, Matsson says Kendall's interior monologue for him — saving an entire scene of introspection while establishing the new adversary's intellectual level.

**D10 Comedy carrying tragedy.** Frank Rich's principle: "drama or comedy" is a false question; "The show is a human comedy, not a satire" — **you laugh with them first, and then you cry with them.** Three ways to do it: (a) catch the darkest confession with a family joke (the S3E9 script's own note reads "It is so dark that it is kind of funny"); (b) demote an undebatable large issue into a debatable small one; (c) use comedy to complete the mourning.

**D11 Escalate a quarrel to the most vulnerable point.** In the S4E07 balcony fight, every line moves closer to the other's most vulnerable point, landing on "you are maybe not a good person to have children." — and the audience knows Shiv is pregnant while Tom does not.
> Let the audience hold a piece of information one party lacks, so the cruellest line carries two layers of injury; leave the reaction to the actors.

**D12 The ellipsis is the theme.** In S4E09, two old retainers finish talking about Logan and look at each other — **"Right?" "Right."**
> Let the show's heaviest moral statement stop inside an ellipsis. The omitted line is the theme.

**D13 When a relationship has no new lines, rewrite it as a physical action.** In S4E10 Kendall's embrace tears Roman's stitches open ("the stitches tear a little, blood seeps"); the show's final passage has Tom extend his palm, "**no squeeze. Just contact.**"
> Rewrite it as a physical action whose meaning both sides take for granted, then fix the relationship with one line of characterization and give no explanation.

## 5. The Room and the Production Layer

The general process is in `sw-writers-room`; only Armstrong's own practices and figures are recorded here, with quotations and the footnote evidence table in reference.md §5.

1. **Write long**: target **70+ pages an episode** (the norm for an hour is 60, and some shows squeeze to 50). The reason: a few extra dialogue pages "return exponential rewards," and only then does the edit have room to refocus. **Draft counts: about 15 on the pilot; at least 30 and usually 50 on every episode after.**
2. **Alts**: a day or two before shooting, pick 0 to 10–15 lines that might be "funnier or better or truer," send them to 3–5 writers, filter the 10–20 options to 5–10 kept in the pocket, and feed them to the actors after the scripted version is shot.
3. **The freebie take**: after the scripted version, do one improvised take. Its value is not the line you happen to catch but the "spirit of freedom and collaboration" it gives every take.
4. **The wall and the mega-chart** (Prebble): a room of 8–12 people, British and American mixed, comedy and tragedy backgrounds mixed, five to six hours a day. Large sheets headed by **character name**, with ideas written underneath; surviving entries go onto the **mega-chart** — characters across, episodes down.
5. **Walk into the room with a fixed season end and no route**: before S3 only three things were locked (the siblings would join forces, Tom had to inform, and "how we got there was totally up for grabs").
6. **Pitch from the trashy version and pull back** (Prebble): open with "This is the trashy version," not "This is shit but –." "**Pitching is the way in which you work out what the show isn't, as well as what it is.**"
7. **Two ordering rules**: **reality beats the mythic; the funniest can occasionally win.**
8. **The "does two things" cutting test**: a scene must accomplish at least two things at once, and **anything doing fewer than two is cut.**
9. **Page → floor → edit, with the last two entitled to veto the first.** The general principle of the edit room: "the edit generates its own powerful centrifugal force – throwing off material which is further from the show's core questions and concerns." (Eight footnotes' worth of evidence are in the footnote table in reference.md §5.)
10. **Leave the final image to the floor**: from the S4 introduction, "You won't find the end of the show, as it went to air, written in these scripts. **That we found on the day.**"
11. **Support systems and firefighting**: consultants divided by field (business moves, media, law, New York society, politics each had their own person); firefighting was done by a fixed handful of writers — "to help me write – and to rewrite – when we had an episode that wasn't firing."
12. **What a showrunner is**: "essentially a writer. But a writer empowered to protect the scripts through the rooms where they are conceived and written and rewritten, performed, captured and edited." Running a room is **throwing a party**; the room was in London rather than New York for "a sense of having been forgotten about"; and **2–3 writers were on set every day.**
13. **Needle-drop discipline**: the scripts wrote a great many needle-drops, and almost all were cut in the edit (only the end of E6 survives in S1); the author found his own music suggestions "far too direct or arch." **A song in a script is only a statement of intent.**
14. **Characters rewritten because of actors** (Frank Rich): Gerri was originally a man, "Jerry"; several supporting parts were expanded because of the performances; the precondition being that "the series' architecture could support it without buckling the overall design." The reverse: an actor can veto what a script implies.

## 6. Ending Engineering

The complete chain of evidence (including the eight steps of the off-screen death, the regressive game, the ghost phone, and *Logan's Dinner*) is in reference.md §6.

1. **Engine pressure loss** — what decides when to end is not the characters. It was not that inspiration ran out ("the motor in the room was still running hot") but that **"the big plot engine… was looking to me like it would eventually start to lose pressure."**
   > Stress-test the material engine: list the deals, positions and resources the show runs on, and ask "how many more seasons can they lose pressure for?" A character's emotional material is never finished; an engine is.
2. **Three season shapes, decided by a vote of the writers.** Pinned to the wall: "one final season of ten episodes, or two of six or eight episodes"; four writers stated a position, and Prebble decided — "**the ten-episode season was the muscular way to go out.**"
   > Before a final season starts, post 2–3 possible season shapes, have every writer state a position with reasons on the record, and let one person decide; once the shape is settled, it does not change.
3. **3–5 anchors, with the rest left to the process.** S4's four: ① Logan dies early, and suddenly; ② the children experience it by phone at a wedding; ③ the GoJo deal **must close** — "That was the case in the world, so it had to be the case for us."; ④ Tom wins.
4. **The eight steps of an off-screen death** (S4E03): a single unreliable source → someone else turns "saying goodbye" into an action → three "by his ear" monologues (each ≤8 lines, unanswered) → the ladder of denial → even "informing people" is an act of power → physically separating them from the body → officializing the death as a string of technical checkpoints → the irony coming from everyone simply doing their own job. Item-by-item quotations are in reference.md §6.
5. **The ellipsis is the theme** (see D12). The method is to lay the setup, hold the tone, build the world correctly, and then "**step back from the mechanism, let it run.**" His definition of satire is one sentence: "Taking a hard look at the world as it is." — and "afflicting the comforted… will go better if you avoid announcing your intention too clearly at the door."
6. **The regressive game.** The high point of the final episode is a **regressive game with no plot advance** (the three siblings mixing the new king a "meal fit for a king" in the kitchen in S4E10). Four points: the three of them are in the same intention for the first and last time, and every joke is **cooperative**; the mother's stinginess supplies the material to imitate; the one time anyone tries to say something tender they are immediately interrupted — **that unfinished pause is the trailer for the ending**; and the spit is the smallest rehearsal of the betrayal.
7. **The ghost phone: abolish the question of the will rather than answering it.** On the S4E10 terrace, Kendall picks up an imaginary phone and answers it, and Shiv plays along; and then the same prop carries the sincerity — "He fucking promised it to me. Promised. When I was seven."
   > Do not reveal whom the father actually meant to leave it to. Let the heirs mock the question together, and use the same prop first to ridicule an opponent and then to hand over something true.
8. **Let the show "not know it is ending"**: keep multi-season versions alive through writing and shooting ("Almost so that the show itself didn't know it was ending"), which avoids an ending that is "freeze-dried and pre-packaged" and avoids "the metal gears of LA business" crushing every possibility early.
9. **Write the most moving passage as a "cuttable background layer."** The S4 appendix *Logan's Dinner* began as no more than "a bit of additional flavour… which might or might not make the cut" on a television in the background of E10; each guest gets a "party piece."
   > Write it as a background layer and it carries no structural risk; use the actors' real skills to get something you could not have written. Put a home video of the dead man's amateur performance in the final episode: each turn is one sentence of characterization, and the reactions of those watching complete the emotional settlement without any dialogue.
10. **The open method is a method, not vagueness.** Quoting Darcey Bussell — what draws you in is not flawless execution but "the fear that she might be on the verge of falling"; a showrunner's power should be used to protect the gaps, or you write something "closed-off, invulnerable… every scene sleek and sealed" — "**the cracks are where the light gets in.**"

## 7. The Set-Piece Template: the Shared Rules of Ten Big Scenes

The ten-scene comparison table (space / who sets the rules / mechanism / exit) is in reference.md §1.

**Nine shared rules**: ① **the set piece happens where there is an answer to "whose ground is this"** — the ground decides who can change the rules. ② **There is always a visible physical constraint**: phones collected / no shoes / no signal / a list / an oath / a refusal to hand over phones. ③ **There is always an enforced speaking order**: "One by one!", a roll call, a witness list, "argue the brief," a speaking order at a wedding, a board roll-call vote. ④ **There is always an outsider who names the game's logic** (Greg, Willa, Kira). ⑤ **Give the audience a list before they set off**: Tern Haven's Dos & Don'ts (forbidden subjects, smart-casual, two drinks maximum, codename Operation "Let Them Dream Their Beautiful Dream"), Hugo's six As (Apologize / Accept / Accountability / Advocates for change / Assist victims / Ally with good actors) and **DUMI** (Distance Undermine Minimize Isolate), Gerri's "Plausibly Paper Ignorant" — **the whole scene is the list being violated item by item, and the suspense costs nothing.** ⑥ **A gift is self-exposure**: Roman's perfunctory recording in S2E8, Connor's excessive sincerity, Kendall's rap, Roman getting the wrong team (Logan is Hibs, not Hearts); the three gifts in S3E7 correspond to power, love and family. ⑦ **Food and drink are a test of obedience**: the doughnuts dissolve the alliance in S3E2; Mencken turns up uninvited with a Coke in S3E6 ("Anointed with a Coke."); the grandson tastes the cheese first in S3E8 (a food taster); Tom bites into Logan's chicken and puts it back in S2E10 — **whoever gives food is the patron, and whoever drinks is the vassal.** ⑧ **Public spaces carry the lines, service spaces carry the truth**: the aircraft toilet in S3E1, the urinals in S3E7, the car park in S3E9, the goods lift in S2E4, backstage in S4E09, the karaoke room in S4E02 ("the Shame Palace"). ⑨ **There must be a private settlement coda afterwards** (a corridor, a car, a bathroom, a card table, a loading bay), converting the public humiliation into specific interpersonal damage.

## 8. Workflow: Writing a Succession-Style Ensemble

1. **Settle the "simple impulse" first, then build the world.** Armstrong: "the better films and TV shows I've been involved with have at their heart a quite simple impulse around which the more subtle layers are spun" — In the Loop was anger about Iraq, Peep Show was male friendship, and **Succession's simple thing was Brexit and Trump**: "what happens when something as important as the flow of information in a democracy hits the reductive brutality of the profit calculation," and then watching it "rebound emotionally and psychologically" inside a family thinking about succession.
2. **Start from real models, then fictionalize.** He resisted fictionalizing for five years (believing a fictional family "never has the power of a real one"), and finally accepted it because only fiction could produce a "propitiously balanced fictional hybrid media conglomerate," pouring the best material from Murdoch, Redstone and Maxwell (plus Conrad Black, Hearst and Beaverbrook) into one man. What the models have in common: **refusing to think about death** (both models called their succession plans "immortality"), a need for control, manic deal energy, a love of gossip and power relations, ruthlessness in hiring and firing, and **forward motion plus a lack of introspection.**
3. **Settle one pitch line.** "Festen-meets-Dallas," "No stars, Dogme 95 camerawork." What a good pitch feels like: "the air thickens… the enthusiasm you are trying to project transforms into an enthusiasm you are actually feeling."
4. **Fix the names until you are living inside it**: "they're a tell-tale sign of whether I'm living inside it."
5. **Write the engine question before the season shape.** The engine question must be **insoluble** (succession), and each season answers only one "version for this year" of it.
6. **Draw the mega-chart**: characters across, episodes down, with "this episode's want → state at episode's end" in every cell.
7. **Give every episode a container**, with the procedure's checkpoints as act breaks; where no ready-made procedure exists, print day one/two/three or timestamps on the page.
8. **Write one core question per episode, and answer it inside that episode.**
9. **Put the seven wants into the container to block each other**; check whether each line contaminates another line at the same occasion.
10. **Sever communications**: add a visible physical constraint, and give the audience a list so they can watch it being violated item by item.
11. **Lay out the humiliation chain**: Logan → Roman → Greg, Tom → Greg; every scene passes the same shame along.
12. **Move the climax's instigator out** (stuck in traffic, elsewhere, off screen).
13. **Write subtext into the stage directions**: one note for the real intention while the lines say the opposite; keep the maybes and the square brackets; an important decision may have no dialogue at all (Kendall accelerating away at the gate in S1E7; walking toward the water and back three times after the crash in S1E10).
14. **At least one aphorism about power an episode, given to a supporting character**; let an outsider translate the epic into a few cents a share. A directly usable list of aphorisms is in reference.md §7.
15. **Write long, to 70+ pages**, then ask 3–5 writers for alts, and do a freebie take after the scripted version.
16. **Cut with the "does two things" test**; cut the most complete but most separable strand first, and put it in the material bank to await a structural gap next season.
17. **The finale has fewer, longer scenes**: 3–5 long pillars, with the room scene where everyone confronts each other placed last — **no act out, only settlement.**
18. **The finale both closes and opens**: every line resolves, but every resolution creates debt for the next season (Greg's photocopies, Tom's cruise ships, Roman's manslaughter, Shiv's double loyalty); **do not resolve the conflict, redraw the lines of alliance.**
19. **Leave the final image to the floor.** The script goes as far as the character's last action.
20. **Do not announce your intention at the door.** Lay the setup, hold the tone, build the world correctly, and then step back and let the mechanism run.

## 9. The "Succession-ize It" Checklist

1. Does the script have day markers or timestamps? Inside each story day, can you point to one transfer of information or power?
2. Does each episode have a container (a public occasion with a fixed procedure)? Do the procedure's checkpoints replace the act breaks?
3. Is there a visible physical constraint inside the container? Does anyone **refuse** to comply with it?
4. What is this episode's core question? Is it in the form "can X do Y today"? Is the answer given at the episode's end?
5. What small want does each of the seven main characters have this episode? Are there two whose wants do not collide?
6. How does the humiliation chain run? Does everyone humiliated pass it to someone weaker in the next scene?
7. Is there an outsider (the weakest person) naming the game's logic, in a line that is simultaneously the joke and the theme?
8. Is there an aphorism about power given to a supporting character? Does the protagonist fail to listen?
9. Do the stage directions carry subtext, or expressions? Is there a maybe? Is interiority written anywhere as a question?
10. Is there a scene where an important decision is made with no dialogue at all?
11. Is the climax's instigator present? What is the reason for their absence?
12. Does this season have a repeatable quantity (a price, a line, a piece of music) used once at each end?
13. Does the family memory have three versions? Does the third party's version trigger the plot turn?
14. Has every character been sent back once this season to a place that makes them smaller?
15. Are the opponents more logical than the protagonist? Are the three opponents' reasons three different kinds (fiduciary duty / money / an old grudge), and is the final blow a whisper?
16. Has a comic plant been recycled once as evidence ("how does it read in a courtroom")?
17. Is there a reversal completed in one word? Was that trigger word established earlier?
18. Does the show's heaviest moral statement stop inside an ellipsis ("Right?" "Right.")?
19. When a relationship runs out of new lines, have you rewritten it as a physical action both parties understand?
20. Does the finale have fewer and longer scenes? Does it redraw the lines of alliance rather than resolve the conflict?
21. Is the person who ends on top the most replaceable one? Is the reason for their appointment spoken by an enemy?
22. Have you announced your intention at the door?

# Succession, 39 Episodes Across Four Seasons: Episode Tables, Season Arc Grids, the Set-Piece Comparison and Source Examples

> Use alongside SKILL.md. The scripts: Jesse Armstrong, *Succession: The Complete Scripts, Seasons One–Four* (Faber).

> This file holds the full comparison tables, source quotations and evidence lists moved out of SKILL.md. The numbering corresponds one-to-one with SKILL.md's pattern numbering (S1–S12 structural layer, C1–C8 character layer, D1–D13 dialogue and stage-direction layer, section 5 the room and production, section 6 ending engineering), so the two can be searched in either direction. **Every division involving "acts," "invisible acts" or "act positions" is a scene-by-scene inference over the scripts (inferred); the scripts themselves carry no ACT marks and no page numbers** — treat it as a checkable analysis, not as an Armstrong statement.

## 2. Source Evidence for the Structural Layer (S1–S12)

**A sample of S1's test ②**: a change of location or vehicle — the whole of S3E1 advances in waiting rooms, in the air and in traffic, and every `Intercut with:` is a transaction of asymmetric information. An act must end on a transfer of information or power, not necessarily on a suspense hook (the act positions are inferred).

**S2 The four-season inventory of containers (21 of them)**: the ball S1E4, the family dinner S1E5, the bachelor-party warehouse S1E8, the wedding castle S1E9–E10, the hunting lodge S2E3, the rivals' ancestral house S2E5, the summit S2E6, the birthday tribute S2E8, the congressional hearing S2E9, the yacht S2E10, the shareholders' meeting S3E5, the donors' summit S3E6, the birthday party S3E7, the wedding S3E9, the rehearsal plus karaoke S4E02, the retreat S4E05, investor day S4E06, the party S4E07, election night S4E08, the funeral S4E09, and the distribution of effects plus the board meeting S4E10.

**S2 The container's four uses**:

- **Clock cards on screen** — the S1E4 ball, at 4 p.m./6:15/6:30/7:20/7:51/8:25/9:26, one small beat every 20–30 minutes: the glass crisis → the butter crisis → the speech.
- **The order of ceremony as the call sheet** — the S4E09 funeral runs Introductory Rite → Liturgy of the Word → the eulogies → Recessional → the interment → the wake (treating those six stages as act positions is an inference).
- **An external event as the act-break caption** — S4E08's "On screen –", where every call is an external act out.
- **Leaving the head of the table empty** — at the S3E9 wedding Logan does not come, everyone is looking for him, and **the ceremony becomes a search.**

**S2 The seating plan is the relationship map**: S3E9 writes out a row of seats, "Kendall, Tom, Shiv, Roman, Contessa, Greg, Comfrey," and three hours later that row has split into two camps.

**S3 Seven instances of the pressure chamber plus severed communications**: the hospital corridor S1E2, Thanksgiving's "Skeleton staff" S1E5, the warehouse party's **phone pouches** S1E8 ("Okay, boys, we're pouching."), the castle's phone check-in desk S1E9–E10, "Phones on the table" S2E3, three rhyming safe rooms S2E4, the white-noise machines and armed guards S3E6. The advanced version, S3E7: the birthday party demands phones and **everyone refuses** — the refusal is itself a declaration.

**S4 The core questions of S2's ten episodes**: sell or not / who goes to Vaulter / who tells the truth / who is in the room / can they fake a happy family for 48 hours / can they sign before the story runs / who gets into the room / who becomes CEO / can "nobody knew" get them through / whom do we hand over.

**S5 Finale scene-count samples**: S3E9 has only 24 scenes (E5 of the same season has 60), with four long pillars: a negotiation, a ceremony, a private conversation, a confrontation. S4E03 and S4E04 have 35 and 34 (the season's two lowest). S1E2 has 84 and S1E6 has 80. Inside S4E03's 35 scenes, the middle section is about 30 minutes of real time without a location change.

**S6 The three quantities of season-shape symmetry**: the price version — in S1E1 Kendall takes Vaulter at $140 a share while his father collapses and the throne evaporates; in S1E10 he uses **the same $140** to buy his father's company while he himself collapses and his father is restored. The line version — S4E03's "What we do today will always be what we did the day our father died" → paid off in S4E10. The music version — Handel's 'Sarabande' appears once each in S1's E4, E7 and E10, each time juxtaposing pomp with pathos.

**S7 Four versions of a climax with the protagonist absent**: S1E6, the no-confidence vote — the instigator is stuck in traffic (a terror threat has closed the skies and blocked the tunnels) and speaks remotely over a Polycom, so the suspense shifts from "the count" to "can he get there"; S1E10, the return after the crash — everyone is dancing, "It's like a dream. **No one missed him.**"; S3E9 — Logan is present for the first four-fifths only by phone, by dispatch and by absence, and **absence is the strongest device for sustaining suspense**; S4E03 — the protagonist **dies off screen.**

**S8 Three samples of the external clock**: S4E08 hands the (inferred) acts to a public event's clock, and the characters can only decide between two calls; S3E5 takes place entirely on the day of the shareholders' meeting, with everyone packed into the green room and only three legitimate reasons to leave (going on stage, the bathroom, taking a call); S2E6 is a countdown to "sign before the scandal runs," with the story finally popping up on a phone under the negotiating table.

**S9 Dimming a plant, positive and negative**: S3E3 cut Tom's explicit "divorce" line to the receptionist on the grounds that it "cast too heavy a shadow over subsequent events," leaving only the lawyer's name **Rex Hendon** for the attentive viewer, to be paid off in S3E9. The reverse: in S2E8 Shiv stops the champagne with "She doesn't drink," and that throwaway line is amplified later in the same episode by Logan himself into "Pricktease."

**S10 Three cases of migrating a cut scene**: the S2E10 Roman–Gerri restaurant scene was cut for material overload and its substance kept for the start of the next season (landing in S3E1, where the footnote says it "find[s] a spot to nestle more comfortably"); the "We knew" argument at the S2E10 card table moved to the S3E2 bedroom, where the footnote says it is "more germane and sprung-in" — **the value of the same scene depends on whether enough pressure precedes it**; and the S1E1 scene of Shiv giving the album of the old house was cut and later stitched into S2E8, "Dundee."

**S11 The cleanest example of one occasion contaminating another**: S4E07–E09 use this throughout. In S4E09 Shiv, in her one moment of unconditional kindness, sends the exhausted Tom home to sleep (the stage direction reads "The greatest gift in the world."), which makes Tom miss the funeral, which Matsson in S4E10's job interview reads as a point in his favour ("Wasn't like he was gonna un-die if I made it?") — **kindness rewritten by structure into leverage, with the script never saying so.**

**S12 The absurd-obstacle list**: "deliver the takeover letter" in S1E10 is a one-line action stretched into a whole passage of suspense by three small obstacles: the printer is connected to the castle's Wi-Fi, a child wanders into the war room, and a colleague passes the buck. Comparable: in S3E3, getting into the building is blocked by a failed keycard, a goods lift and a stuck, turned-down air conditioner; in S4E08, "I am down to my last fucking touchscreen!" and mustard in the eye.

## 3. Source Evidence for the Character Layer (C1–C8)

**C1 The constant layer of S1's seven wants**: Logan wants never to hand over power; Kendall wants to be recognized as heir; Roman wants to be treated as "a serious person" (S1E2, "you're not a serious person"); Shiv wants to win on her own merits outside the family while keeping the leverage of the Roy name; Tom wants to get into the family and be genuinely loved; Greg wants a job and not to be humiliated; Connor wants to be treated as the eldest son.

**C2 The humiliation chain in full**: the whole of S1E8 is one chain — Logan berates Roman for closing a deal where "the other side was smiling" ("It's no good if he's smiling.") → Roman abandons Tom's old friend in a wasteland → Tom uses Greg as a punchbag → Kendall forces Greg to do two lines of cocaine. The Boar on the Floor in S2E3 is the ritualized version of that chain.

**C3 The Dog Pound's three versions** (S1E8): Roman deploys the childhood trauma (the cage, the dog bowl, the leash) in a business meeting to drive the buyer off; Kendall denies it flatly ("It was a game. You enjoyed it."); Connor supplies a third version (you asked to get in the cage yourself; the bowl had chocolate cake in it). Connor then lets slip the father's dog-training theory — "You got two fighting dogs. You send the weak one away… Then everyone knows the hierarchy and everyone's happy." — and Kendall, having heard it, "thinks, makes a calculation. And exits.", switching from selling his shares to launching a leveraged buyout.

**C4 Sending each character back once a season to a place that makes them smaller**: S2E7, "Return," is one episode built on this rule — Logan goes back to Britain, Kendall goes back to the bridge where the man died (and is handed a glass of cloudy water in the dead man's family's kitchen), Roman and Shiv go back to their mother's kitchen, and Tom sleeps on Greg's floor. The intensified version in S2E8: Logan returns to his old house in Dundee and **does not get out of the car** — "Nah. I can see it. I can see it."

**C5 Three pieces of evidence that the heir is the most replaceable person**: the S4 introduction states that the children lack application, and more crucially **their timing is wrong** — these days a non-founder in media can only be "an amenable man like Tom to rise gently like a bubble of air in the tank. A corporate fusion of Kenneth Widmerpool and Joseph Stalin."; in S4E10 Shiv appraises Tom to Matsson as "a highly modular interchangeable part" (**her verdict is the reason for his appointment**); and Matsson offers him the job with "I don't need a partner, I need a **frontman**… So I'll need a **pain sponge** while I get under the hood." **Neither thematic line is spoken by Tom himself.**

**C6 Three old retainers refusing to help** (S3E9, each more personal than the last): **Gerri (fiduciary duty)**, "how does it serve my interests?"; **Karl (money)**, "Golden handshake. Big fucking golden handshake."; **Frank (an old grudge)**, "you are careless and you have been cruel and vain and wanton." — with the last line written as a stage direction, "leaning in so only Roman can hear": **"I've eaten a bellyful for you, son. Go fuck yourself."** Comparable: the show trial in S2E10 has everyone **write a brief against someone else** rather than defend themselves ("argue the brief").

**C7 Four kinds of mirror character and external referee**:

- **The mirror family**: the Pierces in S2E5 are the Roys' mirror — the same anxiety about succession in different clothing (grace before meals, the Latin motto over the door "In veritate triumpho," the house wine, reciting *Richard II*); the more orderly the family across the table, the louder the Roys' silence.
- **Mirrored incapacity**: S3E5 juxtaposes two incapacitated old men — Sandy, after his stroke, "translated" by his daughter, and Logan in UTI delirium, spoken for by his children.
- **The external referee**: Josh Aaronson (S3E4, "So can you work together?"), Mencken, Matsson; their function is **to force the protagonists to say what they would never normally say**, and then leave the truth of the lines suspended — in S3E4 Logan says in public, "He's a good kid and I love him… maybe the best one of all of them", and the script then uses a whole paragraph of stage direction to write the ambiguity of the look that follows ("Did he mean it… Or was it the opposite – disdain.").
- **The weakest person naming the game's logic**: Greg's "I thought you got spared if you told the truth? That seemed to be the rule?"; Willa's "Sell seems cool?"; the victim Kira's "Oh, okay, here it comes!". **The joke is the theme.**

**C8 The graduations of the physical-decline clock**: S3's motif — in E1 Logan struggles to bend and pick up a thrown phone ("All is not well. He's alone."); in E4 he is heat-sick, vomiting and twists an ankle on the death march on the island; in E5 he is delirious with a UTI; in E9 he reads his grandson a children's book about death (*Goodbye Mog*) and, halfway through, decides it is unlucky and switches books. The S1 version: Logan practising walking inside a taped square on the floor, "Walking's not good. It's fucking **normal**."

## 4. Source Examples for Dialogue and Stage Directions (D1–D13)

**D1 Stage directions carrying subtext and "maybe"**:

- "True? Or passive-aggressive? Kendall sort of wants his dad to tell him what to do, but doesn't want to ask for direction." (S1E1)
- "There is subtext."; "Yes. But maybe no." (S2)
- "But Logan is not happy, not at all." (S2E6)
- "Both a real moment and a highly manipulated and manipulative one." (S4E06, **refusing the binary**)
- Interiority written as a question: "When does Kendall think, 'I don't need to have been here'?" (S1E10)
- The S3 "maybe" culture (Prebble): "Now I always keep the maybes in."

**D2 Turning a reversal on one word**: in the S2 finale, Kendall at the press conference **delivers the compliant version in full** — "it has been suggested I would be a suitable figure to absorb the anger and concern" — and then says a single word: **"But."**

**D3 The trigger must be a word established earlier**: the trigger in S2E10 is **NRPI** ("No Real Person Involved"). In S2E9 it is internal company jargon; in S2E10 in his father's cabin it becomes a word of comfort to a son — "Nah NRPI, kiddo. You're a good kid." — the father thinks he is granting absolution and is in fact classifying his son as "not a real person," so **the absolution and the insult are the same sentence.**

**D4 Recycling a comic plant as evidence**: "Mo Lester" is an in-house joke in the early episodes of S2, and read out word for word by a senator at the S2E9 hearing it becomes an accusation — "What was it a joke about? 'Mo Lester'?" — and the more the defendant tries to escape via "it was a joke," the more it proves systemic acquiescence.

**D5 Samples of aphorisms about power given to supporting characters**: Frank's "You are what you do." / "I am just an attendant lord"; Gerri's sin-cake eater; "Young cardinals vote for old popes"; Stewy translating the epic into "a few extra cents on their dollar. **And that's all this is.**"

**D6 Four denials as four miniature biographies** (the cut card-table scene in S2E10, written not as a confession): Kendall's "We knew."; Roman's "Well I didn't know."; Shiv's "What are you saying we knew?"; Connor's "We kind of knew."; and finally Shiv's "I did not know what I was laughing about," the season's most precise character definition.

**D7 Letting the protagonist lie successfully**: in S1E9 Tom asks "Are you fucking around on me?", with the stake written into the stage direction — "If she looks away, he'll know. But she doesn't." She lies successfully, and so Tom volunteers every detail of the cruise scandal to her, including "Greg's expendable."

**D8 Turning a story on a polite question**: the season's biggest turn in S3E9 (Matsson proposing the acquisition) is written as "…if you want to tell me to fuck off, tell me to fuck off?" — and Logan replies, "You're not fucking serious."

**D9 Letting the adversary deliver the protagonist's interior monologue**: the next line of that scene, Matsson's "You're thinking, 'Every bit of me wants to tell him to fuck off and eat shit except for the bit that knows every word he says is true.'" — saving a whole scene of introspection while establishing the new adversary's intellectual level.

**D10 Three ways comedy carries tragedy**:

- (a) **Catch the darkest confession with a family joke**: in S3E9 Roman knocks the killing down a grade at a time to "at worst you're a **manslaughter-er**," and the script's own note reads "It is so dark that it is kind of funny."
- (b) **Demote an undebatable large issue into a debatable small one**: S4E08's "So because we had so much chicken when we were kids, I have to elect a fascist?" / "Yeah. Basically."
- (c) **Use comedy to complete the mourning**: the obituary codebook game in S4E04 — "'A complicated man'" → "Threw phones at underlings."
- The principle (Frank Rich): "drama or comedy" is a false question; the jokes grow out of the characters' own psychology rather than from a bystander's satire; "The show is a human comedy, not a satire" — **you laugh with them first, and then you cry with them.**

**D11 Escalating a quarrel to the most vulnerable point**: in the S4E07 balcony fight every line moves closer to the other's most vulnerable point, landing on "you are maybe not a good person to have children." — while the audience knows Shiv is pregnant and Tom does not; the stage direction says only, "A shift at this partly successful attempt to actually hurt her."

**D12 Three samples of the ellipsis as theme**: in S4E09 two old retainers finish talking about Logan and look at each other — **"Right?" "Right."**; in S4E08 Darwin's caveats never air; the title of S1E10 comes from Berryman's 'Dream Song 29', but the footnote says letting the poem play out of a radio was "far too direct" — **keep the poetic source in the title and delete it from the picture.**

**D13 Rewriting a relationship as a physical action when there are no new lines**: in S4E10 Kendall's embrace tears Roman's stitches open ("the stitches tear a little, blood seeps"), and the dialogue is only "I love you, man." / "I fucking hate you." The show's final passage: Tom extends his palm, "Not eager but available… **no squeeze. Just contact.** … A queen and a prince. **Two bombs being transported with great care.**"

## 5. The Room and Production: Footnotes and First-Hand Evidence

**Writing long (matching section 5, item 1)**: "I like to write long and I encourage my fellow writers to write long. I rewrite long." The reason: a few extra **dialogue pages** (not car crashes) "return exponential rewards," and only then does the edit have room to refocus; writing long also lets you **avoid choosing**, preserving those "small or weird or nothingy" moments.

**The wall and the mega-chart (matching section 5, item 4, Prebble)**: a room of 8–12 people, British and American mixed, comedy and tragedy backgrounds mixed, five to six hours a day. Large sheets headed by **character name**, with ideas written underneath — "Afraid of sharks?" under Roman, "Beaten Dog" under Kendall in S2 and "**Goes Beyond**" in S3; surviving entries go onto the **mega-chart** — characters across, episodes down, and "The basic beats of the season were plotted within."

**Walking into the room with a fixed season end (matching section 5, item 5)**: before S3, only three things were locked — the siblings would join forces, Tom had to inform, and "how we got there was totally up for grabs."

**Pitching from the trashy version (matching section 5, item 6, Prebble)**: open with "This is the trashy version" / "Not this, but maybe – Something like this.", never "This is shit but –" ("Pitching bestows terrible privilege on the articulate."). **The generation chain of S3E7 is the template**: suicide in public → performance art faking a suicide → deciding not to go on stage and not to sing. "**Pitching is the way in which you work out what the show isn't, as well as what it is.**"

**The two ordering rules (matching section 5, item 7)**: "An appeal to reality over the mythic was always the most powerful pitch in the room, only occasionally losing out to whatever was the most funny." A sample argument: whether Logan would sell the company — "Murdoch literally did it, it's more real!"

**The "does two things" cutting test (matching section 5, item 8)**: Tom not going to prison was kept because it does three things at once — showing how the system works, advancing the marriage crisis, and producing the funniest scene. **Anything doing fewer than two is cut.**

**The footnote evidence table: page → floor → edit, with the last two entitled to veto the first (matching section 5, item 9)**

| Episode | The change | The reason the footnote gives | Which stage was vetoed |
|---|---|---|---|
| S4E02 | The fake cancer cut | "a stratagem too far" | Page |
| S4E09 | Gerri's whole comforting passage deleted | "in the cut he seemed too far gone to be reached" — **the character's state no longer admits comfort** | The edit vetoes the page |
| S4E10 | The signing scene rewritten | "Kieran really didn't feel Roman would go into the big room to sign" | The floor vetoes the page |
| S4E09 | Matsson's car | Forced by availability, and better for it | A production constraint improves the page |
| S3E8 | The entire nanny line cut | The reason is only "the episode was just too long" | The edit |
| S3E6 | Roman does not take drugs | Kieran Culkin believed Roman does not take drugs and worked around any script implication | The actor vetoes the page |
| S3E3 | Tom's "Divorce" cut | "cast too heavy a shadow over subsequent events" | The edit (see S9) |
| S2E10 | The Roman–Gerri restaurant scene cut | Material overload; in S3E1 it "find[s] a spot to nestle more comfortably" | The edit plus migration (see S10) |

The general principle of the edit room: "the edit generates its own powerful centrifugal force – throwing off material which is further from the show's core questions and concerns."

**The distribution of the footnotes is itself information**: S1's 15 footnotes cluster in E1 (6), E3 (5), E2 (2) and E10 (2), with almost none in E7–E9 — **the episodes that shot most smoothly have no footnotes.** A footnote is a reason for a change, not a statement of intent.

**Leaving the final image to the floor (matching section 5, item 10)**: the S3E9 footnote, "At script, I didn't know what the final image would be."; the S4 introduction, "You won't find the end of the show, as it went to air, written in these scripts. **That we found on the day.**"

**Support systems and firefighting (matching section 5, item 11)**: consultants divided by field — Merissa Marr on business moves, Jon Klein on media, Jesse Eisinger and Matt Friedrich on law, Tracey Pruzan and Derek Blasberg on New York society, Eric Schultz and Ben Ginsberg on politics; firefighting was done by a fixed handful of writers — "to help me write – and to rewrite – when we had an episode that wasn't firing."

**What a showrunner is (matching section 5, item 12)**: "essentially a writer. But a writer empowered to protect the scripts through the rooms where they are conceived and written and rewritten, performed, captured and edited." Running a room is **throwing a party**; the room was in London rather than New York for "a sense of having been forgotten about"; **2–3 writers were on set every day**; and the soundproofing was bad enough that key plot points got code names (one in S4 was "Larry David").

**Characters rewritten because of actors (matching section 5, item 14, Frank Rich)**: Gerri was originally a man, "Jerry"; Hugo, Matsson and Kerry were expanded because of the performances; Sarah Snook's pregnancy generated a mid-season story line in S4 — on condition that "the series' architecture could support it without buckling the overall design."

## 6. The Chain of Evidence for the Ending Engineering

**1 Engine pressure loss**: not that inspiration ran out ("the motor in the room was still running hot") but that **"the big plot engine, the brutal materialist plumbing which ran under the show, was looking to me like it would eventually start to lose pressure."** The outside world supplied a deadline at the same time: legacy media's war against tech "was not a fair fight, it was a rout"; Redstone died, Murdoch retreated.

**2 Three season shapes decided by a vote of the writers**: Brixton, December 2021, with "one final season of ten episodes, or two of six or eight episodes" pinned to the wall. Will Tracy wanted one season; Jon Brown wanted two (he had always thought five was the right shape); Tony Roche wanted to continue but thought creatively it should end; Lucy Prebble decided — "**the ten-episode season was the muscular way to go out.**"

**3 S4's four anchors**: ① Logan dies early and suddenly, "a blast from the captive bolt pistol"; ② the children experience it by phone at a wedding; ③ the GoJo deal **must close** — "That was the case in the world, so it had to be the case for us."; ④ Tom wins (from S2 on he "started to glitter in the distance").

**4 The eight steps of an off-screen death** (S4E03; Armstrong says this passage "came in a tumble, the long middle section barely revised after its first draft"):

1. The information comes from one unreliable mouth — Tom's "I wasn't there," with the medical authority an anonymous "MD Live," which he then gets wrong as "MD Line."
2. Someone else turns "saying goodbye" into an action — "speak to him… in case it's a last chance."
3. Three "by his ear" monologues, each ≤8 lines and unanswered, each a compression of that character's season arc, with the emotion carried by a technical repeated line — "Am I by his ear?"
4. The ladder of denial — "Medically, right." → "five, three percent possible" → "What if it's a drill?"
5. Even informing people is an act of power — "do we definitely tell him?"
6. Physically separating them from the body — the boat has left the shore.
7. Officializing the death is a string of technical checkpoints — CPR for 25/30 minutes → "They've stopped the CPR." → "Is he covered?" → the share price → the announcement.
8. The irony comes from everyone simply doing their own job — Karolina builds the timeline, Frank writes "my version," Tom has Greg delete the J drive.

**5 The ellipsis is the theme**: the method is to lay the setup, hold the tone, build the world correctly, and then "**step back from the mechanism, let it run.**" His definition of satire is one sentence: "Taking a hard look at the world as it is." — and "afflicting the comforted… will go better if you avoid announcing your intention too clearly at the door."

**6 The regressive game** (the three siblings in the S4E10 kitchen mixing the new king a "meal fit for a king": mayo, cocoa powder, sugar, Branston Pickle, milk, a great deal of Tabasco, and Shiv spits in it). Four points:

- (a) The three of them are in the same intention for the first and last time, and every joke is **cooperative**;
- (b) The mother's stinginess ("don't eat Peter's cheese, darling") supplies the material to imitate;
- (c) The one time anyone tries to say something tender ("We're gonna be alright, right?") they are immediately interrupted ("Drink it and shut the fuck up!") — **that unfinished pause is the trailer for the ending**;
- (d) The spit is the smallest rehearsal of the betrayal.

**7 The ghost phone**: on the S4E10 terrace Kendall picks up an imaginary phone and answers it — "Ghost phone. Spooky. … He's saying, yeah it is Roman. Okay, Dad. What— Kill … Shiv …" — and Shiv plays along; and then the same prop carries the sincerity — "He fucking promised it to me. Promised. When I was seven." / "No. He shouldn't have said that."

**8 Letting the show "not know it is ending"**: multi-season versions were kept alive through writing and shooting ("Almost so that the show itself didn't know it was ending"), which avoids an ending that is "freeze-dried and pre-packaged" and avoids "the metal gears of LA business" crushing every possibility early.

**9 *Logan's Dinner*: writing the most moving passage as a "cuttable background layer."** The S4 appendix is one whole scene written last, and at first it was only "a bit of additional flavour… which might or might not make the cut" on a television in the background of E10. Each guest gets a "party piece": Logan recites in rhyme **a list of losing presidential candidates** — his mnemonic stores only the losers; Gerri's "My party piece has been keeping you out of jail for twenty years."; Karl sings Burns's Green Grow the Rashes, and Logan covers the emotion with "You're fucking murdering it, man." His first line is "**Film it!**" and his last is "I'd like to have it with me always— in case I go fucking rat-catching."

**10 The open method is a method, not vagueness**: quoting Darcey Bussell — what draws you in is not flawless execution but "the fear that she might be on the verge of falling"; a showrunner's power should be used to protect the gaps, or you write something "closed-off, invulnerable… every scene sleek and sealed" — "**the cracks are where the light gets in.**"

### 10.2 The Four Notes on the Text Compared

| Volume | What the Note on the Text records | What it proves |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | The position: a script is "only a recipe" (a recipe – a set of instructions for action and suggested dialogue), but must have coherence and integrity in itself. **Seven causes of difference, ordered by likelihood**: ① the edit (the counter-measure being to write long, 70+ pages an episode); ② alts (pick 0–15 lines a day or two before shooting and send them to 3–5 writers); ③ late changes (written in by ballpoint); ④ fog of war (small changes of word order; an actor's paraphrase counts as an "implied note"); ⑤ improvisation (one freebie take after the scripted version); ⑥ ADR (clarifying plot, adjusting details, adding jokes); ⑦ the remembrance of things past (reordering after the read-through; actors stitch deleted fragments back in) | The ordering is itself the power table of the three stages: **the edit ranks first, and the page only reaches as far as "write long"** |
| S2 / S3 / S4 (identical across all three) | What is printed is **the script as it stood when shooting began** ("as they stood when we started to film"); only **three causes** of difference from the broadcast version are given: cut for length in the edit / new lines given by the author and colleagues on the floor / actor improvisation. Footnotes are added only where the reason for a change is not self-evident, with research sources noted in passing. The S2 volume reports 12 footnotes | The three causes correspond to the last two of the three stages; **a footnote is a reason for a change, not a statement of intent**; and three volumes using identical wording shows this is an institution rather than an accident. Corroborated by the S4 introduction: "the final draft of the show isn't the script. It's the version we air." |

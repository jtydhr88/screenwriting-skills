# Japanese Screenwriting Methods (日本编剧方法)

Practical paths for Japanese original screenplays, drawn from *Screenwriting from Zero: Ten Japanese Directors and Screenwriters on the Script* (Tomari Takahiro, editor) and *Story Creation Masterclass, International Volume* (Arai Haruhiko). For Korean (Oh Seung-wuk, Choi Seok-hwan) and French (Aubitan) screenwriters from the same masterclass, see `sw-korean-french-screenwriting`. Relationship to the Hollywood system: **Kosawa Ryota, Uchida Kenji, and Kakei Masaya acknowledge that the three-act / Introduction, Elaboration, Turn, Resolution framework is the most effective structure, but they "probe why good works take a three-act form"; Miki Satoshi, Sono Sion, Yokohama Satoko, Takahashi Izumi, and Fukuda Yuichi take the alternate path—theme placed later, skipping plot outlines, linking fragments, placing characters before plot events; Yukisada Isao treats the screenplay as a "coordinate axis" and "material for debate."** For Ozu Yasujiro's screenplay examples, see `ozu-screenplay-style`; editor's closing word: "Do not depend on adapted screenplays; win the day with original works." For worksheets and plot outline examples, see [reference.md](reference.md).

## 1. Two Schools of Method: Structure-First vs. Character/Fragment-First

| | Structure-First School | Character/Fragment-First School |
|---|---|---|
| Representatives | Kosawa Ryota, Uchida Kenji, Kakei Masaya | Miki Satoshi, Sono Sion, Yokohama Satoko, Takahashi Izumi, Fukuda Yuichi, Omiya Ellie |
| Starting Point | Subject compass, setting, "if + moreover", ending | Small-material notebooks, actors, character resumes/diaries, personal experience |
| Theme | Fixed before writing or pulled out by subject | "Surfaces unintentionally" when 70–80% written (waiting for herring in Rumoi) |
| Plot Outline | Mandatory; draw framework/tables/A4 full-series grid first | None or minimal ("the moment you write a plot outline, the screenplay loses explosive power") |
| Cost | Writing by structure is not necessarily interesting | Struggling to bridge scene transitions feels like "pure hell" |

The editor: "The humanities have the humanities' way; the sciences have the sciences' way. The right-brain camp relies on the right brain; the left-brain camp relies on the left brain." Choose a path that fits you, but both camps agree: **finish one script first above all** (Miki: "Turn the toy box upside down and write one first!"); **show it to others, because being told it is 'boring' is where real meaning begins** (Sono Sion / Fukuda).

## 2. Structure and Pacing

- **Three acts = Introduction, Elaboration, Turn, Resolution** (Kosawa): Introduction clarifies character relationships; Elaboration unfolds contradictions and conflicts (**if "Elaboration" is brilliant, you can write a great screenplay; the more "Elaboration," the more variations**); Turn and Resolution merge into the climax. Copying the script of *Seven Samurai* and reverse-engineering TV drama recordings into screenplays were his learning methods.
- **The moment the protagonist sets a goal = Plot Point** (Uchida): In *E.T.*, hiding E.T. at home; in *Die Hard*, grabbing the machine gun. Think in four segments divided into 30-minute blocks. College teacher: "Never mistakenly assume that creating according to a three-act structure produces great work; what we must probe is why good works end up in a three-act structure."
- **Determine the ending first** (Uchida): "Arriving at the airport without having considered your destination first"; for Sono Sion's *Love Exposure*, the initial thoughts were also two scenes (persuading the sister at the beach, the sister coming to the rescue at the psychiatric hospital), then working backward through the first half.
- **Sense of speed and jumping** (Kosawa): Several storylines developing in intercut fashion with continuous cutting creates speed; **a boring scene = a scene without a sense of jumping**, delete it or inject new ideas to make it significant; "jump two to three steps at once," making the next scene's content the direct opposite of the previous scene's; let the next line of dialogue negate the previous line; plot swings wildly left and right (influenced by *LOST* and *Evangelion*).
- **End exposition as early as possible** (Miki): "In my works, the exposition portion moves very fast"; using "Milo" chocolate milk as plant-and-payoff preparation for *Instant Swamp*, opening with a 2.5-minute, 108-shot monologue.
- **Idling and fanning the flames** (Miki): Gilliam / *Twin Peaks* style "idling while fanning the flames," "movies become far more interesting when they contain more seemingly useless idling."
- **The 10-minute rule** (Fukuda / Hasumi): An incident must happen within 20 minutes for a movie and within 10 minutes for a TV drama, letting the audience foresee what will happen in this episode.
- **TV drama practical craft** (Miki / Kosawa): One reel (to the commercial break) is roughly 10 minutes; if there are 7 events in 10 minutes, outline 7 points and mark the commercial break; map the entire series structure on a single sheet of A4 before writing (a plant in Episode 2 pays off in Episode 5); ensemble dramas where one case runs through the whole series (*Gonzo: Densetsu no Keiji*); draw the framework of "order that must be presented beforehand" first; fixed routines ease viewer comprehension (Section Chief Kinoshita's ramen reception in every episode).
- **No need to give the protagonist a goal / No need to grow** (Miki): "Must human beings constantly make progress? A fool who remains a fool from beginning to end and experiences zero growth can be fascinating precisely because of that"; *The Three Losers* "spend their whole lives on summer vacation"; *Turtles Are Surprisingly Fast Swimmers* takes *Shane* as its foundation (the departure of the spy friend), "a story where nothing happens."
- **Anti-theory** (Yokohama): "A sense of imbalance should be more interesting"; "if you write strictly according to theory, you cannot produce content that exceeds other people's imagination"; the cost is that transitions become brutally difficult; show the concept to the producer first.
- **Deviation within repeated daily life** (Yukisada): Ozu-style "since we have come here, let us begin shooting from here, and then repeat it," cherry blossom fork roads, changing lanes due to others—"the screenplay's most secretive flavor"; "what I want to depict above all is the richness that does not exist within repetitive daily life."

## 3. Raw Material and Conceptualization

- **One note a day** (Miki / Yokohama / Kakei): Record interesting/curious matters; review 180 notes after half a year to discover what draws you; sketchbook doodle character portraits + keywords (Kosawa); outliner software (Acta/Tree) to arrange and restructure notes into chapters; "turn the toy box upside down" by dumping all materials out to rearrange and find hidden themes; Fukuda takes the opposite view: "Rather than taking notes, engraving them in your heart is better," comedy is a memory game.
- **"If" + "Moreover"** (Kakei): Concept = addition of two things far apart—"beautiful woman" + "canned food", "live football commentary" + "injury time before death", "swapping rooms" + "previous tenant's lover" + "pro wrestler moving crew", "men" + "synchronized swimming" (*Waterboys*); **"Moreover" allows coincidence to be accepted, whereas "Furthermore" spoils the mood**; "if" alone is not enough; mixing in disparate content is the true entry point. Fukuda: "You must have two materials that spark inspiration; having only one always feels lacking."
- **Start from the title** (Miki / Kosawa): The title should make people ask "what is that about?" (*Instant Swamp*, *Burst City*, *Tetsuo: The Iron Man*); "A Love Letter at 60" is an entirely fresh love story purely from the title; familiar form + brand-new execution = "**pseudo-deja vu**" (Miki); Sono Sion: tell the logline to others first, and if there is no reaction, abandon it; for *Suicide Club* and *Love Exposure*, people laughed the moment the titles were spoken.
- **Analyze "what it is mainly about"** (Miki): *Shane* = "meeting someone, they depart, and returning to ordinary life" -> *Turtles*; *The Godfather* = son inheriting father's business -> "poop factory"; "those who struggle with story creation can try using 'what it is mainly about' as a clue to conceptualize." Watch old movies to learn structure; watch favorite genres all the way to the end (Sono Sion's horror films).
- **Summarize in one or two sentences** (Uchida): *Rocky* = "a third-rate boxer wins the right to challenge the champion and fights him to a draw"; *After School* = "a man helps his first love"; if you cannot do this, the middle has definitely gone off course.
- **Altering the time axis costs nothing** (Uchida): "No matter how you alter the time axis, it doesn't cost a single cent"—the secret weapon of low-budget film; "an ordinary vase cannot sell, but say 'press it and it glows' and it might."
- **Field research**: Sono Sion interviewed high school girls, one introducing the next (hair extensions, nosebleeds); Lee Chang-dong walks to gather material, "driving makes you stop feeling like a writer"; Kakei interviewed furniture designers and architects, "precisely because you do not fabricate reality, you can spin wildly imaginative, magnificent lies"; Yokohama encountered a bear and put it into the final shot; Yukisada's blender goldfish, "who did he go with again?"
- **Personal experience is a breakthrough point, not something to copy verbatim** (Yokohama): "Experience does not equal a movie; you must consider how to apply experience to the movie to expand the story" (manure pit, dodgeball, decapitation accident, "God"); Sono Sion joining a cult, fleeing to an extremist base, "all because of food"; Arai Haruhiko: "**What you must do is expose the things you are ashamed of to others**"; Miki: "Embarrassing parts carry powerful infection."
- **Theme placed later** (Miki / Uchida / Sono): Realizing only around Episode 7 or 8 that "the characters appearing in this drama are all waiting for something" -> Rumoi is a city waiting for herring; *In the Pool*: "There is no need for all three people to be healed"; changing names to "Suzume" and "Kujaku" revealed the breakthrough (fantasy type); **once the theme surfaces, cut extraneous shots and lines according to it**, "despise preachiness"; wait for keywords while writing, "take it slowly." Omiya takes the opposite route: the theme can be spoken aloud by the protagonist at the end ("a suspension of business is a preparation period for reopening"), admitting it is "unsophisticated" yet potent; Takahashi: "Craft directive lines or shots" as peaks rather than obscuring the theme.

## 4. Character

- **Character resumes and diaries** (Uchida): Write the protagonist's resume, a three-day diary during the story ("running away here is too cowardly" -> "deceive the opponent before fleeing" -> reverse-engineer plants), imagine "what if they were classmates"; "what the audience sees is merely an island protruding above the sea; hidden beneath the water is a mountain-sized mass of 'wasted effort.'"
- **Characters form the "Introduction" only after gestation and maturation** (Sono Sion): Clumsy writers consider ornate prose before figuring out their characters; as long as a character is interesting, let them steal the lead's scene (Picasso's background overpowering the flowers).
- **Premise determines character** (Kakei): Reverse-engineer profession and personality from the premise—a beautiful woman crawling out of a can requires an unsuspecting theater youth; injury time before death requires a documentary photographer who has a reason to die; "Nobita's helplessness is precisely what gives Doraemon value"; fixed image + reversal ("seems serious, actually a villain") is sufficient, "archetypes are the entryway to the next stage."
- **Character = actor + flaw** (Fukuda): First consider what "visible flavor" the actor can bring to this drama; **start from the flaw** (Spider-Man cannot shoot webs once a woman glances at him; Batman has no flaws and thus cannot be loved); 80% excellence, 20% flaw; decide whether to "showcase uselessness" or "hint at hidden excellence"; desperate striving (especially desperate lying) makes people endearing; villains are both wicked and foolish (the pirate trio).
- **Write "personal character" rather than constructing "a role"** (Takahashi Izumi): "If he were a scholar, people would say 'documents line the room,' but I think 'there should be two volumes of *Doraemon* sitting there'"; focus on the person, not the plot event; look at the "heat" emitted by the character (falsely booking 100 people to escape a company trip).
- **Build characters from visual contrast** (Miki): A punk rocker sitting cross-legged on the subway gives up his seat to an elderly woman -> "Gus"; **names determine characters** (Suzume / Kujaku; Kinoshita "Konosuke"; Omiya: "Names determine character roles, so I am usually very cautious").
- **Trio dynamics** (Miki): "In a one-on-one setup, it is hard to judge which value system is correct and hard to turn the plot; once the dynamic among three people shifts, the plot can turn right along with their values."
- **Incorporate outsiders / onlookers** (Kosawa): In *Gaiji Keisatsu*, the female officer muddleheadedly selected lets the audience see how extraordinary the organization is; the "despicable hero."
- **Let characters appear while writing** (Takahashi): "Just as I was thinking 'Teacher Kobayashi won't show up,' he appeared; since he appeared, let's give him something to do"; Fukuda: "Creating the story while shifting character roles," "I welcome going off course wholeheartedly."
- **Arrange characters like planting a flowerbed / prepping hotpot ingredients** (Omiya); contrasting characters (Section Chief Kimijima vs. Section Chief Kinoshita).
- **Villains**: Sono Sion: "No one is one hundred percent bad" (for Korean screenwriters' methods on villains, see `sw-korean-french-screenwriting`).

## 5. Dialogue

- **Arising from inner dialogue with oneself** (Omiya): "To explore my own feelings, I used many words to express them," "phrases generated from an hour or a month of inner dialogue turn into the dialogue of the script"; do not reference song lyrics (it turns into a karaoke video); determine color tone and onomatopoeia first (sunset glow / bang).
- **Preserve ambiguity, value acoustic resonance** (Yokohama): "Uncertain whether it is true or false," allowing different interpretations for different people; "valuing acoustic resonance over literal meaning"; **state only what happened without saying 'sad'** (strip away sentimentality); long speeches that "voice psychological trauma" read like poetry.
- **Write dialogue first to generate story** (Fukuda): After nailing down characters, write dialogue first, creating while shifting character personas; "hand the straight-man role over to the audience" so conversation never stalls (otherwise "setup, punchline, setup, punchline" exhausts the viewer); with a triple-fool dynamic where "everyone is an idiot," dialogue can still carry forward.
- **Authentic dialogue** (Arai): "Don't hit my face, because I am an actress" originated from an ex-girlfriend; under the trend toward concise dialogue, what matters more is writing each individual scene well so that "I love you" moves people through its preparation.
- **Adapting dialogue** (Takahashi): Manga dialogue functions because it leans on drawings; adapting it to the screen requires rewriting; novel prose must be replaced with spoken vernacular; invent fresh dialogue without altering the meaning; turn passive protagonists into active ones.
- **Directive dialogue** (Takahashi): Use a specific line or shot as a mountain peak to broaden the wilderness at the foot.
- **Regional accents and catchphrases** (Miki / Omiya): Kinoshita: "Time to go home," "Either one is fine," "It's nothing, won't kill you"; for *Fargo*-style onomatopoeia, see `sw-american-case-studies`.
- Dialogue read-through: Read aloud once after writing or have people act out parts in a simulated rehearsal (for Korean and French table read methods, see `sw-korean-french-screenwriting`).

## 6. Comedy and Gags (Fukuda / Miki / Choi)

- **The gag system**: Allocating the boke, tsukkomi, and natural boke (*33-Minute Detective*: Inspector Otahara serves as the "great boke" placing absolute faith in the detective; the assistant chimes in); hand the tsukkomi over to the audience; fixed formats (evergreen line "let me stretch this out to a full 33 minutes", moving camera shots every episode) preserve an "invisible tsukkomi."
- **The detective says things that must never be said**: "This and that is just this and that!" "Twins are accomplices!"—assert first, advance later; borrow classic mystery history tropes to inject informative flavor.
- **What is a sense of reality?** Fukuda: "Where does a sense of reality come from? From daily life? Or from TV dramas seen in the past?"—splashing water -> spraying insecticide -> paramedic secret filming, expanding scope from the news and others' absurd actions, "shift perspective and ask with love why they did that"; "a writer having a narrow range of reality is never something to take pride in."
- **One crying beat is enough, but laughing beats must be multiple**; if one out of 100 viewers does not laugh, you want to ask "where was it boring?"
- Miki's "datsuryoku-kei" (slacker/absurdist): Gags take priority over story; a housewife becomes a spy; stairs that only go down and never up; "swapping a black T-shirt for a white T-shirt" loses something vital—**major oversights begin in tiny details** (*Bonnie and Clyde*'s parallel parking).
- Arai: "Out of ten punchlines, you are doing well if the audience catches three or four; deliver many."

## 7. Original vs. Adaptation, Constraints and Partnerships

- **Originals are born from "situations"** (Yukisada): Apparel maker wants models in clothes -> a funeral film where everyone wears mourning dress; band lyrics -> burning textbooks; shampoo commercial -> three films with Shibasaki Kou playing a wicked woman; mobile phone commercial -> end-of-life videos; "**work after establishing constraints that fit you; content that limits freedom instead spawns creativity; 'please create freely' is the most troublesome command**." Uchida: "Being allowed to cook with only cabbage and eggs is much simpler"; Kosawa: "The stronger the restrictions and lack of freedom, the more interesting content takes shape."
- **A moderately flawed screenplay is a good screenplay** (Yukisada citing Kamoshita Shinichi): Leaving room for imagination excites staff: "I want to do this"; write in things that might get cut and ask "is this necessary?", "the screenplay exists for debate"; **partner with a screenwriter to "buy time"** (dictate -> they write -> adjust differences -> swap in strengths); "only when many people work on a script does its precision sharpen."
- **The screenplay is a coordinate axis** (Yukisada): A movie "lives three times and dies twice" (script -> shooting -> editing); if the central axis is weak, you lose sight of the objective; originality = method of depiction (Kitano Takeshi's ellipses) rather than the premise; do not fear "slight resemblances," do not fear shallowness.
- **Adaptation insights** (Arai / Yukisada / Takahashi / Richards): Examine the original work from a critic's perspective: "An original work you love deeply is very hard to adapt because love numbs you"; keeping only the title is also viable (*W's Tragedy*: a play-within-a-play framing "an extra chasing dreams"); "what did you discover from the novel through your own perspective?" (Ito Chihiro: "Stepping forward one step makes a movie"); director tells the writer first which part moved them; "when adapting, the screenwriter must not know too much." Three people write by playing rock-paper-scissors to claim beginning, middle, and end, writing for one week each before stitching together (Arai).
- **Professional outlook** (Kosawa): The screenplay is "the personal work" of all stakeholders; treat requirements seriously; "a professional should re-create like threading a needle"; commercial success comes before freshness, social impact, or personal challenges; "write screenplays that can be converted into money"; knowing how to revise is an indispensable qualification; create works that "cannot be understood unless watched with full focus"; "the joy of making far exceeds the joy of viewing."
- **Tenacious willpower** (Takahashi): Newcomers write 3–5 scripts a year, 10 hours a day; possessing a lifelong theme ("pain" -> tenderness, with the pendulum swinging toward pain first) allows you to write anything.
- **Sono Sion**: "If a work cannot provoke intense revulsion... it cannot count as genuinely great work"; "there is nothing that cannot be done" (excessive monologues, 4 hours, opening title card dropping an hour in); compare structure to a tree: branches and leaves -> trunk -> roots; construct structure first or advance with serialized bravado; "devoted to the original"—only without an original source's guarantee is it truly fresh.
- **Korean and French counterpoints** see `sw-korean-french-screenwriting` (Oh Seung-wuk's undercover research and "truth-telling liars," Choi Seok-hwan's genre promises and twin reversals, Aubitan's "write dialogue last" and Italian-style table read).

## 8. Diagnostic Checklist

1. Am I structure-first or fragment-first? After choosing, have I finished at least one complete script?
2. Does the title make people ask "what is that about?" How did three people react when told the logline? Did you find the person who said it was "boring" and ask "where?"
3. Is the concept the addition of two things far apart? Did you use "Moreover" instead of "Furthermore"?
4. Is the ending fixed (even if just a single image first)? Is the "Elaboration" abundant and gripping enough? Does every scene have a sense of jumping? Does the next line of dialogue negate the previous one?
5. Did exposition end as early as possible? Is there an incident within 10 minutes? For TV drama, did you list 7 events for a 10-minute reel?
6. Characters: Did you write resumes/diaries? Did you reverse-engineer professions from the premise? Have you figured out the actor's flaws? "Personal character" over "a role" (those two volumes of *Doraemon* in the room)? Are names decided?
7. Material: Did you record one note a day? Are field research and personal experience used as breakthroughs rather than copied verbatim? Have you exposed the things you are ashamed of?
8. Theme: Are you letting it surface on its own (and cutting excess once it does), or fixing it beforehand? Is there any preachiness? Are there directive lines or shots?
9. Comedy: Are boke and tsukkomi allocated? Is the tsukkomi handed to the audience? Does your "sense of reality" come solely from dramas you have watched?
10. Constraints: What is the binding restriction this time (budget, location, actor schedule, client requirements)? Have you wielded it as a weapon?
11. Does the screenplay leave room for debate and alteration? Will staff members feel excited reading it?
12. Must the protagonist have a goal or undergo growth? Is the version without growth more truthful? Have the repetition and deviation of daily life been captured?

## 9. Workflow (Original Japanese Screenplay)

1. **The Toy Box**: Dump out six months of notes; circle "If..." and the shots/images you want to film most; identify two raw materials that can be added together.
2. **Title and One Sentence**: Write a title that makes people ask "what is that about?"; summarize in one or two sentences; tell it to three people.
3. **Decide the Path**: Structure-first -> fix ending, draw four-frame / A4 series grid, write plot outline; fragment-first -> write character resumes/diaries, write dialogue first, advance with serialized bravado.
4. **Characters**: Reverse-engineer professions from setting; actor + flaw; names; outsiders / onlookers; trio dynamics; contrasting characters.
5. **Plot Outline** (if writing): Objective descriptions, write "events" that move the protagonist (tiny ones suffice), do not write "he thinks this"; mark Introduction, Elaboration, Turn, Resolution; after reversals, loop back to plant clues.
6. **Screenplay**: Jumps, negating dialogue, early end to exposition, idling and fanning; dialogue drawn from inner conversations and authentic research; preserve ambiguity.
7. **Read**: Read aloud to harsh critics; find the person who says it is "boring"; delete excess once the theme surfaces.
8. **Project Proposal** (TV Drama): Logline / characters (catchphrases, mottos, POV characters, contrasting characters) / first two episode synopses + episode titles / planning objectives, highlights, and comp titles—"originals must be far more accessible than adaptations."
9. **Shoot**: Set a shoot date for independent films to force out the script; adapt to lack of money; leave room in the script for debate; accept that "films will change because of actors."

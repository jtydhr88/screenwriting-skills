## I. Egri's Three-Dimensional Character Framework Checklist

| Dimension | Items |
|---|---|
| Physiology | Sex, age, height and weight, hair color, eye color, skin tone, posture, appearance, defects, heredity |
| Sociology | Class, occupation (including attitude toward union/colleagues), education (favorite and worst subjects), family life (parents' situation, marital status), religion, race and nationality, community standing, political affiliation, amusements and reading |
| Psychology | Sex life and moral standards, **personal premise and ambitions**, frustrations and chief disappointments, temperament, attitude toward life, complexes, extroversion/introversion, abilities, qualities |

**The Case of Irene** (A girl from a respectable family who turns into a prostitute): 19 years old, shapely and attractive, sensitive to cold; the family fears tuberculosis while she believes she will die young and must therefore enjoy life; middle-class grocery store owner father; refuses housework and loves reading historical romances; mother was once abandoned, attempted suicide, then married a man she didn't love, keeping Irene under strict surveillance; below-normal IQ; dancer who is admired and longs to go to New York; had sexual experience with Jimmie and was relieved rather than upset when he refused to marry her; "any sexual relationship is fine as long as you take care of yourself"; quick-tempered, vindictive, boastful, yet nursed her mother through illness until full recovery; superstitious about the number 13. Thesis = parents want her to marry into a good family; Antithesis = she wants to be a dancer; Synthesis = running away from home and ending up on the streets. **Why must she become a prostitute**: A hunchbacked, humble, or brilliant girl would never take this path; she is conceited, mature, sexually experienced, and too ashamed to admit defeat to return to a small town—"Your task as a dramatist is to eliminate all other possibilities."

**Checklist of Character Compositions** (For assembling characters): Ruthless = urgent pressing matter, no way out, iron determination, ambition, despair, trapped, fear of failure, intense emotion, obsession, self-centeredness, single-mindedness, thirst for revenge, opportunism, greed; Completely unenterprising = daydreams, lack of drive, laziness, aimlessness; Volatile temper = thoughtless toward others, irritable, nervous tension, impatience, easily frustrated, willful, spoiled, quick-witted; Self-righteous = fault-finding, paranoid, unreliable, inferiority complex, domineering, selfish, combative; Suspicious = lack of security, guilt, sneakiness, vanity, timidity, inferiority; Dogmatic = single standard, hidebound, upright and pious, obstinate, inflexible, rigid, fanatical (fanatics are always dogmatic, dogmatists need not be fanatical); Scoundrel = egotistical, unscrupulous, jealous, insecure, vain, fickle, reclusive, uncreative; Ambitious = rebellion against the status quo, craving recognition, asserting one's presence, insatiable, thirst for power, envy, need for control, ruthlessness.

**Checklist of Poles of Growth**: Othello love -> jealousy, murder, suicide; Hedda self-centeredness -> suicide; Macbeth ambition -> murder; The Cherry Orchard irresponsibility -> loss of estate; Hamlet doubt -> murder; Death of a Salesman illusion -> bitter truth; Dead End poverty -> crime; Waiting for Lefty doubt -> conviction; Cat on a Hot Tin Roof depression -> hope; The Iceman Cometh hope -> despair. *Professor Mamlock* in ten steps: Isolation -> still disbelieving even when his own class is targeted -> skeptical yet dogmatic -> terror upon realizing his blindness -> wanting to flee without knowing where -> despair -> joining the struggle -> underground organization -> defiance -> martyrdom in collective action.

## II. Egri's Four Conflict Types and Transition Examples

### Static vs Jumping vs Escalating
- **Static**: "Do you love me?"—"Oh, I don't know."—"When will you know?"—"Soon."—"How soon?"—"I don't know that either." (Can drag on for a month; both characters are the same type, lacking conviction).
- **Jumping**: "Do you love me?"—"I don't know."—"Don't be a fool, make up your mind."—"I have a good mind to slap your face." (She storms out).
- **Escalating** (*Ghosts*, Manders vs Mrs. Alving): "This is the outcome of your reading bad books" -> "My dear Pastor, you are mistaken. It was you yourself who first set me thinking, and I am deeply grateful to you" -> "Grateful to me?!" -> "You forced me into submitting to duty and obligation... when one knot came undone, the whole fabric loosened" -> "Is that the result of the hardest struggle of my life?" -> "Call it rather the greatest defeat of your life" -> "It was the greatest victory of my life, my victory over myself" -> "It was a crime against us both." A counter-attack must always be fiercer than the attack; if she had merely answered "Really?", the conflict would have gone cold immediately.

### The Ladder of Transition
Friendship -> disappointment -> annoyance -> irritation -> anger -> assault -> threat -> premeditation -> murder. Skipping disappointment and annoyance is "like cutting out the liver and lungs."

**Line-by-Line Breakdown of Kitty and Packard in *Dinner at Eight* (Irritation -> Rage)**: "I'm the boss in this house" (viewing chaos as the other party's state)—"Your first wife in Montana?" (counter-attack)—"Leave her out of this"—"A poor, pale, flat-chested creature" (finding the soft spot)—"Shut up" (wishing it to stop)—"No wonder she died so young"—"God damn you" (**a jumping conflict**)—"Cheap bargain... baggage check"—"Drunken father, jailbird brother... throw you down sixty steps"—Tina enters and is shoved out, purse hits the floor—"Pick it up" (transition complete, first genuine rage)—throwing the bracelet "You only gave me this bracelet for yourself" (striking off-target in blind rage)—"I dragged you out of the gutter"—"Dressing me up like a prize horse while leaving me all alone... never sent me a single flower" (hound catching the scent)—"Waiting for papa to come home?" (crisis)—"Did you think you were the only man I ever knew?" (climax)—"You don't like that, do you, Mr. Cabinet Minister"—"Tell me who he is"—"I wouldn't tell you if I died"—Tina: "Nobody's been here but the doctor" (killing two birds with one stone)—"I'll hire private detectives... choke him by the throat"—"You couldn't even get into the Astors' bathroom"—"You dirty rattlesnake... after tonight we are through" slam door.

**Misordered Diagnostic for *The Stevedore***: Florrie slaps Bill -> Bill: "Shut up" -> Her long threat ("I wrote a letter to Helen" is the most crucial revelation, yet placed at the start with zero reaction = static) -> Bill suddenly chokes and beats her (jumping). Correction: First, "Don't you think you can treat me like that and just walk away" -> Bill counters -> "I'm going to Helen" -> Bill threatens a beating -> "I wrote a letter to Helen" -> beating becomes fully motivated and believable.

### Crisis Progression in *A Doll's House*
Krogstad: "If I'm shoved into the gutter a second time, I shall take you with me" (crisis) -> Helmer: "Working alongside him is simply impossible" (climax) -> Nora panics (resolution); firing letter posted—"Call the letter back quickly"—"It's too late"; Krogstad sneaks in: "I will take no legal action for now"—"No matter how much money you offer, I will not surrender the bond"—"In case you were thinking of running away from husband and children... or doing something far worse"—"I have not the courage for that either"—"I have a letter to your husband in my pocket" [crisis begins]—"Before a year is out, the real manager will be Nils Krogstad" [crisis]—"Now I have the courage"—"Under the ice... transformed beyond recognition"—"Your reputation is completely in my hands"—hearing the letter drop into the letterbox [climax]—"Torvald, now we are lost" [resolution].

### Examples of Point of Attack
Oedipus resolving to discover the murderer; Hedda's open defiance; Macbeth hearing the prophecy and lusting for the crown; *Once in a Lifetime*, quitting a job to conquer Hollywood; *Bury the Dead*, corpses refusing to be buried; *Room Service*, the hotel manager demanding his brother-in-law settle the theater troupe's bill; *Faust*, selling his soul; *The Guardsman*, the husband disguising himself as a guardsman to test his wife's fidelity. Diagnosis of a student's one-act play: Premise is "Despair leads to success," but a shy boy is mocked, and a girl pities him and agrees to marry him—no pressing crisis; revised so the opening finds the boy waiting at the girl's house for her father, having sworn to kill himself if rejected -> life-and-death stakes on the line.

## III. Egri's Play Analysis Template (Appendix)

Evaluate systematically across thirteen criteria: **Premise / Leading Character / Character / Orchestration / Unity of Opposites / Point of Attack / Conflict / Transition / Growth / Crisis / Climax / Resolution / Dialogue**.

***Tartuffe***: Premise: "He who digs a pit for others shall fall into it himself"; Leading character: Tartuffe; Orgon: tyrannical, foolish, blindly credulous (motive left unexplained); Damis shows zero growth; Marianne is entirely static; Orchestration: Orgon and Tartuffe are a match made in heaven, while Dorine is Tartuffe's true ideal antagonist; Point of attack should be Orgon's decision to break the betrothal and marry his daughter to Tartuffe (the first half of Act I is purely static exposition); Transitions are well handled; Crisis: Elmire persuades Orgon to hide and eavesdrop; Climax: Tartuffe reveals his true colors and tries to evict them; Resolution: The King identifies the fugitive—**the royal deus ex machina weakens the premise's persuasive power**.

***Ghosts***: Premise: "The sins of the fathers are visited upon the children"; Leading character: Pastor Manders; **All characters are three-dimensional**; Finely orchestrated (Mrs. Alving's defiance vs. Manders's blind piety; Engstrand's wiles vs. Manders's unyielding righteousness; Regine's sharp independence vs. Engstrand's cunning); Unity of opposites: Preserving the late Captain's honorable name, preventing an incestuous sibling union; Act I serves as a masterclass in revealing character continuously through conflict; Crisis: Oswald resolves to marry Regine; Climax: Oswald's mental collapse; Resolution: Mrs. Alving frantically searches for the morphine.

## IV. Indick's Screenwriting Psychology Quick Reference

### Sigmund Freud
| Concept | Cinematic Parallel | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Eros / Thanatos | Love and hate, sex and violence, revenge | *Duel in the Sun*, *Natural Born Killers* |
| Oedipal Rivalry | Rivalry over love as the goal; dual rivalry (same antagonist for both trophy and woman) | *The Graduate*, *Tin Cup*, *Rocky* |
| Forbidden Fruit | Transgressive love ending in tragedy; antagonist must be unworthy for hero to "win the prize without punishment" | *Romeo and Juliet*, *Titanic*, *Unfaithful* (plot hole) |
| Helplessness / Role Reversal | Caretaker turns monstrous; attacks in bed, bathtub, or sickbed | *The Shining*, *Misery*, *Gaslight* |
| Id / Ego / Superego | Villain / Protagonist / Mentor | *Cape Fear*, *Star Wars* |
| Villain's Retribution | Fitting the crime, dying at the protagonist's hand, dramatized spectacle | *Die Hard* falling from the tower |
| Three Dimensions of the Anti-Hero | Self-serving goal -> moral awakening -> inner conflict | *Shane* |
| The Fallen Hero | Pursuing redemption only to achieve damnation | *Scarface*, *The Godfather* |
| Guilt Complex | Universal motivation, must be tied to a father figure or role model | *Spider-Man*, *Drugstore Cowboy* |
| Oral Stage | Smoking = beats/breathers + inner conflict; obesity = greed; addiction = conquerable obstacle | *Casablanca*, *Goodfellas*, *Rio Bravo* |
| Anal Stage | Obsessive neatness vs. slovenliness = comic pairing; repression vs. release | *The Odd Couple*, *Straw Dogs*, *Mutiny on the Bounty* |
| Phallic Stage | Weapon = status symbol; penis envy = female empowerment | Lightsaber, Excalibur; *G.I. Jane* |
| Genital Stage | Seducing the virgin; virgin hero | *Lolita*, *American Pie* |

**Defense Mechanisms** (unconscious to the character, apparent to the audience): Repression (*The Remains of the Day*), Denial (*Jaws* mayor; genuine belief = tragedy vs. deliberate deceit = villain), Identification (*High Noon* refusing to identify with the cowardly elders), Sublimation (*Like Water for Chocolate*; "the power of love" renders the unbelievable credible, but motivation must first be believed), Regression (*The Big Chill*, followed immediately by confessing secrets), Reaction Formation (*The Quiet Man* saying no with words while eyes say yes; Col. Fitts in *American Beauty*), Displacement (*Falling Down*, *My Darling Clementine*), Rationalization (Woody Allen), Projection (*The Treasure of the Sierra Madre*, *Gypsy*), Isolation of Affect (*Shane*, *The Searchers*), Freudian Slip (used only to reveal raw emotion), Humor (placed immediately before or after peak dramatic tension).

**Dream Mechanics**: Wish-fulfillment structure (Act I: dream comes true; first half of Act II: pure indulgence; conflict arises organically around "what inner conflict does this new identity trigger?"); Succubus/siren can embody any conflict; surreal atmosphere (fog, smoke, soft focus, odd angles, slow motion) makes audiences believe anything is possible; write an individual dream sequence for each main character.

### Erikson's Eight Stages
| Stage | Conflict | Cinematic Parallel |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Trust vs. Mistrust | Suspicious protagonist (*Chinatown*) -> leap of faith (*The African Queen*); gullible character learns to say no (*Romancing the Stone*) |
| 2 | Autonomy vs. Shame/Doubt | Rebel hero begins in subservience (*Spartacus*) |
| 3 | Initiative vs. Guilt | Guilt as a driving force (Scrooge) or as an obstacle (*Ordinary People*, Conrad) |
| 4 | Industry vs. Inferiority | Underdog theme (*Rocky*, *The Dirty Dozen*)—motivation anchors the structure |
| 5 | Identity vs. Role Confusion | Act I establishes identity; moratorium; identity cancellation (*Malcolm X*); sending the protagonist alone into the abyss (the cave in *The Empire Strikes Back*) |
| 6 | Intimacy vs. Isolation | Intimacy = exposing secrets; screwball comedy intimate vignettes (*It Happened One Night*); heart-to-heart at the end of Act II; resolving Act III alone without mediators |
| 7 | Generativity vs. Stagnation | Stagnant mentor persuaded by the apprentice (*The Karate Kid*); tragedy of preying upon youth (*American Beauty*) |
| 8 | Integrity vs. Despair | "I am the author of my own life story"; *Ikiru* |

### Jungian Archetypes and the Quaternity
| Archetype | Function | Positive Pole | Shadow Pole |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hero | Ego | Luke Skywalker | — |
| Persona | Public mask (Star persona) | John Wayne, Clark Gable | — |
| Shadow | Villain / Alter Ego | — | Darth Vader, Green Goblin, masked slashers |
| Goddess | Mother / Female Sage | Fairy Godmother, Galadriel | Wicked Stepmother, *Mommie Dearest* |
| Wise Old Man | Father / Mentor | Obi-Wan Kenobi | False Mentor (Vader: "I am your father") |

Quaternity = Hero, Villain, Love Object, Mentor; Plot Archetypes: Encounter and Integration, Transformation (can occur in supporting characters), Synchronicity (use boldly), Destiny (use sparingly), Healing (wounded hero confronting the past), Hieros Gamos (romance sequence completing the hero).

### Campbell's Seventeen Stages (*Braveheart* / *Gladiator*)
Departure: Ordinary World (primary mentor frequently dies in Act I) -> Call to Adventure (within the first 20 minutes) -> Herald -> Refusal of the Call -> Escalating the Stakes (murder of wife and child) -> Supernatural Aid (weapon + symbolic talisman: handkerchief / figurine) -> Crossing the First Threshold (threshold guardians; assembling allies) -> Belly of the Whale. Initiation: Road of Trials -> Meeting with the Goddess (deceased / spiritual) -> Woman as Temptress (turncoat from enemy ranks, requires a leap of faith) -> Atonement with the Father -> Apotheosis (brushed by death) -> The Ultimate Boon. Return: Refusal of the Return -> Magic Flight -> Purification (must tie directly to the main conflict) -> Rescue from Without -> Crossing of the Return Threshold -> Master of the Two Worlds (becoming the mentor who inspires the secondary hero) -> Freedom to Live (hero's death becomes legend + "a joyous afterlife").

### Murdock's Ten Stages (*Erin Brockovich*)
Separation from the feminine (babysitter departs) -> Identification with the masculine (Ed the boss) -> Road of trials: slaying the two-headed dragon (job vs. children), the ogre tyrant (the corporation + inner "at any cost"), knight in shining armor (the illusion of a man who will fix everything) -> Illusory boon of success (wonder woman) -> Strong woman learning to say no (the King) -> Initiation and descent to the goddess (the bedridden Donna) -> Urgent yearning to reconnect with the feminine (the wild woman) -> Healing the mother-daughter split (Grandmother Spider) -> Integrating the loving inner masculine (George) -> Transcending duality (fulfillment in both career and home).

### Alfred Adler
- Hamartia = The root source of the hero's inferiority complex that must be overcome; the supervillain's physical deformity = inferiority -> overcompensation -> superiority complex; mentors can also carry superiority complexes (the coach driving athletes mercilessly to redeem his own failed career).
- The Child Hero Formula: Helplessness -> orphan-like emancipation -> wish fulfillment -> "home is where love lives" -> role reversal (child saving helpless parents or marriage) -> animal hero paired with an animal mentor who must also overcome inferiority -> joyous reunion at home.
- Six Elements of Sibling Rivalry (*East of Eden*, *Duel in the Sun*): Good/bad duality, parental favoritism (can cross lines), rivalry over romantic love, hunger for validation, external objective, moral clash. Antagonist and mentor trading places across sequels (the *Rocky* franchise).
- Four Lifestyles: Ruling (Crassus), Getting (Batiatus), Avoiding, Socially Useful; ensemble cast configurations = hero is socially useful / ally is avoiding / villain is ruling / henchman is getting (*Seven Samurai*); anti-hero arc = getting -> avoiding -> socially useful (*Yojimbo*); a single character traversing all four (*The Robe*).

### Rollo May
- Four Stages of Consciousness: Innocence (emerging hero) -> Rebellion (death of innocence: murder of aunt and uncle; the power of hatred; inner "demons" that must be cast out) -> Ordinary Consciousness (throwing oneself into external goals in Act II; catharsis coincides with slaying the Act I murderer) -> Creative Consciousness (epiphany regarding true identity, dedicating oneself to a transcendent purpose).
- Resolution answers "what happened"; the ending answers "what happens next."
- Archetypes of the Narcissistic Age: The American Hero, The Cowboy (Shane, Ethan Edwards), The Solitary Crusader (*High Noon*), The Renegade Cop (verging on cliché), The Outlaw (the fatalistic trap of film noir), The Private Eye (two-thirds renegade cop + one-third gangster), The Dueling Cops (*Serpico*, *Bad Lieutenant*), The Mad Scientist / Reclusive Genius (existential reversal: sacrificing life's work for love, destroying one's creation), The Willing Sacrificial Lamb (mentor dying beforehand).

## V. Lu Jun's "Antagonist Must Be Formidable" Casebook

| Technique | Case Examples |
|---|---|
| Meticulously Foreshadowed Formidable Antagonists | Wu Song fighting the tiger: tavern rumors, stripped tree bark, official government notices, sudden squalls and thunder, snapped cudgel, barehanded battle vs. Li Kui slaying four tigers in succession leaving no impression |
| Handing the Antagonist a Blade | Butcher Zheng armed with a sharp meat-carving knife; *The Tenor Singer*'s three daggers (seductive beauty / pathetic pleading / emotional threats of suicide); Cai Jiu in *Striking the Brass Gong* having lost to Lin Shiniang the previous year; the disabled ex-husband abandoned by his second wife in *Should He Be Forgiven*; the "Single Shear" vs. "The Tigress" in *Abida Visits Her Mother* |
| A Formidable Opposing Coalition | *The Women's Representative*: patriarchal husband + feudal mother-in-law + the vacillating Aunt Niu; *The Reassuring Pill*: three magical pills + Guo Dashan reinforcing the front |
| Innate Dramatic Flaws | *Prayer of the Sword* avoiding the core tension between lust and sacred duty; Party secretary's wife pulling strings while their son suffers an accident (revision: disabled son runs independent research netting tens of thousands); *The Death of Mao Sui* where the trap lacks cunning, denying the protagonist room to struggle |
| Acquired Dramatic Flaws | The abandoned foundling turns out to be the illegitimate child of the blind-date bride; the miserly sister-in-law suddenly turning generous at the curtain |
| Contrived Struggles | *The Cid*, second half: Ximena insists on blood revenge despite everyone's pleas; *The Merry Widow*, reactionary forces lack concrete dramatic focus; *Ferry Maiden Bridge*, Wu Kui offering obstinate, static resistance |
| The Strongest Opponent Is Oneself | Hamlet's "giant's ambition coupled with an infant's will"; Salome unable to conquer her own lust; *Lin Chong's Night Flight* and *Longing for Secular Life* staging a one-actor psychological tour de force |
| No Summoning Auspicious Clouds | Higher-up phone calls, bureaucratic memos, surprise inspections; *The Girl on the Balcony*, village girl crushed by empty political rhetoric—write it as a tragedy |

## VI. Neill D. Hicks's Character Toolkit

- **Stanley and the Dog** (Minimalist action escalation): A timid accountant is kept awake all night by the neighbor's barking dog -> buries head under pillows -> calls police / animal control -> slips an anonymous note under the door -> after a week of insomnia, pulls on a bathrobe and storms over to pound on the door -> the door opens to reveal Suzy, a scantily clad beauty who invites him inside—now he faces an active dramatic choice; the clever writer sets an indispensable career-defining meeting early the next morning.
- Universal Formula for Love Stories: Stanley and Suzy fall passionately in love (farmers' markets, tandem bicycles, trips to the zoo), but the audience perceives their self-concepts are diametrically opposed (classical music vs. Elvis)—only when their self-concepts undergo mutual realignment can they truly unite.
- Five Primary Conflict Focuses with Examples: Personal / Internal (*The Prince of Tides*); Interpersonal (*Ordinary People*, the single-room dynamic of *Driving Miss Daisy*); Situational / Circumstantial (*Twister*, where the core remains the estranged couple's relationship; *The Marrying Kind*, where digging a well quickly exhausts its dramatic mileage); Social (*Full Metal Jacket*, Joker executing the female sniper); Conflicting Interests (*Casablanca*, Major Strasser; *Terminator 2: Judgment Day*, "I know now why you cry").
- Antagonist Perspective Test: In *The Verdict*, viewed from Concannon's perspective, there is nothing inherently villainous—his goal, "to win the case," is identical to Galvin's. Who are the antagonists in *The Silence of the Lambs*, *Titanic*, and *The Piano*?
- Maslow's Hierarchy: Physiological (survival epics, heist/money plots); Safety (thrillers, universally relatable); Love/Belonging (relationship dramas, romantic comedies); Esteem (*Heat*, *Amadeus*); Self-Actualization (the hardest to dramatize effectively, requires a functional foil/antagonist: the nun in *Dead Man Walking*).

## VII. Blake Snyder's Rules for Protagonists and Antagonists

- Five Criteria for the Protagonist: Make me identify with them; make me learn something from them; possess compelling charisma that keeps me watching; make me believe they deserve a happy ending; possess a primal, gut-level drive.
- Character Swap Experiment: Swapping the mild teacher in *The Georgia Rule* for an ex-special forces soldier -> turns into an entirely different movie; swapping the new hire in *National Lampoon's Vacation* for a 65-year-old retiree -> "A Jack Lemmon indie, except he's dead."
- Cast by Actor Types, Not Movie Stars: The Young Man on the Make, The Good Girl Next Door, The Plucky Kid, The Sex Goddess, The Hunk, The Wounded Soldier, The Damsel in Distress, The Lovable Rogue, The Jester, The Wise Old Grandfather.
- Save the Cat: Al Pacino in *Sea of Love* letting the father with the kid go free; Aladdin stealing a loaf of bread only to give it to starving street children; making anti-heroes likable through humor (the Royale with cheese banter in *Pulp Fiction*); if the protagonist has glaring flaws, have an abhorrent boss throw someone out the window first.
- The Bad Guy Must Be Badder; Protagonist and Antagonist are two sides of the same coin (Batman and the Joker); everyone in the story should have an arc except the antagonist; supporting characters require "a limp and an eyepatch" (a memorable, distinct visual quirk or hook).

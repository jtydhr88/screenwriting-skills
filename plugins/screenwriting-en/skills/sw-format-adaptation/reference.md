## II. Hicks's "Harem Format Template" Breakdown

- Scene Heading: INT. ISLAMIC HAREM - DAY — quickly establish location and day/night.
- Action description opens with a broad establishing image of the scene introducing harem maidens; eunuch guards' introduction needs no camera direction, only enough to make the reader recognize and remember them.
- Fatima's "withered, wrinkled face like a dried persimmon" — describe with abstract, distinctive actions, not generic height and weight.
- Avoid camera directions such as MS, Close-Up, Pan Right; "The eunuch opens the door, wind billows silk robes, all eyes turn to the brass doors" implies returning to a wide shot with a new element entering.
- Caliph enters briskly — name and give action to major characters quickly; "waves his hand" — a good actor will choose an appropriate wave.
- Parenthetical guidance "(deadpan)": when scene intention is clear, omit parentheticals; use them only when addressing one specific person in a group is ambiguous.
- Audio cue "crisp metallic tinkle of ankle bells" foreshadows a grave situation — write only sounds that redirect attention (traffic noise while sitting alone matters little; a car door slam when expecting an assassin is a life-or-death cue).
- V.O. = the voice of a speaker who is off-screen; O.S. = sound from off-screen used to grab the on-screen character's attention (an off-screen explosion making dinner guests jump); use both with precision.
- "(eyes locked on her new rival)" — ask yourself if this serves the storytelling or is self-indulgent decoration.
- Transitions: CUT (simple, immediate change), DISSOLVE (longer time or larger emotional shift), FADE IN (years passing); on the page, its function is to ensure the reader fully recognizes that the master scene has changed. New master scene "EXT. THIEVES' DEN CAMPSITE - DAY" — Fatima's "Every rose has thorns" carries tension into the new scene; the rest is the screenwriter's job: make events happen.

## III. Henson's Format Specification Checklist

- Courier 10 CPI = Courier New 12 pt; white paper, single-sided; left margin 1.5–2 inches, right >= 1 inch, top 1 inch, bottom 0.5–1.5 inches kept consistent; altering margins to fake page counts is easily caught with a ruler.
- Title page: title in large underlined type centered 3 inches from the top, single-spaced name, address, and phone number in lower right corner; no names on subsequent pages, no WGA registration numbers or copyright notices.
- Action paragraphs single-spaced internally, double-spaced between paragraphs; "FADE IN:" only at the beginning, "FADE OUT" only at the end.
- Scene Heading: INT./EXT. + location + DAY/NIGHT, never "EXT. OUTSIDE THE PALACE" or "INT. IN THE VILLAGE"; details belong in action lines.
- Action paragraphs resemble lists; include given circumstances (time, season, weather, environmental conditions affecting action, physical details providing clues, detailed actions revealing character and emotion); four essentials: visualize; decide what the audience watches; write visual images conveying meaning without explanation; write only what the camera can shoot and actors can perform.
- Imply camera angles through text: Scene Heading = wide shot, banner = medium shot, Darren walking to microphone = close-up; every scene naturally ends in a cut.
- Character names in ALL CAPS upon first appearance; minor characters named by function (TWO POLICE OFFICERS, A WAITER) also capitalized; "Is a corpse a character? Only if it speaks."
- Dialogue indented 3 inches from the left edge of the page, 3.25 inches wide; avoid awkward page breaks: insert action breaks, have a second person chime in with a word or two, or as a last resort use "(MORE)" + "NAME (CONT'D)" with at least two dialogue lines per page; reconsider any speech running over five or six lines (Hamlet syndrome).
- Parentheticals: one or two words, never more than three, verbs only (smiles), (nervous), (angry); "Using parentheticals on paper is directing from the keyboard."
- Bases for transitions: location/time shift, addition/subtraction of characters, change in dramatic objective; double space between final line of dialogue and next Scene Heading.
- Sound effects capitalized for one or two words in action text only; omit visible sources (pouring rain, barking dog), include invisible sources (thunder, ringing phone, dog barking off-screen); (O.S.) for off-screen dialogue, (V.O.) voice-over only for material that can neither be seen nor heard in the scene; special effects describe only what the audience actually sees.
- Passive voice is a curse; "She stands there thinking" cannot be filmed; ruthlessly eliminate adjectives and adverbs.
- Exercises: adapt 7–10 pages of a novel into 3–5 script pages without parentheticals, O.S., V.O., or narrative summary; reverse-engineer the first 5 minutes of a favorite movie into screenplay format.

## IV. Henson's "Fresh Cheese" Dual-Version Comparison

**Bad Version**: INT. UNITED NATIONS - DAY "For the past hour, delegates have been listening to Dash rant... clad in a spacesuit, babbling incessantly" DASH "I analyzed rock samples on the moon and made an astounding discovery: fresh cheese!" AMBASSADOR looking contemptuous "This Dash has been an eccentric rogue since the day he joined the space agency." AIDE "Perhaps we should call General Hotz." — Begins with backstory, past tense, passive voice, impression of Dash comes from secondhand descriptions, characters endlessly deliver exposition to help the audience get into the story while dramatic action remains dead frozen.
**Good Version**: EXT. MOON BASE - DAY Dash, clad in a spacesuit, gathers the final sample, slips it into his pocket, and heads for the survival module; INT. SURVIVAL MODULE - DAY Dash, in a biker jacket and jeans, sporting tattoos, plays a CD of Mozart; he empties a pale green rock sample, sniffs it, flicks open a switchblade, slices off a sliver, and pops it into his mouth: "My God, it's cheese!"; INT. UNITED NATIONS - DAY Dash waves the cheese: "We can solve world hunger and achieve permanent world peace." Low murmurs. The Ambassador glares with contempt. The Aide dials: "General Hotz? One moment." The Ambassador takes the phone: "Harvey? Dash just finished talking. (listens) Right, my office in ten minutes, bring grenades and a bazooka." — Appearance, wardrobe, and musical taste instantly reveal Dash's unconventional nature without anyone saying it; transitive verbs, present tense, and active voice showcase actions that provoke real reactions.

## V. McKee's Three-Step Method and the Treatment Example

- **Step Outline** index cards: "He enters the house expecting to see her, only to find a note saying she has left forever"; note structural placement on the back; the purpose is to destroy your own work — sketch a scene a dozen ways and discard them, dismantle sequences and acts; keep filing cabinets overflowing with character biographies, world notes, thematic observations, and visual images.
- **Pitching the Story in Ten Minutes**: do not show the outline; an enthusiastic reaction = a quiet gasp of "wow" followed by thoughtful silence; "If it doesn't work in ten minutes, it will be ten times worse across one hundred and ten minutes."
- **Treatment**: expand each scene into one or more double-spaced, present-tense paragraphs describing moment-by-moment action without dialogue, laying bare the Subtext (Jack truly hopes she is not home; smashing her prized Chippendale chair; tears of genuine relief); 60–90 pages.
- **Screenplay**: write five to ten pages a day; dialogue flows best here (characters have been silenced for so long they are bursting to speak, each with a distinct voice); when flaws appear, return to the treatment to fix setups and payoffs.
- Description: strict present tense; concrete nouns and active verbs; avoid "is/are" throughout; metaphors must pass the test of "what is seen and heard on screen" (a door slamming like a gunshot works; "the sun sinks slowly like a tiger's eye" is a directorial trap); cut "we see/hear"; master scenes describe only essential angles, using single-spaced brief paragraphs to imply camera changes (wide shot -> tracking shot -> close-up of note -> close-up of ring -> medium shot).

## VI. Diamond & Weissman: The Nine Steps on One Page and Character Action Chart

**Nine Steps**: 1. Opening: establish character and status quo; 2. Introduce an opportunity or challenge; 3. Plot Point I: character makes an irreversible commitment; 4. Turn (around p. 45): game-changing information radically raises the stakes; 5. Midpoint reversal: a bigger, more unexpected shift forces a regrouping; 6. Escalation (around p. 75); 7. Act II Climax: protagonist appears to lose everything; 8. Then it gets worse: the ultimate test; 9. Final Climax or Set Piece: digging deep to summon qualities they never knew they possessed.

***Star Wars* Nine Steps**: Luke stuck on farm longing for the academy -> Leia's distress message inside R2 -> Obi-Wan asks him along, he refuses, aunt and uncle murdered -> hires Han Solo, learns the Force en route to Alderaan, arrives to find it obliterated -> tractor beam pulls them into the Death Star, princess imprisoned, "delivery" becomes a rescue -> princess rescued, Obi-Wan sacrifices himself -> Han takes reward and departs, Empire tracks ship to rebel base -> fighters attack Death Star and are picked off one by one, Han swoops in to blast the wingman -> Death Star destroyed, hero celebrated.

***Time Reversal* Nine Steps** (authors' original pitch): Physicist and lifelong friend test a device that travels back a few minutes -> framed and fired by rival due to fame-seeking -> pitch using science as "problem solvers" -> first client rejects them, help a victim recover $5,000, make the news, and raise fee to $1M -> smooth sailing, romance with science reporter leads to sharing core secret -> reporter betrays them with front-page headline -> jobless again, facing lawsuits, friendship shattered, "and there's an even bigger problem" -> Pentagon black op creates a sinkhole threatening to become a black hole, requiring their help -> regroup for the final mission, arriving in 1977 with friendship and wealth restored.

**Character Action Chart** (Han Solo): absent from Act I; appears at minute 45 in the cantina purely for cash to settle Jabba's debt; Alderaan destroyed, princess captive — to Luke delivery turns to rescue, an escalation; Han does not care: "She's rich" — "How rich?" Re-enlists for money; escaping Death Star, Obi-Wan falls — Luke at rock bottom, Han on a high (double pay, thrilled to break out); Leia recognizes his skill yet detests his cynicism; Death Star flaw found, Han pockets cash and leaves ("makes total sense, yet deeply disappointing"); climax: classic "Han Solo entrance"; receives medal with Chewie. "Can you tell the entire story from every character's perspective?"

**Connective Scene**: Millennium Falcon traveling from Tatooine to Alderaan — cannot cut straight to arrival, nor can they merely sit eating peanuts; the answer lies in the Character Action Chart: mentor, eager student, and mercenary trapped together -> Obi-Wan teaches lightsaber and the Force, Han scoffs at Jedi mysticism.

***Paternity Leave* Breast Pump Set Piece** (Trailer Moment Example): Jack searches for milk, finds only a bottle of breast milk, prepares to pour it into coffee -> "Do you know how hard I worked to pump that one bottle?" -> The women go shopping -> Jack opens cabinet, spots double breast pump -> plugs it in the living room, hears the rhythm, finds it amusing -> takes off shirt, suctions cups to chest -> watches Dodgers game with beer -> three hours later, falls asleep with pump still running -> Peggy's mother walks in: "No..." -> Jack jumps up, tripped by the weight of the machine -> pulls off suction cups to reveal two bottles with three and a half ounces of milk each -> ER doctor: "Male lactation... actually fairly common in POW camps" -> "Let's fit you with a protective brace" -> "You're looking at... what, a B-cup?"

## VII. Hoxter: Outlines and Treatments

- John August's sequence outline for *Big Fish*: "Sequence 01 pp. 1-6 The Catfish: Will's entire life his father tells the big fish story, still telling it at the wedding, father-son argument" ... "Sequence 04 Will receives phone call."
- Terry Rossio's opening beat in the treatment for *The Mask of Zorro*: "Zorro arrives under full sail, raiding the execution grounds... A black phantom under the moonlight, Alejandro and Joaquin watch in slack-jawed awe."
- Four questions of adaptation: Are rights secured? Can it be cinematized (visualized)? What are the original medium's unique traits (comic panels and pages)? Can its thematic symbolism be utilized? First write a "faithful reproduction" outline, then prune.
- *La La Land* opening: blank-space paragraph breaks = individual camera shots; "Moving slowly, we see more cars" implies a tracking shot; audio marks the visual transitions (prog rock / opera / hip hop / French chanson -> original song).
- *The Bourne Identity* fishing boat surgery: "Now -- Giancarlo with flashlight in teeth -- CLINK -- CLINK -- CLINK -- shrapnel drops into an olive jar" — double hyphens = jump cuts, onomatopoeia captures the genre; "Do not blindly ape style; ask what it seeks to achieve."
- *Lost in Translation*: "Bob is distracted" (action, not emotion); "They don't know what to say. For some reason, breakfast feels ambiguous. Charlotte notices her every move as she eats."

## VIII. Field: Formatting Elements and the "Subject of the Shot"

- `EXT. ARIZONA DESERT - DAY`; double space; newly introduced characters capitalized; character cue centered; dialogue centered; sound effects and music capitalized; `CUT TO:` (fades and dissolves are editorial and directorial decisions).
- The Rule: identify the subject of the shot (Bill walks out of apartment: subject is Bill; Bill drives down street: subject is the car).
- Twelve terms replacing "camera": ANGLE ON / FAVORING / ANOTHER ANGLE / WIDER ANGLE / NEW ANGLE / POV / REVERSE ANGLE / OVER-THE-SHOULDER SHOT / MOVING SHOT / TWO-SHOT / CLOSE SHOT (use sparingly) / INSERT.
- Do not number scenes in spec scripts; title page contains only script title, author name, address, and phone number.
- Adaptation case study: disaster novel begins at page 278 with glacier measurements; Halloween blizzard = Plot Point I; ship locked in ice = Plot Point II; Act III is entirely original — nine survivors living like Eskimos, concluding with childbirth. Female editor-in-chief biography: 26 pages of chronological notes -> restructured using the breaking news story that won her the editor-in-chief post as the "hook."
- Three stages of collaboration: Ground Rules / Preparation / Writing (mutual editing in 30-page blocks); four functional roles: writer, researcher, recorder, editor.

## Grace's development-document templates

The following working templates adapt Yvonne Grace's *Writing for Television*. Use the document the production requests, not a fixed sequence for every show.

1. Pitch: world / period / essential turning point and jeopardy / protagonist or ensemble. Reduce a page to a paragraph for a verbal story meeting.
2. Treatment: plot / jeopardy / theme or message / tone and style / influences / channel and slot / target audience. Grace reports four to ten pages. Develop the idea rather than applying feature-treatment page rules.
3. Series outline: later episodes in broad strokes, not every beat. Grace notes that a treatment which already shows longevity may make a separate series outline unnecessary.
4. Scene-by-scene outline: scene / where / who / what happens / scene intention / plot and character beats. Use this to see weak mechanics, misplaced scenes, or missing turns.
5. Storyline document: Grace's illustrative *Harkness Hall* template has episode number and TX, cast and interior/exterior set checklists, block identification, and contacts for executive producer, producer, story executive, script editors, and storyliners. For each A, B, or C story, record title, characters or group, location/set, pick-up point, episode arc and beats, and cliffhanger moment. State where the strand starts and ends so the next writer can continue it. Grace's example uses about five to six paragraphs for A and three to four for a smaller B, roughly one paragraph per scene. These are example proportions, not universal quotas. Describe speech and action in prose without dialogue. The writer chooses scene order after the document. A C story may begin within this episode and provide lighter material.

# Format, Process & Adaptation (格式·流程·改编)

Three matters: **how to write on the page** (formatting and action paragraphs), **how to move from idea to finished draft** (outlines / treatments / draft chains / revisions), and **what to do when adapting source material from others**. Sources: Wendy Jane Henson, *Screenwriting: Step by Step*; Richard Walter, *Essentials of Screenwriting*; Neill D. Hicks, *Screenwriting 101: The Essential Craft of Feature Film Writing*; Syd Field, *Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting*; Robert McKee, *Story*; Julian Hoxter, *The Creative Screenwriter: 12 Rules to Follow and Break to Unlock Your Screenwriting Potential*; David Diamond and David Weissman, *Bulletproof: Writing Scripts That Don't Get Shot Down*; Erik Bork, *The Idea: The Seven Elements of a Viable Story for Screen, Stage or Fiction*. For examples and a symbol table, see [reference.md](reference.md). For industry transactions and business practices, see `sw-industry-business`.

## I. You Are Writing a Selling Script, Not a Shooting Script

- Hicks: Format originates from outdated studio shooting scripts; **what you are writing now is a selling script**, so avoid useless abbreviations and numerical markers; **the most fundamental rule: make the script easy to read**—do not make the reader work hard, and do not make them flip back to previous pages to check how a scene came about; "You are manipulating the reader, not the film."
- John Milius: "I never write camera directions, never write 'we push in' or 'we can see'—that is supremely presumptuous. Format only needs to tell the reader day or night, interior or exterior; the rest is like a novel—clear, precise, and inspiring an interest in reading."
- Henson: If the format is incorrect, no one will accept it; script readers scan the first few pages first, reading the content only if the format is correct; "Never give anyone a single reason not to read your script"; a screenplay is a "blueprint" guiding production, and audiences do not read scripts.
- Walter's Principle 32: **If you want others to treat you as a professional dramatist, first hold yourself to professional standards**; "It just needs to look like it was written by a writer" (Bork); if you faithfully obey the limitations of image and sound, you can almost disregard format.
- Diamond: Read only screenplays (the good, the bad, the produced, the unproduced)—thousands of free scripts are available online; but Henson and Walter warn: most scripts online are shot-by-shot shooting scripts, so do not imitate their camera terminology.

## II. Hard Rules of Formatting (Henson / Walter / Hicks / Hoxter / Diamond Combined)

- **Font and Page Count**: Courier 12 (one page ≈ one minute); 100–110 pages is ideal, 90–120 is acceptable, <80 looks amateurish, >125 makes "everyone think you don't know how to write," and 130 is out of control; do not change the font size to cheat the page count.
- **Title Page**: List only the title and author name (contact information may be added if you are unrepresented); do not include dates, draft numbers, WGA registration numbers, "Original Screenplay," or illustrations; bind with two brass brads.
- **Page Numbers**: Begin at the top right corner of page two; do not number scenes, and do not write "continued"; "FADE IN:" belongs only at the very beginning, and "FADE OUT." belongs only at the very end.
- **Scene Headings**: INT./EXT. + location + DAY/NIGHT, all in uppercase; move from general to specific ("INT. LAMBEAU FIELD, LOCKER ROOM - DAY"); place specific details (date, hour) in the action paragraphs; terms like "LATE NIGHT" or "DUSK" may be written to aid reader comprehension.
- **Action Paragraphs**: Present tense, active voice ("Joe is led by Paul" → "Paul leads Joe"); reads like a list rather than a description; place time, season, and weather first, then physical features of the location; write only what the camera can photograph and actors can perform ("She stands there thinking" cannot be filmed); cut adjectives and adverbs; capitalize character names upon first appearance and include their age; capitalize props and synthetic sound effects (alerting the production manager and sound recordist); describe only sound effects whose sources cannot be seen (thunder, telephone ringing, off-screen dog barking); double-space between paragraphs—"a screenplay page is not a wall of text."
- **Never Mention the Camera**: No CU, POV, CUT, DISSOLVE, or "we see/hear"; imply camera shots through prose (Scene Heading = wide shot, a banner = medium shot, Darren stepping up to the microphone = close-up); the end of every scene is naturally a cut, so do not write "CUT TO:" (cutting these can reduce ten pages); FADE IN, FADE OUT, and DISSOLVE should be in all caps on the extremely rare occasions they are needed; use P.O.V. only where essential, such as through binoculars or for subjective point-of-view shots.
- **Write Master Scenes Only**: Dramatic action with continuity of thought and movement can span multiple locations (George and Martha arguing from the living room to the bedroom door to the French window remains a single scene: "INT. GEORGE AND MARTHA'S HOUSE"); a spy meeting at a hot dog stand needs only a wide establishing view plus essential details—breaking down shots is the editor's job.
- **Dialogue**: A left-aligned narrow column starting approximately 2.5 inches from the left edge of the page, with a column width of approximately 3.5 inches (**not centered on the page**; Henson's original book stating "3 inches from the left edge with a width of 3.25 inches" is merely another way of measuring the same column); see the next section for complete layout specifications; character names in uppercase; avoid breaking across pages (interrupt with action or another character's interjection; if unavoidable, use "(MORE)"); lines running across pages constitute "Hamlet syndrome"; parentheticals should be ≤3 words using only verbs (smiles / tense)—99% are unnecessary, with exceptions for clarifying who one is speaking to in multi-character scenes; no underlining for emphasis, no exclamation points, and while ellipses may appear once or twice, a third time is too much; avoid off-screen dialogue (O.S.) and voice-over (V.O.) whenever possible; do not write phonetic dialect; dialogue must be complete sentences, not grunts or groans ("S. or N.").
- **Character Introductions**: Give only gender and age (Walter); describe through distinctive, abstract actions ("A shriveled, puckered face like a dried persimmon") without detailing height, weight, or skin tone unless required by the integrity of the whole; do not include cast lists, character biographies, or casting suggestions; choose names that clarify gender, and avoid homophones or identical initial letters.
- **Basis for a Transition**: A shift in location or time, the arrival or departure of characters, or a change in dramatic purpose.
- **Special Effects**: Describe only what the audience sees; consider avoiding a Montage first, but when used as a tool to simplify complex continuous action, one may write "CHASE" and list key beats (reducing thirteen pages to one-fifth of a page).
- **Flashbacks**: Ideally, avoid writing the word "FLASHBACK"; if worried the reader will be confused, one may label it "EXT. BARN - FLASHBACK"—"a device resembling a cheat"; cheating (clarifying that this person is the boss, or that these two are sisters) is permissible naturally in small doses; a "gentle reminder" ("JANEY, the woman we saw earlier vandalizing the vending machine at the laundromat") is allowed.
- **Email Submissions**: Send as a PDF, never Word; do not state that it is written specifically for television.
- Spelling and Punctuation: Be a pedant, use a paper dictionary; "I have never seen a script with sparkling dialogue and unforgettable characters that was a mess in format, spelling, grammar, and punctuation."

## II-2. Layout Specifications, Element Conventions, and Output Formats

> The numerical values in this section derive from empirical measurements of professionally typeset screenplays (US Letter, Courier 12) and Final Draft default templates. Element-level conventions and Chinese and Japanese industry formats reflect accepted industry practice and **do not originate from this skill's source books** (added pursuant to issue #2). For examples, see Section X of [reference.md](reference.md).

### Layout Specifications Table (US Letter, Courier 12: 10 cpi, 6 lpi)

| Element | Left Offset from Page Edge | Width / Right Margin | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Page Margins | Left 1.5", Right 1.0", Top 1.0", Bottom 1.0" | Approx. 55 lines per page | Wider left margin accommodates binding |
| Scene Heading | 1.5" (flush left) | To 7.5", approx. 60 characters | Uppercase; preceded by two blank lines (some templates use one) |
| Action Paragraph | 1.5" (flush left) | 6.0" wide, approx. 60 characters | Single blank line between paragraphs |
| Character Name | 3.7" (approx. character position 22) | Not centered; fixed indent | Followed by extensions (V.O.) / (O.S.) / (CONT'D) |
| Parenthetical | 3.1" | Approx. 2.0" wide | Single line, primarily action verbs |
| Dialogue | 2.5" | 3.5" wide (to 6.0"), approx. 35 characters | Left-aligned narrow column, single-spaced within column |
| Transition | Right-aligned to 7.5" | Begins at approx. 6.0" | `CUT TO:` in all caps with a colon |
| Page Number | Top right corner, 7.0"–7.5", 0.5" from top | Formatted as `2.` | Beginning on page two |

The one page ≈ one minute conversion is built upon this exact table; altering any single parameter distorts the estimated page count.

### Element Conventions

- **(V.O.)**: The speaker is not physically present in the scene's spatial setting—voice-over, the other end of a phone call, recordings, internal monologues. **(O.S.)**: Present in the scene's spatial environment, but off-camera (in the next room, outside the door). Television scripts frequently use (O.C.) as equivalent to O.S. Use sparingly, but when used, use accurately.
- **(CONT'D)**: Added after a character's name when their dialogue is interrupted by an action paragraph and then resumes; when splitting across pages, add `(MORE)` at the bottom of the page and `(CONT'D)` after the character's name on the following page—screenwriting software handles this automatically, but for manual drafts, prefer adjusting page breaks.
- **(PRE-LAP)**: Sound from the next scene begins before the current scene ends. Use **(INTO PHONE)** / **(ON PHONE)** as parentheticals; if the party on the other end is not visible, use (V.O.), optionally appending (FILTERED).
- **Dual Dialogue**: Two characters speaking simultaneously in parallel side-by-side columns; in Fountain, append `^` after the second character's name.
- **Montage / Series of Shots**: Begins with `MONTAGE - TITLE`, with each item on its own line starting with `--`, and closes with `END MONTAGE` or `BACK TO SCENE`; `SERIES OF SHOTS` functions identically. Still adhere to the earlier rule: "avoid if possible."
- **Intercut**: After `INTERCUT - LOCATION A / LOCATION B`, cut freely between them without writing new Scene Headings.
- **Flashbacks**: Prefix the Scene Heading with `FLASHBACK - ` or enclose the block with `BEGIN FLASHBACK` / `END FLASHBACK`.
- **Super**: `SUPER: "THE EIGHTEENTH"` (within an action paragraph).
- **Sound Effects and Newly Introduced Characters** are fully capitalized in action paragraphs (in English scripts); in Chinese scripts, use boldface or leave unadorned, maintaining consistency throughout a draft.

### Output Format Contract: Fountain

Scripts generated by the agent **must default to Fountain plain text delivery** (`.fountain`), not Markdown, and not text with manually typed spaces. Rationale: Fountain elements correspond one-to-one with the table above, can be version-controlled, and can be rendered to PDF or exported to .fdx.

**Core Syntax** (for full specification, see fountain.io):
- Title Page: Key-value pairs at the file head for `Title:`, `Credit:`, `Author:`, `Draft date:`, and `Contact:`, followed by a blank line before the body text.
- Scene Headings: Lines starting with `INT.`, `EXT.`, `INT./EXT.`, or `I/E` are recognized automatically; **force** it with a leading `.` (e.g., `.内 沈越家·客厅 夜`, INT. Shen Yue's home, living room, night).
- Character Names: Uppercase lines preceded by a blank line are recognized automatically; **force** it with a leading `@` (e.g., `@沈越`, Shen Yue). Extensions follow directly after the name: `@小七 (V.O.)` (Xiao Qi). For dual dialogue, append `^` to the second character name.
- Parentheticals: `(...)` on the line beneath the character name. Dialogue: Consecutive lines following the character name, terminated by a blank line.
- Transitions: Uppercase lines ending in `TO:` are recognized automatically; **force** it with a leading `>` (e.g., `> 切至：`, CUT TO:). Centered text: `> 剧终 <` (THE END).
- Action: All other paragraphs; force it with a leading `!`. Page breaks: `===`; notes: `[[...]]`; boneyard: `/* ... */`; sections: `#`; synopses: `=`; lyrics: `~`.

**Chinese Scripts Must Use Forced Markers**: Fountain relies on capitalization and INT./EXT. to parse elements, neither of which exists in Chinese; every Scene Heading must include a leading `.`, every character name an `@`, and every transition a `>`, otherwise the rendered output will collapse entirely into action paragraphs.

**Rendering Paths**: Fountain → PDF: afterwriting (CLI), Beat (macOS, free), Highland, Slugline; Fountain → .fdx: direct import into Fade In or Final Draft, or export via afterwriting. Chinese rendering requires specifying a CJK monospaced font (Sarasa Mono, Noto Sans Mono CJK), as Courier lacks Chinese glyphs.

**Integration with `sw-workflow`**: The Stage 5 deliverable is the `.fountain` file; the story-bible records only the file name and page count.

### Chinese Film and Television Scripts: Scene Numbering Format

Standard structure across the modern Chinese film and television industry (minor details vary by production company, but must remain consistent within a single draft; see reference.md for examples):
- **Scene Line**: `场号 时 内/外 地点` (scene number, time, INT/EXT, location), such as `1. 日 内 沈越家·客厅` (1. day, interior, Shen Yue's home, living room); episodic series use `集-场` (episode-scene, `3-12`). Time uses `日/夜/晨/黄昏` (day, night, morning, dusk); two traditions exist for the order, `日 内` versus `内 日`, so select one and stick to it.
- **Character Line** (optional): `人物：沈越、小七` (Characters: Shen Yue, Xiao Qi), listing characters appearing in this scene.
- **Action Paragraph**: Paragraph begins with `△` (some use ▲ or omit it), written in the present tense, one dramatic unit of action per paragraph.
- **Dialogue**: `人名：台词` (name, full-width colon, line), with parentheticals placed after the character name, e.g., `沈越（低声）：` (whispering); off-screen dialogue is marked `（OS）` or `（画外）`, and voice-over `（VO）` or `（旁白）`.
- **Do not write transitions**; flashbacks add `（回忆）` (flashback) to the scene line; superimposed titles are written as `字幕：` (SUPER:).
- An episodic script of 40–45 minutes per episode runs approximately 12,000–15,000 Chinese characters; a scene-by-scene outline (one line per scene) is the precursor to the Scene Numbering Format and is identical to the step outline in `sw-story-structure`.
- The Scene Numbering Format **does not use Fountain**; deliver it as plain text directly. The agent must ask the user whether Hollywood format or the Scene Numbering Format is desired before outputting.

### Japanese Screenplay Format: Scene Heading, Stage Direction, Lines

- **Scene Heading (`柱`, hashira)**: The scene line, formatted as `○地点（时间）` (location, then time in full-width parentheses), such as `○沈越の部屋・居間（夜）` (Shen Yue's room, living room, night); scene numbers may be placed before `○`.
- **Stage Direction (`ト書き`, togaki)**: Action description, indented three full-width spaces at the start of the paragraph; introduce characters with their age in full-width digits, e.g., `沈越（３４）` (Shen Yue, 34); passages of time are represented by `　　×　　×　　×`; pauses are indicated by `（間）` (pause).
- **Lines (`セリフ`, serifu)**: `人物名「台词」` (name, then the line in corner brackets), the name followed directly by full-width corner brackets without a colon; parentheticals are placed after the name, e.g., `小七（声）「…」` (voice); `（Ｎ）` marks narration, `（Ｍ）` marks monologue, and `（回想）` (flashback) is appended to the Scene Heading.
- **Draft Stages**: `準備稿 (junbikō) → 改訂稿 (kaiteikō) → 決定稿 (ketteikō)` (preparation, revised, final), written on the title page; printed copies are typeset vertically, while text files may remain horizontal.
- For Project Proposal (kikakusho) formatting, see Section IX of reference.md; it is an entirely distinct document type from the screenplay format.

## III. Writing Strong Action Paragraphs

- McKee: **Absolute present tense + continuous vivid movement**; concrete nouns (a sharp iron spike, not a large nail), action verbs (pad, amble, stroll, mosey, saunter, stagger, tiptoe, creep, shuffle, waddle, trudge, lurch, hobble, instead of "moves slowly"); **eliminate is/are throughout** ("A mansion guards the entrance to the village"); follow Hemingway by avoiding Latinate abstractions, adjectives, and adverbs; metaphors must pass the test of "what is seen or heard on screen"; delete "we see" and "we hear"; use small, single-spaced paragraphs to imply shot transitions (wide shot → tracking shot → close-up of note → close-up of ring → medium shot).
- Walter's Shorthand Symbols: **Hwk?/See/Hear** ("This truth reminds Charlene of her mother's years of abuse"—how does the audience see this?); **Ess. Det. Only / SIFYN** (save the aroma of pastrami for your novel; Hudson Valley feast → "A lavish party is underway on the lawn"); **Drekt/Akt** (never write "pauses thoughtfully"); **Notnot** (do not write "does not answer" or "does nothing"); **Prez** ("The room is empty" rather than "Everyone has left"); **Novry** ("very fast" → sprints, bolts, tears); **Conk** (concrete); **No Tt** (do not sound like Tarzan: "Picks up gun. Walks to window. Loads."); **4 v. 6** (ratio of needed to unneeded words: "Trimming one hundred and ten pages at this ratio eliminates fifty-six pages"); **Fmpmt** (find the point, make the point, move the story forward: "Harry leaves"—two words); **NoFX** (no centered text, right alignment, italics, boldface, or color); **Clok** (how much screen time does this beat take up? Even one minute lacking drama drags heavily).
- Henson: For two weeks, keep every sentence to ≤10 words, cutting conjunctions, subordinate clauses, adjectives, and adverbs while retaining transitive verbs; "Pam folds laundry in the utility room. The phone rings. Pam darts to the kitchen and lifts the receiver. No answer. Pam hangs up."; learn concision from the original shooting drafts of produced scripts (compare the two drafts of *Aliens*).
- Diamond: "He reaches into his back pocket, pulls out his wallet, extracts a five-dollar bill, and hands it to her" → "He pulls out his wallet and hands her five dollars"; test: describe the scene to a friend—use no more detail than you would when telling the story aloud; do not do the jobs of the director, production designer, costume designer, or composer.
- Hicks: Write images, not film pictures; "Evoke the inner imagination, do not describe a picture"; write action featuring causality with a beginning, middle, and end to give the reader rhythm (a snowmobile chase is a counterexample); style = the tonal expression of context—the opening scene gives the reader clues on how to read the screenplay (the most common Hollywood complaint is that scripts are misread).

## IV. The Process from Idea to Finished Script

### Before Writing
- **Bork's 60/30/10 Rule**: Success is 60% core idea, 30% structure, and 10% execution; first condense the concept into a one- or two-sentence logline plus a one-page summary to gather objective feedback, self-checking with the PROBLEM seven-element criteria (see `sw-premise-theme`).
- **The Diamond and Weissman Process**: The three C's (Character, Concept, Context) → reference film list → one-page nine-step synopsis → character action chart (recounting the nine steps from each character's perspective) → narrative meld + connective tissue + subplots + comic relief → outline → set pieces → script → revision → submission. "Do not begin writing until the pitch, treatment, and outline have satisfied all parties."
- **Henson**: Write the statement of purpose (theme → basic action + statement of objective) plus the three-sentence dramatic premise (inciting disruption / Act I hook / Act II hook, each with its central question) on a single sheet of paper to serve as an outline; writer's block means the concept has holes or has been derailed by a subplot.
- **Walter**: An outline is not a synopsis (a synopsis robs the story of pleasure, and an unfinished script's synopsis is disregarded); writing an outline is "like trying to hold steam in a bucket"—it is the hardest and most indispensable step; outlines should contain some specific dialogue; **Principle 44: The outline is past tense; the screenplay is present tense**—"The real problem is not straying from the track, but staying on it"; scene cards (numbered index cards, around sixty scenes, each scene functioning as a mini-movie) facilitate experimentation; when stuck two-thirds of the way through, return and convert the story back into cards.
- **McKee**: "From the inside out": spend the first four of six months writing 3x5 cards for a **step outline** (one or two sentences per scene, noting structural placement on the back; the goal is to destroy one's own work, sketching a dozen-plus variations of a scene before discarding them) → **pitch the story for ten minutes**, watching the listener's eyes → **treatment** (60–90 pages, present-tense moment-by-moment description, no dialogue, bringing out the subtext) → **screenplay** (five to ten pages a day, where dialogue arrives at its best). Skipping this process to write the script directly produces a sham treatment and is the slowest method; "Premature dialogue writing stifles creativity."
- **Field's Card Method**: One day for Act I, two days for Act II, one day for Act III; build the four primary units first: the beginning, the two plot points, and the ending.
- **Hicks's Draft Chain**: Sofa writing → outline ("concept sketch") → prose treatment (present tense) → inclusive first draft (at least one hundred pages, often across multiple passes) → revised draft (cutting stage directions for actors, eliminating furniture, converting to simple present tense, proofreading) → agent draft (shown only to critics who truly understand screenwriting) → market draft.
- Hoxter: An **outline** is for yourself (scene-by-scene, following your chosen act structure); a **treatment** is for buyers (prose, written in a cinematic tone; a comedy treatment must read funny, 4–30 pages); see John August's *Big Fish* sequence outline as an example. Diamond: Pitch presentations are best kept under 20 minutes; narrative treatments cannot be sold on their own—the mainstream market only buys completed screenplays.

### Writing Habits
- Tommy Thompson: "Get fully dressed every single day"; Walter's Principle 41: **Writer's block is the natural state of writing**; "A writer does not have the luxury of writing only when inspired"; Principle 42: Deadlines are a writer's friend; Principle 43: Do not let perfectionism become an obstacle; "Where a writer writes is far less important than what they write"; begin each day by revising the previous day's single page before writing new material—never revise from page one every day (which creates a brass opening with a flabby ending); Coppola stacked pages face down, never revising until the draft was finished.
- Field: Two to three hours a day, four days a week, three pages a day; "When in doubt, write"; a first draft should be too long rather than too short (180–200 pages); write across three stages (6–8 weeks) → compress to 130–140 pages → polish; when stuck, ask "What happens next?", return to character biographies, or write a scene from five different angles five times.
- Hoxter: Start the next scene before finishing work for the day; you do not have to write in chronological order; make major changes in the outline first; push through in a single sustained momentum. Diamond: "Make haste slowly"—allow yourself a day or two of procrastination before writing to clear mental clutter; reread the previous day's work first each day.
- Bork: Generate ideas by making lists and cross-matching elements, spending 15 minutes a day, prioritizing quantity before filtering.

### Revision
- Walter's Principle 46: **Real writing, especially screenwriting, is rewriting**; respond to producer notes with "Yes, but..."; Principle 47: Learn to enjoy cutting; Principle 48: Whenever in doubt, throw it out; experiment (proving something should be cut is itself a success); leave the anger inside the script.
- Diamond: A first draft is something "no sane person would buy"; address macro concepts first (the three C's, premise, genre expectations) before micro details; **the Mrs. Thomas revision** (give every secondary character a distinct perspective and voice, casting them in your mind); trimming: cut the heads and tails ("A scene truly begins only when a clear objective is introduced and either supported or obstructed"), eliminate purposeless scenes, and cut two to three lines per page without losing dramatic punch (cutting five pages out of *Moon Landing on Guam* in a few hours without the director noticing); pacing matters more than page count; never show work to industry professionals before doing your absolute best.
- Hoxter: Technical pass (typos, formatting, internal consistency) → creative pass (tightening descriptions, unifying voice) → targeted mechanical overhaul against specific objectives (Act II is usually the problem); preserve every version; print out hard copies to mark up; track character arcs against the outline.
- Henson: Use checklists for character biographies; if six people read it and five like it, you are safe; if five say it feels limp, start over.
- Field: Maintain a "Best Scenes" file; "If you are unsure whether a scene works, it probably doesn't"; show your script to two friends who will tell the unvarnished truth, not four.

### Grace: television development documents

- Yvonne Grace, *Writing for Television*, describes a pitch of at most one page, or one paragraph for a verbal story meeting. Name the world, period, jeopardy, and key characters. Her reported treatment range is four to ten pages, not a universal page rule.
- Grace uses the treatment to develop the idea and a series outline to show later episodes in broad strokes. Use `sw-series-showrunning` for continuing-series design.
- Grace distinguishes a scene-by-scene outline, which states each scene's content and intention, from a storyline document that groups an episode's A, B, and C story beats and handover points. Use the adapted templates in [reference.md](reference.md).

## V. Adaptation

- **Field**: "Apples and oranges"—an adapted screenplay is an original script that takes the source material as its point of departure; you need not remain faithful to the original book, only faithful to the "integrity of the original material"; *All the President's Men* ends halfway through the source book; Coppola's word-for-word fidelity in *The Great Gatsby* resulted in "a visually magnificent failure"; novels depict internal life, plays depict spoken language, and screenplays depict external behavior; adapting stage plays requires visual dramatization of events merely spoken of in dialogue; historical dramas need not reproduce characters with strict accuracy, but events and historical outcomes must remain accurate; biographical adaptations select only a few key events (a female editor using a specific news story as her hook); journalism moves from the specific to the universal, whereas screenwriting moves from the universal to the specific; adapting a disaster novel might begin on page 278 with the glacial measurements, constructing an entirely new Act III.
- **McKee**: Principle One—The purer the novel (internal conflict) and the purer the play (interpersonal conflict), the worse the film will be; choose material featuring all three levels of conflict with heavy emphasis on the personal-extra-personal dimension (*The Bridge on the River Kwai*); method: read the original repeatedly without taking notes until you can "smell the cologne," condense each event into one or two sentences, and ask "Is this a well-told story?" (nine times out of ten, it is not); Principle Two—Be willing to reinvent: rearrange chronology, craft a step outline, cut and add scenes, and discover visual expressions for internal conflicts; Miloš Forman: "You must give birth to your child a second time"; "The camera cannot photograph thought"—novelists save their best prose for narration rather than scene dialogue; to adapt, one must reinvent from the inside out.
- **Hoxter's Four Questions**: Do you hold the rights (if not, do not do it)? Can it be made cinematic (visual)? What are the distinct properties of the source medium (the panels and page turns of a comic)? Can the original thematic symbolic system be carried over? Method: construct an outline following the original strictly first, then begin cutting and reshaping.
- **Bork on Adapting True Stories**: Reality does not possess dramatic story structure; real events must be organized, edited, manipulated, exaggerated, and infused with fictional elements; two primary questions—why does the goal matter, and how difficult is the journey (it is the "perilous, obstacle-strewn ordeal" that makes a movie); in reality, there was no airport firefight in *Argo*; Graham Yost's method: absorb the material thoroughly → determine what story you are telling → write in one continuous burst → return to cross-reference with historical research.
- **Hicks on True Stories**: Consult an attorney first and have the subject sign a Life Story Option Agreement; "Never assume that writing about your sister or uncle means you automatically have the rights"; public figures retain rights, and the privacy rights of non-public figures are paramount.
- Walter: Adapting bestselling novels is often disappointing (cinema cannot adequately capture open fantasy; one can count the buttons on a shirt, but it cannot match the fluid, shifting impressions in a reader's mind); "Craft sweet lies rather than literal truth."
- Lu Jun's five methods for transforming source material learned from Shakespeare (elevating secondary characters' worldviews, reframing the antagonist, heightening the protagonist's wisdom while stripping passive endurance, patching plausibility holes while revealing character, and redesigning the climax to align all characters on a single collision course for a swift resolution)—see `sw-premise-theme`.

## VI. Diagnostic Checklist

1. Does the title page contain only the title and author name? Are scene numbers, dates, draft numbers, and registration numbers absent? Is it Courier 12? Is the page count between 90 and 120?
2. Do Scene Headings contain the three core elements, moving from general to specific? Are specific details kept in action paragraphs?
3. Action paragraphs: Present tense, active voice, zero instances of is/are, concrete nouns and action verbs, no camera jargon, no "we see," no "thinks / remembers / realizes," no "does not answer," and sentences ≤10 words?
4. How many seconds does each action paragraph occupy on screen? Is it worth the audience's money ($?)? Does it have purpose, value, and optimal execution (3 Strikes)?
5. Are character names capitalized with ages on first appearance, giving only gender, age, and a single distinctive action? Are character lists, background dossiers, and casting notes omitted?
6. Does dialogue avoid bad page breaks, unneeded parentheticals, underlining, exclamation points, phonetic dialect, and meaningless grunts?
7. Are you writing master scenes only? Can Montages be avoided or compressed into "chase + key beats"? Are Flashbacks marked with minimal "cheat" indicators?
8. Before writing, did you complete a one-page synopsis, statement of purpose, dramatic premise, character action chart, and step outline? Have you pitched it verbally for ten minutes?
9. Does the treatment or outline contain subtext while omitting dialogue? Which intermediate step was skipped?
10. Did revision tackle macro concepts before micro details? Were heads and tails trimmed? Does every secondary character have a unique voice? Does pacing take precedence over page count?
11. Adaptation: On which dimension does the original conflict primarily reside? What has been reinvented? Are the rights or option agreements signed? Have the true story's "big stakes" and "perilous journey" been identified?
12. Output: Is the deliverable a `.fountain` file (Hollywood format) or Scene Numbering Format plain text, rather than Markdown? In Chinese Fountain files, does every Scene Heading, character name, and transition have forced markers? Are V.O. and O.S. used correctly? Does split dialogue include (MORE) and (CONT'D)? Did you ask whether the user wants Hollywood format, Scene Numbering Format, or Japanese format?

## VII. Workflow: From Idea to Submission Draft

1. One- or two-sentence logline + one-page summary → gather feedback on the idea from three readers (Bork and Diamond's test: is it "I have to have this" or merely "Okay, I get it"?).
2. The three C's + reference film list + one-page nine-step synopsis + thesis (theme).
3. Statement of purpose and dramatic premise (Henson); character action chart (Diamond); three-dimensional profile (see `sw-character-conflict`).
4. Step outline / scene cards (McKee, Field, Walter): one to two sentences per scene, marking the Value Turn; story meld + connective tissue + subplots + set piece placement.
5. Pitch the story aloud for ten minutes; write a treatment (no dialogue, rich in subtext) or prose treatment.
6. First draft: track by daily page targets, write long rather than short, and do not revise backward beyond the previous day's single page.
7. Revised draft: cut directorial instructions, furniture, adjectives, and adverbs; convert to simple present tense; cut two to three lines per page; trim heads and tails; apply the Mrs. Thomas revision; perform technical formatting checks.
8. Agent draft: share only with readers who understand professional screenwriting; target five out of six readers liking the script.
9. Market submission draft: PDF format; minimalist title page; WGA registration completed (without noting registration numbers on the title page).

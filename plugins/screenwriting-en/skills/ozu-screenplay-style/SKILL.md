# Ozu Screenplay Style (小津安二郎剧本写法)

This skill is distilled from a scene-by-scene breakdown of six complete screenplays in *The Screenplays of Ozu Yasujiro* (written by Noda Kogo and Ozu Yasujiro, Chinese edition translated by Wu Fei): *Late Spring* (1949, 103 scenes), *Early Summer* (1951, 145 scenes), *Tokyo Story* (1953, 176 scenes), *Floating Weeds* (1959, 121 scenes), *Late Autumn* (1960, 110 scenes), and *An Autumn Afternoon* (1962, 98 scenes).

Core tenet: **All major events occur off-screen; the screenplay portrays only the everyday domestic life immediately before and after the event.** What the audience witnesses is not the event itself, but the ripples it leaves across human relationships.

For scene-by-scene structural outlines and original script excerpts for each film, see [reference.md](reference.md) in the same directory.

## 1. The Common Skeleton of the Six Films: A Family Member Leaves

| Title | Core Event | Driver | Lie / Scheme | Absent Figure | Closing Pillow Shot |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Late Spring | Father marries off daughter | Aunt | Father falsely claims he is remarrying | Suitor Satake | Apple peel breaks -> Night sea |
| Early Summer | Family marries off daughter | Managing director + entire family | None (daughter decides herself) | Suitor Manabe (face unclear in photo) | Barley field + bridal procession of strangers |
| Tokyo Story | Parents visit children -> Mother dies | None (Time) | None | — | Deserted platform at dawn -> Steam launch receding into distance |
| Floating Weeds | Father meets illegitimate son | Mistress | "Uncle" persona; bribes someone to seduce him | — | Night train |
| Late Autumn | Mother marries off daughter | Three old schoolmates | Friends fabricate mother's "remarriage"; mother's "wish to stay single" blurs truth and fiction | Goto (appears, but functionally weak) | Folding haori jacket -> Empty hallway |
| An Autumn Afternoon | Father marries off daughter | Old schoolmates + mirror characters | Two practical jokes (faked death, faked matchmaking) | Matchmaking prospect ("very tall") | Dressing mirror in the dark |

Four films center on "marrying off a daughter," one on "losing a wife," and one on "acknowledging a child." **The true event is always "a family member leaves."** The script depicts the everyday domestic life before the departure and the emptiness left behind.

To write an Ozu-style screenplay, answer three questions first:
1. Who is leaving this family? (The departer)
2. Who is left behind? (The keeper = the protagonist)
3. Who is pushing this to happen? (The driver—usually acting with selfish motives)

## 2. Fifteen Writing Techniques Repeated Across Films (Ranked by Reusability)

### 1. Events Off-Screen, Reactions On-Screen
- Weddings, matchmaking sessions, deaths, proposals, and confessions are never depicted on-screen. Scene 74 of *Late Spring* skips straight to "a week has already passed since the matchmaking meeting"; the wedding shows only a quiet "Father..." before the mirror before leaving, followed by the tavern and empty room upon return.
- Mother's death in *Tokyo Story*: Scene 143 shows the father keeping vigil -> Scene 144 "Dawn: An empty platform—deserted street—gentle waves lapping against the stone seawall" -> Scene 145 "Tomi's face covered with a white cloth." No death rattle, no weeping or shouting.
- *Early Summer* contains not a single proposal scene; Noriko accepts Kenkichi through his mother. When Kenkichi returns tipsy and is told "she is willing to come," he only mutters, "Come where? ... Come for what?"
- **Directive: Identify the three largest events in the screenplay and push all of them off-screen. Write only the scene immediately preceding each event and the scene immediately following it.** Carry the event itself through a sequence of uninhabited landscape shots.

### 2. Lie-Driven Narrative Momentum
- In *Late Spring*, the father falsely claims he is remarrying -> the daughter agrees to marry; the father never confesses the fabrication to his daughter on-screen, and the truth is spoken only by an outsider (Aya) near the end: "I could never tell such a beautiful lie."
- In *Late Autumn*, three old schoolmates invent a "remarriage" for the mother -> the daughter accuses her completely oblivious mother of being "filthy" -> on their final night together, the mother says, "Mother still prefers to stay alone... please do not think Mother lied just to marry you off"—a line readable both as a white lie and heartfelt truth, with the character herself denying it was ever a lie.
- In *Floating Weeds*, the identity lie of the "uncle"; Sumiko pays Kayo one thousand yen to seduce Kiyoshi.
- In *An Autumn Afternoon*, two practical jokes (Kawai claiming Horie "died"; Horie claiming "a matchmaking meeting took place yesterday") rehearse what the protagonist dreads most, both delivered as teasing banter by those closest to him.
- **Directive: Have the driver tell a well-intentioned lie; let the truth be exposed by an onlooker or left unrevealed forever; render the parent's final declaration indistinguishable between truth and falsehood.**

### 3. Minimal-Syllable Confessions
- In Scene 71 of *Late Spring*, three simple "Yes" utterances carry the entire turning point (see Example A in the reference): "Remarriage?" "Yes..." "You are getting remarried, aren't you?" "Yes." "To that person from today?" "Yes."
- In Scene 119 of *Early Summer*, during the family conference: "Definitely not?" "No." "Truly not?" "..." "Positively not?" "No." Followed by the parents: "Mm... mm... mm... let us sleep..."
- **Directive: Deliver key admissions or refusals in a single syllable, prompting the other person to ask three times in succession; write silence ("...") as a complete line of dialogue.**

### 4. Relaying Through Go-Betweens
- The aunt shuttles messages up and down the stairs, translating Noriko's quiet "Yes" into "She says she is willing to marry!" (*Late Spring*, Scenes 77–80); Koichi peeks out from behind the sliding door three times to coach his sister-in-law: "Ask her tactfully" (*Early Summer*, Scene 61); Shige phones to offload her parents onto Noriko (*Tokyo Story*, Scene 58); the trio sits around a table scheming (*Late Autumn*); the father sends his son to inquire with Miura (*An Autumn Afternoon*, Scenes 64–71).
- **Directive: Always place an intermediary between the protagonist and their prospect; authoritative instructions must pass through a go-between, turning the act of relaying into comedy.**

### 5. Faceless Suitors
- Satake receives only three teasing descriptions: "looks like Gary Cooper / looks like an electric meter reader / Kumataro"; Manabe is represented only by a golf photograph where "the face cannot be seen clearly at all," an entry in the gentleman's directory, and word of mouth; Okazaki's second son is merely described as "very tall."
- **Directive: Never reveal the face of the suitor who is chosen or rejected; supply only three descriptive lines from others. The audience has no need to see whom the protagonist turns down—the wrong choice is merely a collection of information.**

### 6. Pillow-Shot Scene Breaks
- Between every emotional sequence, insert a 1- to 2-line landscape scene: "Long afternoon shadows—the calls of bush warblers"; empty seats in the theater -> Noriko walking alone in Marunouchi (*Late Spring*, Scenes 40–42); carp streamers; a drifting balloon with a severed string; barley fields; the night sea; a train.
- **Directive: Format transitional pillow shots with independent scene numbers, described in a single line without emotional adjectives or narrative commentary.**

### 7. Echoing Objects and Catchphrases
- "Filthy" (*Late Spring*, Scenes 25 -> 26 -> 85 -> 88 -> 90, appearing five times: evolving from teasing banter into profound guilt); pickled radish sliced with lingering threads appearing twice, its meaning reversed the second time; the smoking pipe (*Late Autumn*, four times: Akiko gives it to Mamiya -> Ayako delivers it to the executive office -> Taguchi receives one as well -> at the end, Hirayama remarks, "Lucky you, everyone received a pipe"); the Warship March (*An Autumn Afternoon*, three times: Sakamoto salutes and circles -> Hirayama politely declines -> on the wedding night he requests it deliberately, with a drunken patron scoffing "we lost the war" -> hummed alone in the finale); "Blessed" (*Tokyo Story*, three times: neighbor's envy -> Numata's sarcasm -> the old couple's self-consolation, cheapening in value each time); "Spreading a quilt over a grave" (Keizo first uses it as a quip, later as remorse); matches (two struck at the end of *Floating Weeds*).
- **Directive: Select a small object or short phrase and have it recur three times, advancing its meaning with each shift in context (joke -> wound -> reconciliation, or envy -> irony -> self-derision). The deceased person's belongings can circulate among supporting characters, concluding by counting "who ended up empty-handed."**

### 8. Supporting Characters Speaking for the Protagonist
- In *Tokyo Story*, the elderly parents never complain themselves: Minoru throws a tantrum over his desk being moved, Kyoko fumes "they are far too selfish," and Numata grumbles "they treat me like a burden"; the heaviest comment the old couple makes about their situation is delivered with a gentle smile: "We have ended up homeless after all."
- In *Late Autumn*, Yuriko storms into the executive office to confront the three older men: "Why throw stones into a calm pond just to stir up trouble?"
- In *An Autumn Afternoon*, the daughter turns the tables: "Father, didn't you feel the same way? Thinking it would be better if I just stayed here like this..." leaving the father able only to reply, "That is not true."
- **Directive: Let the protagonist's grievances be voiced by characters of the lowest social standing; entrust confrontations with authority to the youngest outsider, followed immediately by comedic deflation (drunkenness / a sushi bar); have the protagonist merely smile or gaze at the sky.**

### 9. Truth Spoken Only to Outsiders
- Shukichi to his old friend Numata: "This is the selfish desire of parents everywhere, and selfish desire knows no bounds"; Tomi to her daughter-in-law Noriko: "If there is someone suitable, you should marry them"; Shukichi's final words to Noriko: "Compared to our own flesh and blood, it is you, an outsider, who has treated us best..."; Akiko to Yuriko; Komajuro to Oyoshi.
- **Directive: Place the most intimate lines within relationships outside blood ties; assign the "thematic debate" to two young characters (Kyoko/Noriko), and anchor the "emotional conclusion" in an everyday domestic remark ("Had I known, I should have treated her better while she was still alive").**

### 10. Mirror Characters
- Onodera's remarriage (*Late Spring*) -> Father's "remarriage"; Numata's son "exaggerating section chief into department director" (*Tokyo Story*) -> Shukichi's disappointment; the Gourd and his daughter (72/48, noodle shop) -> Hirayama and his daughter (57/24) thirty years later; Horie's 28-year-old second wife -> a farcical version of remarriage.
- *An Autumn Afternoon*: Hirayama sees the Gourd and his daughter (Scenes 26–27) -> hears Kawai warn "if you are careless, you will end up just like that" (Scene 30) -> hears the Gourd's confession "I kept my daughter by my side for my own convenience... I was wrong" (Scene 57) -> returns home that very night and asks "don't you want to marry?" (Scene 60). The decision never originates from the protagonist's internal monologue.
- **Directive: Provide the protagonist with a mirror figure who has already lived out their future path, prompting the protagonist to make a decision the very night they witness it. Use a secondary character to "rehearse" the theme first (Noriko initially calling Onodera's remarriage "filthy"), then project that exact dilemma onto the protagonist.**

### 11. Children's Subplots as Metaphor
- In *Early Summer*, young Minoru wants a toy railway track, but his father brings home bread -> Minoru kicks the bread -> gets slapped -> runs away; simultaneously, the entire family is vetting "well-qualified" suitors for Noriko—the adults offer bread, while the child yearns for the track.
- In *Tokyo Story*, Minoru throws a tantrum because his study desk is moved out into the hallway; in *Late Spring*, painting the baseball bat red.
- **Directive: Have a child grow furious over a small item that serves as an unspoken metaphor for the main plot; the two storylines need never refer to each other in dialogue.**

### 12. Ensemble Catchphrase Relays
- In Scene 40 of *Early Summer*, three women engage in a rapid-fire verbal round-robin: "Right?" "Don't you think so?" "Right?" setting married and unmarried camps against each other; in Scene 65 of *Late Autumn*, the trio repeats: "—Ah, there is no rushing it..." "Yes, no rushing it..."
- **Directive: During ensemble scenes, let a short phrase bounce between three characters, using repetition to establish alliances and rhythm rather than conveying exposition.**

### 13. Displaced Quarrels
- In Scene 68 of *Late Spring*, the quarrel over the shortcake: "Please eat it!" "I simply do not want to eat it!" "I told you to eat it! I will make you eat it even if you do not want to!" "I will not!"—the argument revolves entirely around "eating cake," while the true subtext is "getting married."
- In *Early Summer*, Takako and her husband bicker over carrots and a smoking pipe; in *An Autumn Afternoon*, Koichi and his wife squabble over golf clubs and a white leather handbag (Scenes 45–51).
- **Directive: Characters never argue over the real issue; they fight over a trivial possession. Build the exchange entirely from short, repetitive phrases, relying on rhythm rather than exposition to release emotion. Use an argument over household items between a young married couple to show the audience "what married life really looks like," replacing expository commentary on marriage.**

### 14. The Single-Monologue Rule
- Each film contains exactly one extended speech, reserved for the penultimate movement: Shukichi discussing happiness (*Late Spring*, Scene 93, roughly 300 words), Akiko's final night (*Late Autumn*, Scene 101), the Gourd's confession (*An Autumn Afternoon*, Scene 57), and Shukichi discussing selfish desires (*Tokyo Story*, Scene 101). Every preceding "Yes" and "..." exists to build emotional pressure toward this release.
- **Directive: Permit only one monologue exceeding 150 words across the entire script, positioned at the story's ultimate emotional outlet.**

### 15. Diminishing Endings
- *Late Spring*: Tavern -> home -> empty pillow shot; *An Autumn Afternoon*: Friend's house -> bar -> home -> dressing mirror in the dark; *Late Autumn*: Trio's restaurant (laughter) -> apartment (folding haori) -> empty corridor.
- **Directive: Following the climax, scale down spatial intimacy progressively: "social space -> semi-private space -> home -> uninhabited space," ending on an unpeopled scene. Grant one scene each to the comedic thread and the thread of isolation, laughing first, emptying last.**

## 3. Character Configuration Template

| Role | Function | Film Examples |
|---|---|---|
| The Keeper | The left-behind parent, protagonist; passively absorbs emotional weight | Shukichi x3, Akiko, Komajuro, Hirayama |
| The Departer | Daughter / son / late wife; decides in an instant, explaining with mundane domestic analogies | Noriko x3, Ayako, Michiko, Kiyoshi, Tomi |
| The Driver | Matches / urges / pressures, driven by private interests, prone to missteps | Aunt Masa, Director Satake, The Trio, Sumiko |
| The Contrast | Peer friend of the departer, holding opposing views (divorcee / single advocate / sushi shop daughter) | Aya, Yuriko, Takako |
| The Mirror | The protagonist's future self | Onodera, Numata, The Gourd, Horie |
| The Go-Between | Relayer of messages | Sister-in-law Fumiko, son Koichi, daughter-in-law Akiko, Aunt |
| The Absent Figure | Deceased / missing figure, anchored to a tangible object | Mother (wristwatch), Shoji (barley ear), Shoji (photograph/quilt), Miwa Shuzo (pipe), Kimura (advance scout who never returned) |
| The Onlooker | Framing the opening and close | Neighbor lady ("What a blessing" / "So quiet again"), Shige and her husband, ferry waiting room attendant |

Key Configuration Points:
- **Write the driver's motivation honestly**: In *Late Autumn*, the three older men are secretly motivated by old affections for their deceased friend's wife; their wives know this and tease them ("using her as your little distraction").
- **Every word spoken by the most abrasive character must make pragmatic sense**: Shige spending 3,000 yen to send her parents to Atami because "it saves money," packing mourning clothes "hoping we won't need them," demanding the obi sash and linen fabrics the moment her mother dies, and noting "it would have been better if Father had gone first"—she is not malicious, merely "busy." The script never judges her; judgment is left to the audience and the younger characters.
- **The moralist betrays their principles in the very next scene**: In *Floating Weeds*, Kichinosuke rails against a troupe mate contemplating theft, shouting that "anyone who forgets kindness is worse than a dog," only to steal the troupe leader's wallet and flee in the subsequent scene. No explanation offered.
- **The absent figure serves as narrative glue**: Shoji, who died in the war, threads through *Early Summer*—the credit bureau discovers "Shoji and Kenkichi were classmates" -> "He is never coming back" -> "There was a barley ear enclosed in Shoji's letter" -> the title. Noriko's trust in Kenkichi stems from "he was my brother's friend."
- **The deceased needs only a living person who "somewhat resembles" them**: "There is a woman over there who looks just like your mother when she was young... when she lowers her head, she looks a little bit like her here." No flashback scenes required.
- **Old monikers introduce temporal depth**: Teachers are referred to exclusively by nicknames (The Gourd, The Lion, The Badger, The Emperor); Sakamoto addresses Hirayama as "Captain"; Hirayama calls the Gourd "Sensei." The opening words instantly evoke decades of history.

## 4. Master Plot Structure: "Marrying Off a Daughter" (Directly Applicable)

Synthesized from the sequence of movements in *Late Spring*, *Early Summer*, *Late Autumn*, and *An Autumn Afternoon*:

| Movement | Content | Technique |
|---|---|---|
| Prologue | Small talk at station / breakfast / memorial service; pillow shots establish the season | Establish family dynamics through an onlooker or a meal; introduce the marriage topic in the very first scene (clerk collecting contributions in *An Autumn Afternoon*) |
| Setup | Relaxed everyday intimacy between father and daughter (or mother and daughter) | Codependent domestic habits ("pour tea, pour tea"); surface dialogue consists of trivialities (asking directions, mahjong, spelling) |
| False Lead | Lead audience to suspect someone else is the suitor (Hattori) | Deliver the reversal through comedic timing (Noriko suppressing laughter while asking for tea) |
| First Push | Driver proposes matchmaking; daughter refuses, claiming "I must care for Father/Mother" | The proposed suitor never appears on-screen |
| Mirror Appears | Protagonist glimpses their own future (Gourd and daughter / Onodera's remarriage) | Have the daughter judge the mirror figure first ("Remarriage is filthy") |
| Midpoint | Driver suggests the parent should remarry (real or fabricated) | Daughter's "face suddenly clouds over"—the true raw nerve is struck |
| Cold War | Almost zero dialogue | "Going shopping..." departing dejectedly; empty seats at the theater |
| Dialogue-Free Climax | Nodding / bowing / gazing at the Noh theater | Portray jealousy purely through stares, without a single line concerning marriage |
| Displaced Outburst | Quarrel over shortcake / golf clubs | Repetition of short phrases |
| Showdown | Three "Yes" answers | Daughter dashes upstairs in tears |
| Resolution | Contrasting peer advises marriage ("Men are all worthless... marry anyway"); daughter accepts | Parent asks, "You are not just giving up, are you?"—the decision is reluctant, and the parent knows it |
| Farewell Journey | Kyoto / Ikaho / Lake Haruna | The script's single extended monologue belongs here |
| Wedding Day | A single "Father..." before the mirror -> "Ah, I understand" -> empty room with only mirror and chair | The wedding = a single still photograph or group portrait |
| Epilogue | Tavern -> home -> pillow shot | Truth uncovered by a third party; apple peel snaps; humming the Warship March: "All alone in the end" |

Variations:
- *Early Summer* places the "decision" before the wrong person (Kenkichi's mother remarking "just an idle daydream of mine"); the protagonist's wedding is omitted, concluding instead with the bridal procession of strangers.
- *Late Autumn* renders the drivers as a comedic trio acting out of personal self-interest, causing the daughter's accusation to strike an innocent target (the daughter denounces her mother as "filthy" while the mother knows nothing about it—the audience learns the truth first).
- *An Autumn Afternoon* incorporates "missed opportunity": the young man the daughter fancied is already engaged, caused by the father's own hesitation ("Didn't you say back then she wouldn't be marrying for quite a while?")—**the protagonist's hesitation itself becomes the root cause of loss, pinpointed by a single line from an outsider.**

## 5. Reusable Framework: "Losing a Loved One" (*Tokyo Story*)

1. Opening onlooker frame: "What a blessing."
2. Arrival: The son's home falls short of expectations—remarking only "I thought it would be a bit livelier," offering no explicit critique.
3. First abandonment: The grandchildren's anger speaks on behalf of the grandparents.
4. "Outsourced": Daughter phones to pass the duty off to the daughter-in-law.
5. The only heartfelt hospitality relies on "borrowed" liquor and takeout food.
6. Filial piety misinterpreted by children as sending parents away (Atami); illness foreshadowed with a single physical cue: "her body swayed slightly, as if struck by dizziness."
7. Midpoint: The old man's sole commentary on their plight is delivered with a smile: "We have ended up homeless after all."
8. **Parallel nights**: The father's drinking binge cross-cut with the mother's nighttime conversation at the daughter-in-law's flat—one voicing disillusionment through drunkenness, the other speaking of grace beneath the quilts, separated throughout the night. The audience pieces their thoughts together.
9. Death foreshadowed three times and brushed aside: "body swayed slightly," "getting forgetful lately," "if anything happens to us, you need not trouble yourselves to travel back"—cut short each time with lighthearted laughter.
10. Death occurs within pillow shots.
11. Funeral: Mourning and petty calculations share the same table; a colloquial quip first uttered as a joke returns as bitter remorse.
12. Chain of passing objects: Shoji's photograph -> Shoji's quilt -> Tomi's wristwatch -> Noriko holding the watch to her ear on the train. The final object is "heard" at the conclusion.
13. Epilogue circles back to the neighbor lady: "So quiet again."

## 6. Secret-Driven Structure (*Floating Weeds*): Layered Information Leaks

- Audience learns first (Scenes 23–27) -> Yatazo sees through the barbershop mirror (45) -> Sumiko interrogates (48) -> Sensho mentions "a lifetime bond" (50) -> Rokuzaburo points through the lattice window of the music room (57) -> Sumiko confronts the shop (69) -> Kayo is dispatched (77) -> Kiyoshi learns Kayo was sent on purpose (90) -> Oyoshi discloses his parentage (115).
- **Each link leaks only a fraction of the truth, always passing through a "third party"; the protagonist never confesses willingly.**
- Information travels through "seeing" rather than "hearing": mirrors, lattice windows, "(long shot)," "long shot from inner room"—camera viewpoints explicitly designated in the script.
- The turning point occurs when the manipulated pawn confesses voluntarily (Kayo: "I intended to deceive you at first"), not when they are exposed.
- **The immediate reaction to revelation is rejection** ("I do not want a father! Tell him to get out!"), leaving reconciliation entirely off-screen (Oyoshi: "Deep down, he has already forgiven you").
- The protagonist's physical violence meets resistance only at the end, delivered by the very person he sought to protect (the son strikes back with a punch, instantly followed by the revelation of his birth).
- Major confrontations escalate across three tiers: **debts of gratitude -> social origin -> bloodline**, ending with an irrevocable line ("My boy is not made of the same coarse stuff as you lot").
- Play-within-a-play dialogue foretells the outcome of the main plot (Chuji: "Setting out on a journey with no destination and no end" -> Komajuro boarding the night train); those watching the performance are themselves observed.
- Reconciliation hinges on a small, repeatable action: the first match is refused, the second accepted, followed by borrowing the flame in reverse—not a single word of apology uttered throughout.

## 7. Dialogue Writing

### 1. Surface Idle Chatter
- Asking directions ("Is the sea this way?" "No, that way"), winning on a loose tile in mahjong, spelling Liszt (L-I-S-Z-T), "What play are they putting on this time?"
- Function: Establishes relaxed intimacy; **allows weightier matters to be slipped into idle chatter** ("How about Noriko?" "It is about time that girl settled down").
- Directive: Conceal the operative line in the middle of casual banter, wrapped in everyday small talk before and after.

### 2. Stage Direction Tone Vocabulary (Original Terms)
Faintly / Coldly / Indistinctly / Brightly / With a gentle smile / Dejectedly / As if talking to oneself / Lowering voice / As if nothing happened / Filled with deep feeling / Involuntarily / Increasingly sharp / As if doubting one's own ears / Filled with emotion / Murmuring contentedly as before / Suddenly wilting.

### 3. A Defining Catchphrase per Character
Oyoshi: "I have long grown used to it" / "He will return... he will surely return"; Komajuro: "Ah, there is simply no helping this line of work"; Shukichi: "Mm..."; Shige: "You really are something." Repetition creates character.

### 4. Grounding Motivations in Plain Domestic Analogies
Noriko explains why she chose Kenkichi: "It is like cutting fabric—you cannot find where you put the scissors, you search all over, and then suddenly realize they were right before your eyes." When Aya presses, "Then you love him!" Noriko insists, "No! ... He is someone I can trust." **No psychological jargon.**

### 5. Thematic Lines Spoken by Youth, Misinterpreted and Relayed by Elders
Ayako: "Love and marriage should be considered separately" -> The three older men relaying it on the golf course: "That only shows how much harder the world has become."

### 6. The Three Steps of Acceptance
Smiling acceptance ("It is fine, Father... I only don't want to have regrets later") -> A third party reporting tears ("Sister seems to be crying upstairs") -> The parent walking up to say, "If you truly dislike it, you can refuse," before stepping onto the veranda to gaze at the sky.

### 7. Ritualistic Actions Replacing Explicit Confessions
Taking a group photo (Noriko asking to take one more photo of her parents alone, without any line stating "this will be the last time"), farewell banquets, applying lipstick before a mirror (= farewell to Tokyo life), folding a haori jacket, peeling an apple.

### 8. Reaction Queues Following Major News
*Early Summer*, Scenes 118–121: Family meeting concludes -> parents trudge upstairs -> Noriko applies lipstick -> Koichi and his wife remain rooted in place; Scenes 133–134: Old couple stands up abruptly and walks out -> Koichi moves to his desk -> Fumiko traces shapes in the brazier ash -> Noriko eats ochazuke in chilling silence. **Each character reacts individually within their own distinct space, rather than erupting into an ensemble argument.**

## 8. Screenplay Formatting Conventions (Synthesized from Originals)

```
**Scene Number  Location (  Time / Specification)**      <- Bold; location first, time optional
Single-line description of landscape or action--           <- Ending with dash = ongoing / sustained
Character: Dialogue. (action / tone in parentheses)       <- Action inserted directly into dialogue
Character: ...                                            <- Silence written as a complete line
Character: (coldly) What?                                 <- Tone indicators placed in parentheses at start
Exits while speaking.                                     <- Actions on separate lines, subject often omitted
```

- Scenes are exceedingly brief: average 1–1.5 pages, the shortest being a single line ("**22  Shunyokai Exhibition Poster**").
- A single spatial transition is broken into multiple scene numbers: Entryway -> hallway -> living room each receive separate scene headings (Scenes 30–35 of *Tokyo Story* trace continuous action across the same house).
- Time indicators are marked only at the start of major movements: "Next day," "That evening," "Several days later."
- First character appearances include bracketed age and relationships: "Noriko (28, wife of Shukichi's second son Shoji, who died in the war)."
- Stage directions specify directorial viewpoints: "(long shot)," "long shot from inner room," "high-angle shot."
- Sample Scene:

```
**12  Next Day  Platform at Kamakura Station**
The up-train bound for Tokyo has just departed--the station clock--points to around ten thirty-eight.

**13  Near Kamegayatsu Tunnel**
The up-train speeds past.
Emerging from the tunnel--

**14  Inside Third-Class Carriage**
Shukichi and Noriko stand amid the crowd in the swaying train.
Shukichi: You brought the manuscript with you?
Noriko: Yes, do not worry.
```

## 9. Inventory of Thematic Lines (For Direct Citation or Adaptation)

- "Happiness does not wait around for you; you must build it with your own hands. Marriage in itself is not happiness." (*Late Spring*)
- "Somewhere out there, a child who accidentally let go of a balloon must be crying." (*Early Summer*)
- "Perhaps right now is the happiest our family will ever be... Human greed knows no end once it begins." (*Early Summer*)
- "We have ended up homeless after all..." (*Tokyo Story*, spoken with a smile)
- "This is the selfish desire of parents everywhere, and selfish desire knows no bounds." (*Tokyo Story*)
- "Once children grow up, they inevitably leave their parents... Anyone will feel their own life is what matters most." (*Tokyo Story*)
- "Love and marriage should be considered separately." (*Late Autumn*)
- "At this stage in life, having to start climbing all over again from the foot of the mountain—I have had quite enough of that." (*Late Autumn*)
- "I kept my daughter by my side for my own convenience... in the end, one spends this life entirely alone." (*An Autumn Afternoon*)
- "Having an actor for a father is worse than having no father at all." (*Floating Weeds*)
- "When everyone gathers together it only complicates things; who would have thought it was actually this simple?" (*Late Autumn*)

Placement pattern for thematic lines: They appear **while gazing at scenery** (a lost balloon, barley fields, standing on a cliff saying "What a beautiful morning"), never during arguments.

## 10. Workflow: Writing an Ozu-Style Screenplay

1. **Determine the Departer and the Keeper**. Write a one-sentence logline: "X (age, role), in order to let Y leave them, does Z; once Y departs, X remains alone to..."
2. **Establish the Driver and their private self-interest**; give the driver a flawed, idiosyncratic habit (eating apples, clutching a pipe, directing behind a sliding screen).
3. **Establish the Mirror Character**: Who has already lived through the protagonist's future? Outline the scene where the protagonist observes them and the decision made that night.
4. **Establish the Absent Figure and their anchor object**: The deceased / missing person + a tangible artifact (barley ear, wristwatch, pipe, photograph).
5. **Identify the three largest events and push all of them off-screen**; write the scene before and after each event, using pillow shots for the event itself.
6. **Choose an echoing object or catchphrase**, arranging three distinct appearances with advancing emotional weight.
7. **Design a subplot featuring children or a young married couple**, employing a trivial object to mirror the core theme.
8. **Write the showdown scene**: permit only "Yes" and "..."; prompt the other party to ask three times in succession.
9. **Write the displaced quarrel**: argue over cake or golf clubs, using repeating short phrases.
10. **Write the single extended monologue**, positioned in the penultimate movement (night conversation during travel).
11. **Write the diminishing ending**: social space -> home -> uninhabited pillow shot; one comedic beat followed by one solitary beat.
12. **Formatting check**: Bold scene numbers, location first, ongoing dashes, silence written as complete lines, age and identity bracketed upon introduction, transitional pillow shots given independent scene numbers.

## 11. Self-Check Checklist

- [ ] Does the protagonist refrain throughout the entire film from directly venting their own grievances? (They should—supporting characters speak for them)
- [ ] Is the pivotal admission restricted to a single syllable?
- [ ] Is the rejected suitor kept from ever showing their face? (They should remain off-screen)
- [ ] Is there a one-line pillow shot between every emotional movement?
- [ ] Is there only one extended monologue, and is it placed in the penultimate movement?
- [ ] Does the argument revolve around a trivial household item rather than the actual conflict?
- [ ] Is the final closing scene devoid of people? (It should be uninhabited)
- [ ] Are thematic lines delivered while gazing at scenery?
- [ ] Is the driver's selfish motive teased and unmasked by others (spouses/outsiders) with lighthearted laughter?
- [ ] Does the deceased figure possess a symbolic object or a living stand-in who "somewhat resembles" them?

## 1. The Pilot Beat Sheet: Calvisi's Benchmark, the Full Beat Chain, and Miller's Landmark Model

### 1.1 Calvisi's Benchmark (Teaser Plus Five)

**Calvisi's Benchmark (Teaser plus five)**: Teaser 2–10 pages → Act One 12–15 pages → Act Two 6–10 pages (out around page 30) → Act Three 8–12 pages (out at page 40) → Act Four 6–12 pages (out at page 48) → Act Five 2–8 pages.

### 1.2 The Full Beat Chain

**The beat chain**: OPENING SEQUENCE → **CATALYST** (the teaser out) → establish the A/B/C/D lines plus the entrance of the New Arrival / Shadow / Dynamic Ally → **INCITING INCIDENT** → **TURN / Roadblock** (the Act One out) → Aftermath → **FIRST TRIAL / FIRST CASUALTY** → New Complications → **MIDPOINT** (the Act Two out, carrying a ticking time clock) → Aftermath → **SHADOW SHOWDOWN** → Partial Integration → **ASSUMPTION OF POWER** (the Act Three out) → Skirmishes / Integration / Stopping Points → **DECLARATION OF WAR + ALL IS LOST** (the Act Four out) → **CLIMAX (A, B)** → **EPILOGUE / THE NEW WORLD** (or BIG ENDING) → **HANGING QUESTION / CLIFFHANGER**.

### 1.3 Two Directly Checkable Numbers and Four Beats Unique to Television

**Two directly checkable numbers**: **the AOP falls on pages 37–40**; **the DOW sits about 4/5 of the way through** — proportionally, the Shadow Showdown in Scandal, True Detective and The Walking Dead all land around 4/5 (the TWD pilot runs 66 minutes because of its extended premiere slot). **Four beats unique to television** (treat these as dedicated checks when writing a pilot): **Assumption of Power, Declaration of War, Shadow Showdown, First Trial / First Casualty.**

### 1.4 Calvisi's Own Boundaries

**Calvisi's own boundaries**: beats overlap and shift back and forth; "this is not an exact science. It cannot perfectly fit every one-hour television pilot."; some shows subvert it entirely ("Game of Thrones with its multi-protagonist structure comes to mind"); with a four-act show (True Detective, Mad Men) you have to merge beats yourself. The qualifier repeated twice across the book: **"This is form, not formula."** One other hard requirement: **"Act One should be able to operate as its own closed story, with a beginning, middle and end**, albeit with a cliffhanger at the end." — and the Act One out must be "a shocking yet inevitable turn in direction."

### 1.5 Miller's Landmark Model (Chapter 3)

**Miller's landmark model** (chapter 3) gives no page numbers, anchoring only to act breaks. The correspondence: **the point of attack sits inside the teaser** ("In television, the point of attack generally happens in the teaser.") / end of Act I = the Act One out / mid-point = the Act Two out / end of Act II = the Act Three out / the climax in Act Four. The six landmarks: **point of attack** (the first whiff of the problem) → **hero committed** (until now they could still turn back to the old life) → **hero succeeds—almost** (the midpoint; **"It's not 'the hero fails'"** — the audience needs to see what success looks like before it is taken away) → **emotional squeeze** (a choice between two bad options, and the choice must show the character has changed) → **hero succeeds** (often with an emotional twist) → **hero's new life**. In a pilot, "hero committed" should kill two birds: in The Knick, Thackery takes the chief-of-surgery job while insisting his deputy be Gallinger rather than the board's choice Edwards — one action commits the protagonist to the whole season and erects this episode's obstacle at once. **Which landmarks make good act outs** (read off the grid of The Walking Dead's "Days Gone Bye"): point of attack, succeeds—almost and new life are all good act ends, as is Rick being shot at the end of Act One; **the squeeze usually carries little suspense and "needs to play out and needs a solid resolution."** **The tragedy variant**: the squeeze flips positive and the climax flips negative (It's Always Sunny's "The Gang Gets Racist") — "a tragedy is the inverse of a comedy."

### 1.6 Three Strategies for Ending a Pilot (Landau 1e Tip 17)

**Three strategies for ending a pilot (Landau 1e Tip 17)**: **Launching the Franchise** (the last two words of The Killing pilot script are "Find him.") / **The Final Montage** (Boardwalk Empire, Friday Night Lights) / **The Final Discovery** (Vic shooting his colleague Terry Crowley in the face in The Shield). The floor: "give us something to discover by the end." The matching withholding principle: "**don't tell us everything in the pilot**."

### 1.7 Structure Is Inherited: the Breaking Bad Pilot Against #308

- **Structure is inherited.** Calvisi compares the Breaking Bad pilot (T 1-3 / A1 4-17 / A2 18-31 / A3 32-43 / A4 44-57) against the same show's #308 (T 1-4 / 5-15 / 16-31 / 32-42 / 43-53): the series reuses the pilot's act structure and page ranges. **Writing the pilot casts the structural mould for the whole show.**

## 2. The Episode Container: Page Anchors, Scene Ratios, Act Conversion, Page Count and Episode Length

### 2.1 The Four-Act Page Anchors (Douglas p0090–0091)

**Page anchors** (Douglas p0090–0091, a four-act draft including a teaser): **Act One out at pages 17–18 / Act Two at page 30 / Act Three at page 45 / Act Four around page 60 ("or anywhere between page 55 and page 65")**. The cause: commercials come "roughly every 13 to 15 minutes… after the breaks you have less than 50 minutes left, and no act runs a full 15 — about 12 and change."

### 2.2 Scene Counts and Three Sets of Ratios (Douglas / Oberg / Chase)

Douglas's arithmetic: "If an act runs 14 minutes and a scene runs about 2, 14 divided by 2 is 7." The elasticity: five solid scenes can fill an act, and an episodic show may count 9–10. Totals: **a four-act hour generally runs about 28 scenes; a five- or six-act show only 20.** Two more cross-checkable rulers: **Oberg's ratio** — A around 50% (14–15 scenes), B around a third (9–10), C the remainder (4–5), about 30 in total, though he warns that "a modern scene runs about 1 minute, an important one 3–4, and the old 'two-minute scenes' model is out of date"; **Chase (in his Sopranos foreword)** — about 35 beats an episode, "thirteen or so in the A and B stories, usually five to six in the C story, and sometimes a D story with just a few beats."

### 2.3 Converting Four Acts Into Five or Six

**Douglas (p0091–0092)**: start by conceiving a four-act show → **stretch the teaser into roughly 10 pages so that it becomes a new act** ("you must inject the dangerous, the contentious or the active straight into the story from the opening") → each later act runs about 10 pages, or about 8 pages at six acts. **The six-act tail design** (the single most useful item): commonly all arcs complete at the end of Act Five, "and then a very short Act Six adds an unexpected turn, one that… raises a new subject or a new challenge rather than closing out an existing story line" — that is, **Act Six is really the next episode's teaser** (Nip/Tuck). Other answers: **Peter Blake (House)** writes four acts and then adds two breaks, **splitting whichever later act is longest**; **Miller**: six acts = the four-act version with **acts three and four each split in two**, and the new act break still has to "hit…hard with some holy shit! moments"; **Douglas's five-act recognition test**: "look for which two acts are the shortest."

### 2.4 Page Count and Episode Length (per author)

**One-hour pilot 54–58 pages, ceiling 60** (Calvisi); **one-hour 48–63 pages**, with fast-paced shows (Scandal, The West Wing, E.R.) running 85+ (Landau); **drama pilot 55–65 pages** (Miller); **one-hour drama about 60 pages, teaser plus four acts** (Blum chapter 14). Episode lengths (Oberg, 2023): ad-supported network 42 minutes / premium 56 / streaming 45–75 / European 50–52 / half-hour comedy 22. Landau 2e on the trend (WandaVision): "**Varying episode length is now a common trend along with shorter season orders. There is no standard, predictive pattern or formula.**"

> **Currency flag**: Blum (early 2000s) and Goldberg & Rabkin (2003) describe the **classic broadcast system** (act count set by ad slots); Landau 1e (2014) reflects the peak of five and six acts on the networks; Landau 2e (2021) and Oberg (2023) reflect "no single industry standard." Cite page counts with the publication year attached.

### 2.5 Whether to Print ACT on the Page: Five Positions

- **Landau 2e**: **break story and outline by act break** (which may be renamed **turning points**), **but delete the labels in the final draft** — "story over format."
- **The counter-position kept on the same page**: many showrunners still want the act breaks on the page, "as evidence that the writer has a clear grasp of the structural twists and turns."
- **Miller chapter 9**: whether to include act breaks is a **market judgement** — mandatory if the content could air on a broadcast network or non-premium cable, since "Leaving out act breaks can make you look like you don't understand television."
- **Mazzara (TWD)**: the pilot had no act breaks, later converted to teaser + 5 acts (AMC wanted another ad slot); "It forces you to make choices. **It's about compression.**"
- **Alex Pina (Money Heist)** on writing for a platform where you do not know where the ads go: "We write in three acts, but permeating these with many **milestone moments**."

## 3. Teaser / Cold Open: Ten Types, Length Figures, and the List of Shows That Do Without

- **Ten types** (Douglas's seven paradigms plus Landau's three): a portrait of the world (the protagonist absent) | a cinematic anomaly (the protagonist does not know yet) | arriving after the fact | the case happens first | an ensemble's day begins | the thematic tie-in (unconnected to this episode's story) | the environmental cue | **bookend** | **(seemingly) innocuous** | **DPU (direct pickup)**.
- **Length**: Douglas (p0097) — the teaser is the cold open, dramatized material before the main titles, "which may be a one-minute hook or a ten-minute montage of small scenes, making the teaser almost equal to a conventional act." Landau 1e Tip 15: roughly 5 pages (**the teaser of The Good Wife pilot runs 16 pages**). Oberg is tighter: **within 3–5 minutes, usually ending on a cliffhanger or a surprise.**
- **A teaser is not compulsory**: Landau 2e — "not every series uses a teaser… and just starts in act 1" (GoT, the Sopranos, Homeland and Mad Men go straight into Act One; Grey's Anatomy and Once Upon a Time are "six acts with no teaser"; Scandal and Modern Family use "a short Act One as the teaser"). Oberg: a teaser is only warranted when it serves a dramatic purpose, otherwise it is "just pointless."

## 4. Invisible Acts: The Succession Measurements Behind the Three-Part Test, and the Season 4 Container Data

### 4.1 The Three Tests, One by One

1. **Story-day or time-block markers** — `day one / day two / day three` (S3E9, S4E5, S4E10), on-screen clock cards (S4E01's "LA 12 p.m."), timestamps (S4E08's 4:54 P.M. → 3:40 A.M.). **A new day is a new act.**
2. **A change of location or of vehicle** — S3E9 runs villa → boat → Pienza → car park → car → villa, six acts against six spaces; "a car = a moving sealed room, one space for a whole act."
3. **The moment the central question turns**, often closing on a "loneliness button" stage direction (at the end of S3E9's second act, Roman scrolls his phone on the lake — "No one to call."). The decision rule: **every act must end on a transfer of information or of power**, not necessarily on a suspense hook.

### 4.2 The Container Pattern and Scene Counts Across Succession Season 4

The accompanying pattern (Succession season 4, ten episodes): **8 of 10 episodes compress the whole episode into a container with a fixed procedure** (a wedding / a retreat / investor day / a party / election night / a funeral), with **the procedure's checkpoints replacing act breaks**; where no ready-made procedure exists, the markers go on the page instead — **that is, streaming writers are still writing acts, they have just disguised the acts as a schedule.** Scene count varies inversely with container density: a closed single day runs 34–42 scenes, a multi-location multi-line episode 76–78. Landau 1e Tip 19 also demonstrates reverse-engineering the six act positions of Homeland's "The Vest."

## 5. Act Outs and Cliffhangers: A Library of Types and Examples

### 5.1 Ten Kinds of Cliffhanger (seven from Landau 2e plus three from 1e)

The seven in 2e: **① character in peril | ② ticking bomb | ③ love is in the balance | ④ surprise twist / aha moment | ⑤ death of a character** ("figuratively re-setting chess pieces and altering relationships") **| ⑥ discovery of a secret | ⑦ life events.** 1e lists three more: **⑧ lack of closure** (the Sopranos cutting to black — "if a cliffhanger feels too ambiguous or information is withheld, there can be viewer backlash"; the counter-example is The Killing season 1 not naming the killer, "Lessons learned.") **| ⑨ mini-cliffhangers = the act outs themselves | ⑩ comedy cliffhangers** (the break between the two acts of a sitcom: something upsets the balance, and Act Two is the effort to restore harmony).

Two highly transferable techniques: **complete the reversal in a single word** (the Succession season 2 finale, where Kendall delivers the full compliant version and then says "But."); **do not go out on the laugh, hold the reveal instead** (the Hacks season 1 finale — "going out with the anticipation of what will happen when Deborah finds out").

### 5.2 The Position and Time Limits of a Cliffhanger

- **Position and time limits**: an hour drama saves the climactic cliffhanger for the end of the episode; **a multi-camera sitcom usually puts its cliffhanger at the midpoint** (the ending has to hit the reset button back to normal). You may delay the full revelation, **but reveal it before the season ends** — dragging it past season one "is perhaps testing your patient audience's good will."

### 5.3 The Eight Act-Out Types Measured on West Wing, the Wordless Act Out of Two Cathedrals, and Downton's Four Types

Eight types measured across six West Wing episodes: **the suspense question / the reversal line / the emotional reveal / wordless action / a held prop / a repeated line inverted in meaning / a severed setup / a planted gesture paid off**; "only the Pilot and A Proportional Response use a conventional hook, and the later four episodes lean further and further toward emotional act outs." Demonstration: at the end of Act One of the pilot, Josh looks at a photograph and says "That's a good picture of her." — **an act out can be a soft private line, as long as it opens a new line.** The accompanying rules: **give each act out to a different line**; **at least one act must go out on wordless action or a prop, not all four on dialogue** — and a wordless act out must have had its grammar taught (in Act Three of Two Cathedrals, Mrs. Landingham recites the components aloud — hands in pockets, look away, smile — so that when Bartlet performs the gesture at the end of Act Four and the screen cuts to black, the answer is never spoken). **Downton's four types** (Fellowes's footnotes): a request made across a line | a hidden fact stated in one sentence ("He is standing.") | a Dowager-style button ("Worse luck.") | a physical fact (a corpse, handcuffs, a breakdown into tears).

### 5.4 An Act Out Can Be Purely an Information Event: the Two Goldberg & Rabkin Appendix Episodes

**An act out can be purely an information event.** Comparing the two appendix episodes in Goldberg & Rabkin: Martial Law's act outs are physical (holding a live bomb, unable to move), while those of seaQuest 2032's "Depths of Deceit" are entirely about "who now holds what / who has lost what" (one act out stacks a dead man returning to life plus an ally arrested for treason plus the antagonist suspended). Three laws common to both: **① an act ends not on the biggest danger but on "the protagonist pushed into a position with no way back"; ② the Act Two break must change the nature of the story, not merely raise the danger; ③ the Act Three ending must turn the protagonist's victory into a new defeat.**

### 5.5 Acceleration: Rabkin's 24 / Scandal Comparison and the L+3 Logic

Rabkin (2017): **24 is the art of the act break**, a big reversal before every commercial — but the new scene only overturns what was just said, "the illusion of movement when there really was none," a gimmick; **Scandal delivers "major changes not only in plot, but in character and story every seven or eight minutes."** His direct instruction for a spec pilot: **"That really cool thing that you have happening at the end of act four – move it to the end of the teaser. And then keep that pace up for the next sixty pages."** The industry logic is L+3 ratings — the only answer is to put "the scene everyone talks about the next day" in every episode (Ned Stark beheaded, Will Gardner shot in the courtroom, Sophia walking out of the barn), "deliberately planted … a requirement of contemporary television"; "A plot twist that comes after your reader has stopped reading is the same as no plot twist at all." **Streaming lets you drop three things**: the "previously on"; in-story implicit recaps (one of Netflix's notes on Jessica Jones was that she spent too long restating earlier episodes); and the post-commercial scene reset.

## 6. The Four Information Tools, Four Red Lines, and the Rhythm Formula of Suspense

| Tool | Definition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Dramatic irony** | The audience knows, at least one character does not (that character is the victim) | → suspense; or make the **antagonist the victim** (The Night Manager). In Ted Lasso season 1 Rebecca hires Ted to destroy the club — season 2 ends that irony, which "takes away a significant part of the story engine." The matching **ironic question**: "How and when will the victim find out, and how will they react?" |
| **Surprise** | Suddenly giving the audience information they did not expect | → raise the stakes; in comedy "almost every gag is a surprise" |
| **Mystery** | Giving the audience exactly enough to know that they do not know | Slow burn (Broadchurch, Mare of Easttown). The risk: introducing a mystery starts an intellectual process, and sustaining it too long turns the audience off. Recommended: **one big mystery a season, resolved in the finale and replaced with a new one** (Lost is the successful exception) |
| **Suspense** | The audience is aware of the danger | **Does not necessarily require dramatic irony** — "As long as the audience knows where the threat comes from, there is suspense, even if all the characters are aware of the danger." (Joyce's line in Stranger Things: "There is no mystery about the reality of the danger.") |

The four red lines:

1. **Do not let the protagonist know more than the audience for long stretches**, especially about backstory needed to understand their actions and feelings. The counter-example is Sharp Objects — "we struggle to identify with her emotionally because we don't know what caused the trauma." Fix A: let the audience catch up fast (Happy Valley season 1 does it inside episode one). Fix B: let the protagonist not know either — amnesia, a coma (Rick is shot at minute 10 of The Walking Dead and wakes up catching up alongside the audience, **avoiding a long exposition block**), a repressed memory. Keeping a mystery around a main character or the antagonist is fine (Eleven in Stranger Things).
2. **Prefer an "information bomb" for a cliffhanger; do not just cut a fight in half.** The end-of-episode cliffhanger in the Transparent pilot is achieved entirely by information management — "purely through managing information."
3. **Closed mystery vs. open mystery**: closed = neither protagonist nor audience knows (True Detective, Broadchurch); **an open mystery is really dramatic irony** (Columbo at the episode level, Happy Valley season 1 at the season level) — the audience knows whodunit, and the mystery is when and how the protagonist will get there.
4. **"You can have some mystery over the nature of the antagonist, but not over the reality of the danger it represents."**

### 6.1 The Rhythm Formula of Suspense (Douglas's six-beat NYPD Blue demonstration)

- **Douglas's rhythm formula** (p0139; in English, Anticipation — Expectation — Surprise): **anticipation → expectation → surprise** — "the audience is first led to anticipate something… then the story turns suddenly and produces a sense of surprise. **And then you begin a new suspense passage.**" (She demonstrates beat by beat on the six-beat cold open of NYPD Blue's "Hearts and Souls"; her method is to write a mini-outline for an important opening first.)

## 7. Defining A/B/C, Worked Examples of Weaving, and Douglas's Grid

### 7.1 Four Definitions of A/B/C/Runner

- **Douglas (p0089–0090)**: the three lines are "not subplots but three independent stories," each with different supporting characters, the same territory and the same protagonist. **A = the most central or most resonant story (not necessarily the one with the most pages); B is next; C is sometimes the comic relief in a serious drama, or a runner.** Extremes: ER routinely exceeds three; Monk and The X-Files have **only an A line.**
- **Calvisi**: **the letters are ranked by screen time**, A most and D least; "**These letter labels should never appear in your pilot script; they are for your use, only.**" Each line is a **story engine** and must contain **a character, a goal, and the conflict standing in the way**; ideally each line has at least three beats. True Detective has only A and B; Scandal and GoT have four each.
- **Landau**: **the A story = the franchise** (the show's signature and sweet spot), with the most scenes, and the protagonist's active goal almost always sits on it; the **B story** is the personal story, the internalized emotional stakes, which "serves to make the main character relatable and vulnerable"; **runners = C/D** (semi-trivial conflicts, a supporting character's personal story, or a running gag).
- **Oberg**: at least A+B an episode, at most 4–5 lines; **only A and B need to be designed in three acts, the others can be runners of 3–5 beats**; a season-level B or C line can become a given episode's A line (GoT focuses on the Starks one episode and the Targaryens the next).

### 7.2 Worked Examples of the Hard Rules (Terriers / Game of Thrones / Mad Men)

8. **Lines cross most effectively when there is a thematic link** (Landau 1e); family is the strongest link (the Sopranos' crime family vs. actual family). **A runner can grow into a main line** (Terriers' three-step promotion: things go missing → someone breaks in at night → the schizophrenic sister living in the attic → she helps him crack the big mystery); **a serial need not reach every character every episode** — "Game of Thrones rotated its emphasis from episode to episode."
9. **A model runner**: Peggy's rat in Mad Men S6E11 — the trap, the bloodstain, asking Stan to come over in the middle of the night and being refused; by the end the problem is solved, **but we never see her buy a cat, only the cat sitting on the sofa beside a much calmer Peggy at the end.** Landau calls this **elliptical storytelling** ("telling the story 'in the cut'").

### 7.3 The Procedural A/B Twin Track (Ann Donahue, CSI: Miami, Douglas chapter 3)

**The A line is the investigation, the B line is the emotional line**; **you must cut back and forth between A and B** — "If Act One is nothing but a clue takes you to this character… you're failing."; **"you have to have a warm body by the end of Act One, within the first 17 pages"**; **not everyone questioned has to be guilty, but each must hand over something else so the story builds, builds, builds**; "no one cares about plot… But story — story is not the same thing." Three room orders from the same book: **The Good Wife settles where the character arcs sit and then fits cases to them; CSI starts with the case; House plans A (the medical puzzle) / B (House's own struggle) / C (a relationship issue among the doctors, which must interlock with the case).**

### 7.4 The Extreme Case of Multi-Line Ensemble Writing (Downton, Fellowes)

- **Downton's extreme case (Fellowes)**: season 2 runs 8–12 lines simultaneously, with the ensemble specification that "**one scene advances six or seven lines, and each often gets a single line of dialogue**"; every episode carries at least one line "wholly unconnected to this episode's main crisis" to let the audience breathe (the Ethel line against the war in episodes 3–5, the Bryant line against the influenza in episode 8). **Never open a separate scene for a line that could have been folded into an ensemble scene.**

### 7.5 The Basic Four-Act Grid and the Three Steps for Taking an Episode Apart

**The basic four-act grid**: horizontal axis = T (teaser) | Act One | Act Two | Act Three | Act Four; vertical axis = scene slots 1–7 per act; one letter per cell. **The three steps**: ① watch it through and name the lines; ② **attach every story to one regular and sum up an arc in a single sentence** (= each line's log line); ③ fill in the letters cell by cell — "once the grid is filled it looks like a chessboard." The attitude and the diagnosis: "there are **no rules** for how to make that chessboard look good"; a line that ends in Act Three, a C story that does not begin until Act Two, an Act Four that lays out A's ending all at once — "**all of it is OK. And structure serves story. Learn from other shows, but do not imitate them.**"

### 7.6 Reverse-Engineering

**Reverse-engineering**: fill only three key points into the grid first — **the opening, the "worst case" suspense three-quarters in (end of Act Three in a three-act show, Act Four or Five in a longer one), and the ending**; do only the A line, or A+B, "leaving room for a C story you conceive later." Its purpose is to **stop you from reverting to earlier beats in a direction opposite to the suspense.**

### 7.7 The Requirements of a Log Line

**Log-line requirements** ("Hearts and Souls," p0138; all three lines take a regular rather than a guest as their subject, e.g. the B line: "When Sipowicz is challenged by Katie's drinking and despair, he must contain his own anger, guilt and drinking history in order to help her"): **"Write genuinely dramatic log lines… and you guarantee that the stories you write are full of conflict — that they are real stories and not merely situations."**

### 7.8 Oberg's Three-Step Alternative

**Oberg's three-step alternative**: **Mapping the Strands** (the ratios) → **Sequencing the Strands** (find each line's main dramatic action → its inciting incident and climax → its three acts → cut Act 2 into dramatic sequences by **subgoal** → cut each sequence into scenes by sub-subgoal) → **Weaving the Strands** (notation: X = inciting incident, BA2/BA3 = the start of each line's second/third act, CH = cliffhanger, MAC = mid-act climax). Two laws: **start the A line first so the audience knows the main stake**; **the more storylines, the fewer sequences each gets** — **an hour holds about 5 dramatic sequences** (a plot-led procedural's main line can have 4–6; a theme-led serial with five equally weighted lines gives each only one an episode).

## 8. Scene-Level Specifications and Measurements: Escalation Counts, Five Additional Series Specs, and the Structural Work of Stage Directions

- **Quantity**: at least three escalations, "but it's better to have more. More escalations give the scene time to build and breathe." **Prefer action** — "If all of your escalations are oral… **Your TV show turns into a radio show.**" Measured: the FBI men's-room scene in the Hannibal pilot (1 min 57 sec) contains 14–15 escalations; the woods scene in the How to Get Away with Murder teaser (1 min 40 sec) contains 11.

**Five additional specifications at the series scene level**: ① **target length 2 pages** — "a one-minute beat is more welcome than a five-minute scene" (Douglas). ② **You do not have to describe appearance** (Douglas p0117): "**what you most need to write is the emotional activity of a person at this delicate moment**"; **dialogue often starts on page 1.** ③ **"ANGLE" is a literary device, not an instruction to the director**: with the same time and place and no new log line, use it to mark a new dramatic beat. ④ **Layering several strands inside one scene** — Douglas uses four layers of simultaneity in "Hearts and Souls" to "create depth on the screen"; but the order she gives beginners is the reverse: separate the lines first, weave only when writing the teleplay. ⑤ **Do not micromanage actors** (Miller): the real cost is that "**You're actually training readers to skip your description.**"

**Stage directions carrying structural work** (the Succession scripts): **write subtext in the stage directions, not into the dialogue** ("Kendall sort of wants his dad to tell him what to do, but doesn't want to ask for direction."); **let a character lie successfully and put the stake in the stage direction** ("If she looks away, he'll know. But she doesn't.").

## 9. Season Shape: Templates and Measurements Show by Show

### 9.1 Tentpoles (Landau 1e Tip 13)

**7.2 Tentpoles.** Landau 1e Tip 13 groups teaser / act break / tag together as **tentpoles**. The function chain: **the teaser plants the hook → Act One establishes the problem the main characters will face → the middle acts complicate the problem, raise the stakes and make the solution look impossible → the last act climaxes and resolves → the tag is an epilogue that "puts a button on the episode,"** paying off a runner, or serving as a cliffhanger for the franchise or the B line.

### 9.2 The Logistical Layer and the Dramatic Layer (Stranger Things season 1, and the episode-count check)

**7.1 The logistical layer and the dramatic layer.** Oberg on Stranger Things season 1: the Duffer brothers' pitch bible uses logistical three acts at **3-3-2** (E1–3 / E4–6 / E7–8); the dramatic three acts are Act 1 at about 10 minutes, **Act 2 at 371 minutes total** (Joyce starts looking for her son at minute 11 of E1 → Will is rescued at minute 38 of E8), and Act 3 at only 13 minutes. His conclusion is measured: "Both conscious and unconscious approaches can work in harmony." **Season-level rhythm discipline**: in a serial, **each line usually gets at most one dramatic sequence per episode** (the Joyce line gets 10 across eight episodes), each sequence closing on a success or a failure. **The episode-count check**: "**Are you starting the story too early?**" — if it only gets good at episode 3, compress the first three into one; **dropping one episode (10→8, 8→6) usually speeds a season up.** Landau 2e agrees: demote the pilot to backstory and "**begin your series pilot in medias res**."

### 9.3 The Bottle Episode: West Wing's "17 People" Measured

**7.3 The bottle episode: budget is the cause.** In his preface to West Wing's "17 People," Sorkin records that Warner required the next episode to have "no guest cast, no locations, no new sets, no extras and no film," so "**I got to write a play**." Measured at 58 PDF pages, with act lengths of **6 / 14 / 10 / 10 / 18** (the reverse of the preceding four episodes, with Act Four the longest); the whole episode is one night (ACT ONE opens on the card `11:35 P.M.`); **one night plus one building = a bottle**; the stage directions repeatedly write "The place is empty except for the SECURITY GUARD" to legitimize having zero extras. Three rules: ① **a bottle episode may end acts on deepening rather than on a turn** (the three act outs here are three excavations of the same fact: the diagnosis is stated → the medical detail → the accusation out of control); ② **with no exteriors to shoot, the number of lines becomes the source of rhythm** (four lines normally; five in the bottle episode and seven in the finale — add lines rather than lengthen scenes); ③ **invert those four constraints into three choices**: one night, one building, and a main-line beat that says "time to tell the truth," **seizing the chance to advance the season-arc secret you have been avoiding** (Bartlet tells Toby about the MS).

### 9.4 A Bottle Episode Is Not a Single-Line Episode: the Sopranos' "Pine Barrens"

**A bottle episode is not a single-line episode.** The Sopranos' "Pine Barrens" (S3E11, 64 pages, 71 scenes) strands two men in the woods for a night while **the B/C/D lines advance as usual** (Gloria, Meadow–Jackie Jr., the couples' therapy), using cross-cutting as counterpoint (starving in the forest ↔ three meals at Tony's house). The episode's other device: **a chain of information decay** — three relays turn "16 Chechens / the interior ministry" into "16 Czechs / an interior decorator"; **the failed phone connection is what guarantees that everyone holds only half the intelligence.**

### 9.5 Container Episodes and Ritual Episodes: Succession's Two Templates

**7.4 Container and ritual episodes (Succession's two templates).** **The pressure-chamber episode** (lock everyone into one building: the hospital, the Thanksgiving apartment, the warehouse party, the castle in season 1) **always comes with a "communications restricted" premise** (the corridor, the phone-confiscation pouches, the phone check-in desk plus a signal blocker). The pattern: **restrict the flow of information first, then make information travel by body** — "go back and get the phone to make a call" becomes a playable action. **The ritual episode** (a day with a schedule: the charity ball with 4 p.m.–9:30 clock cards; the order of arrivals at Thanksgiving doubling as the order of introductions; the wedding's rehearsal / speeches / first dance / fireworks) — **a ritual supplies a free beat sheet, and the writer need only stuff one conflict into each fixed stage**; the S3E9 notes explain why it works: a wedding forces everyone into the same space, forces courtesy, and forces stretches of silence, so the conspiracy can only advance in corridor asides, bathrooms and car parks — **the stronger the ritual's order, the greater the transgression of the hidden line.** **The absent protagonist**: in S1E6's no-confidence vote Kendall is stuck in traffic and speaks over a Polycom; in S3E9 Logan is barely in the same room as his children across nine scenes — **absence is the strongest device for sustaining suspense**; remove the instigator of the climax from the room and the suspense shifts from "how will it turn out" to "can he get there / will he be found out."

### 9.6 The Two-Parter: West Wing's "In the Shadow of Two Gunmen" I/II Measured

**7.5 The two-parter.** Measured on West Wing's "In the Shadow of Two Gunmen" I/II: the first half is a 7-page teaser + 21/14/11/14 = 67 pages; the second half has **a teaser of only 2 pages** + 11/14/16/18 = 61 pages — **the second half of a season-opening two-parter needs only one arrest scene as its teaser.** The division of labour: **the first half alternates between two timelines to create "two worlds," the second half puts every act out in the present tense; one prop stitches across both episodes** (the ID badge, the necklace).

### 9.7 Season and Series Finales: Downton / Succession / the Sopranos / Sorkin

- **Downton (Fellowes)**: "You've always got to give the audience a reason for coming back next time."; the last act is pure pay-off plus **"streamers"** — "we won't resolve this tonight, but if you keep watching, at some point we will."; **the last act only pays off, introducing no new information**, and before the penultimate act ends the audience must know exactly which debts remain.
- **Succession season 1**: **the finale turns around symmetrically with the pilot** — in E1 Kendall buys Vaulter at $140 a share and loses the throne; in E10 he moves on the company at the same $140 a share and loses everything, "completing the shape of a season with the same number." **The finale both closes and opens** — all ten lines resolve, but every resolution creates debt for the next season (Greg's photocopies, Tom's cruise ships, Roman's manslaughter, Shiv's double loyalty). **Season 4 inverts the method**: one central question an episode, answered by the end of that episode, with suspense carried instead by the new configuration of power.
- **The Sopranos' anti-climactic close**: use a public announcement, one line, or one action rather than a cliffhanger ("You got mayonnaise on your chin."); **a season-arc antagonist may be resolved incidentally by a third party** (Richie shot dead by Janice at the dinner table), but the setup has to be planted in the relationship detail well before that episode (the gun-to-the-head kink).
- **Sorkin's finale method (Two Cathedrals)**: his preface is little more than **a list of images** ("The cigarette. The storm. The motorcade. The press conference. Mark Knoffler.") — **settle seven images for the finale first, then let structure serve them**, and run one object through three acts (the cigarette: appears in Act One → stubbed out in Act Three → picked up by a cleaner in Act Four). Measured act lengths: **3 / 13 / 13 / 12 / 17**, with **a teaser of only 3 pages marked `CONTINUOUS`**: a finale's teaser does not re-establish the world, its only job is to set that night's deadline.

### 9.8 The Six-Episode Season Shape (the Fleabag Template)

**7.7 The six-episode season shape (the Fleabag template).** Episode 1 lays out every character and the season's suspense at once (**this is not a premise pilot**; season 2 does it in a single engagement dinner in one location) → 2 converts episode 1's emotion into a followable want → 3 **plays the first card face up** → 4 **the bottle episode**: a two-hander deep dive away from the regular locations (the silent retreat in season 1; the confessional in season 2) → 5 **the false victory at its peak** ("the best day") building potential energy for the collapse → 6 **the finale = a closed social occasion** (Sexhibition / the wedding), recalling everyone into one space where every line closes. The episode shape is fixed: **cold open (1–3 minutes) → TITLES → 3–7 movements → button (≤1 page, usually wordless)**; **the button of a finale is never plot** — the last image of a half-hour resolves the theme, not the plot. Two transferable devices: **one object runs through the whole season, changing hands once an episode, and revealing the truth when it is returned in the finale** (the statue: stolen → sold → given as a birthday present → confessed → returned → used as a trophy → a wedding present → "She was based on your mother." → she takes it away again); **put the bottle episode at episode 4 and use a single prohibition as its engine.**

### 9.9 The Multi-Line Season Shape (Downton Season 2)

**7.8 The multi-line season shape (Downton season 2).** The act count differs episode to episode: six acts in Ep1, four in Ep2–5, five in Ep6, five in Ep7, six in Ep8 and seven in the Christmas special. Line lengths are tiered: **a cross-season main line, a single-season arc, and guest lines running one to three episodes.** Two rules: **the retention mechanism** — a popular regular never leaves, and characters already pushed to the door are pulled back by "bankruptcy" (Thomas), "a change of mind" (Carson) or "a reduced sentence" (Bates), so that **the ones you can actually kill are the outsiders** (Lang, William, Lavinia, Vera, Bryant); and **before you kill a character, make the audience guess a different one** — "It's like a whodunit. The killer has to be someone who was there all the time."

## 10. The Alternative-Structure List and Multiple Timelines

**7.9 Alternative structures (Landau 2e).** **Linear** and **flashback** (when the "story present" appears only at the head and tail it is a **bookend structure**, as in Chernobyl opening on Legasov's tapes and suicide); then zigzag, hopscotch, three timelines (the OITNB pilot), **Rashomon structure** (The Affair), **time loop** (the earliest is The Twilight Zone episode 62, "Shadow Play"; then Russian Doll), and **told backwards** (The Assassination of Gianni Versace rewinding from the murder through the earlier victims — "**The trick here is that the writer didn't tell the origin story until the series finale.**"). **Five questions for choosing** (the premise being "Time is the container for your series"; when unsure, use classic three acts): ① how much time does the story cover? ② is there information far in the future that the audience must know for the story to work? ③ would scrambling the order raise tension by changing audience expectation? ④ does the structure best fit the story's **underlying experience** (the narrative gaps of I May Destroy You matching an experience of drugs, blackouts and large lost stretches of memory)? ⑤ do you genuinely want to try the new way? **The warning**: "if you deviate too much from the linear basics, you might lose your audience."

**Multiple timelines as a tonal regulator (Alex Pina, Money Heist)**: when the adrenaline peaks and the audience's anxiety is unbearable, cut to a flashback — "above all, we go to another 'flavor' or genre"; the reason is that "**If you do not work in different time planes, the emotional continuity makes it impossible for you to change the anguished or sad or dramatic tone of some of the actions.**" **The dissent (Mazzara)**: "I don't believe that flashbacks are an element of horror films. They're an element of science fiction films… I don't find flashbacks scary."

### 12.4 Episode and Season Containers

| English term | Usage in this pack | Official Chinese rendering (Yan Min) | One-sentence definition |
|---|---|---|---|
| bottle episode | bottle episode | not recorded | An episode confined to existing sets because of budget; the cause is stated in Sorkin's preface to West Wing's "17 People" (Warner required "no guest cast, no locations, no new sets, no extras and no film"), measured at 58 pages, act lengths 6/14/10/10/18, all in one night. |

| two-parter | two-parter | not recorded | A unit telling one story across two episodes; measured (West Wing's "In the Shadow of Two Gunmen" I/II): the first half is a 7-page teaser + 21/14/11/14 = 67 pages, the second half **a teaser of only 2 pages** + 11/14/16/18 = 61 pages. |

### 12.6 Three Translation Traps That Must Be Flagged

1. **log line is rendered "计程绳" (中译 p0282).** The translator took the literal nautical sense of a log line (the knotted rope); current usage is "一句话故事 / 故事梗概句." The Chinese definition itself is correct: "one sentence, occasionally two or three, that can sum up a story; it conveys the series' premise and dramatic arc or plot quickly"; Douglas EN adds the etymology — **the FCC required stations to keep a one-line log for each programme.** This pack always writes **log line**; when reading chapter 4 of the Chinese edition and the "Hearts and Souls" demonstration (中译 p0138), restore "计程绳" to log line.
2. **staff writer is rendered "试用编剧" (中译 p0285, also "宝贝编剧," baby writer).** The same page renders freelance writer as "自由编剧"; current usage renders staff writer as "编剧组成员 / 初级编剧." The word "试用" (probationary) invites the misreading that the job is unpaid or informal — it is in fact the first rank **on staff** (the rank ladder is in `sw-writers-room`).
3. **"晚进早出" is a same-name opposite and must not be equated with "enter late, leave early."** In appendix 2 of Yao Kougen's *An Introduction to Television Drama Writing* (**p0367–0368**), "晚进早出" means: **enter late = lay in atmosphere first, let the audience get familiar with the new location and accumulate expectation, and only then light the fuse**; leave early = cut away the moment the turn is complete and a new concrete goal and new suspense exist ("this entering and leaving keeps the scene under constant tension"). The American **enter late, leave early** of the McKee lineage means the exact opposite by "enter late" — **skip the lay-in and go straight into the conflict.** The two books agree on "leave early" and contradict each other on "enter late"; a Chinese-language structure document must state which system it is using, or scene lengths will differ by a factor of two. (Yao's exit test — leave as soon as new suspense exists — can be stacked with Oberg's cliffhanger "before we leave a strand.")

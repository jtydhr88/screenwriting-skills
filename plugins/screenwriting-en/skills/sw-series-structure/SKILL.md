## 1. How Series Structure Fundamentally Differs From Film Structure

- **A tighter form, so harder rather than easier.** Douglas, chapter 3: "A television script demands that all the same elements be compressed into a tighter form." A film scene can run 5–7 pages; **television targets 2 pages a scene** — "if you open with a four-page meeting scene, that is a red flag."
- **A teleplay simulates the cut film, not the shooting material**; the reader should get the experience of *watching* the show (Calvisi).
- **The writer labels the acts personally, with nowhere to hide** (Calvisi lists this as the first formatting difference of a teleplay): "There is no place to hide." A pilot with no act breaks looks like cheating, in the same family as shrinking the margins — an instant amateur tell.
- **A numbered scene is not a dramatic scene.** Douglas: an exterior of a building "is not a scene in the full dramatic sense"; **one dramatic beat can contain several slug lines, because a beat tracks story development, not location.** Oberg's counting rule: **protagonist or goal changes → new dramatic scene; location or time changes → new logistical scene**; structure documents count dramatic scenes.
- **Cut length and script length are two different numbers** (Calvisi): a broadcast hour minus advertising is only 45–48 minutes of show, yet the pilot script must run at least 50 pages — long rather than short, "but try not to exceed 60 pages."
- **A series "act" is not a dramatic principle, it is an ad slot.** Oberg's chapter 1 calls any division that fixes the number of parts and their page lengths a **story format (logistical)**, descended from changing candles in the theatre, changing reels in cinema, and inserting commercials on television — "But all this is gone." His sausage-string image: the logistical method decides only "how many sausages, and how long." **The single most important judgement in this skill: act count is a delivery format; three acts (before / during / after one main dramatic action or evolution) is a design tool.**
- **But structure is also the production schedule.** Miller, chapter 9: the first thing the production department does with a script is count days in and out; **write only DAY or NIGHT** — write "dawn" and production will schedule that day's shoot precisely around sunrise.
- **Structure is inherited.** Episodes reuse the pilot's act structure and page ranges (the act-page comparison of the Breaking Bad pilot against the same show's #308 is in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 1) — **writing the pilot means casting the structural mould for the whole series.**

## 2. The Episode Container: Teaser, Act Count, Page Anchors

### 2.1 The Function Chain of the Broadcast Four Acts (Goldberg & Rabkin, 2003)

**Act One** introduces character, conflict and stakes → **the Act Two out must push the story in a "startlingly new and unexpected direction"** (Law & Order: the police arrest someone nobody expected) → **the Act Three out has the protagonist believe they have it under control and then discover they are wrong**, "They are all going to die. There is no hope." → **Act Four** finds the way and reaches the goal.

> **Currency flag (2003)**: the book states "one-hour drama is always four acts," which is **out of date** — from 2006 the broadcast networks generally went to five, ABC mandated six on new shows (Douglas, p0091), and streaming dropped act labels altogether. But **the four-act function chain still holds**: Miller and Landau (2021) both still require you to break in four acts first and cut later.

The four page anchors (Douglas p0090–0091, the four-act draft with a teaser) and the origin of "a commercial every 13 to 15 minutes" are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 2.

> ⚠️ **These page numbers hold only inside a 48–63 page American hour, and cannot be scaled proportionally to a Chinese 45-minute episode.** The two systems cut on different grounds: the American one cuts acts by **page count and ad slots**, the Chinese one by **time block and segment** (about 41.5 minutes of show, a 3-minute opening + a small hook every 15 minutes + a big hook at the end). When writing a Chinese series, take only the medium-independent parts of this section (the act-out taxonomy, "the Act One out leaves the protagonist with no way back," the Act Two break changing the nature of the story, the five-part scene structure, the stakes test, the four information tools); use `sw-chinese-series-practice` for time blocks and scene counts.
> **Half-hours are equally exempt** (a 30-minute script runs about 30 pages, and both act and scene counts are a different system): for act lengths, page numbers and scene counts see `sw-sitcom-comedy` Section 4, and **do not scale the one-hour numbers here down to fit**. Diagnostics 4 and 5 below apply only to the American hour.

### 2.2 Scene Count: 5–7 Scenes an Act, About 28 an Episode

Douglas's arithmetic, the totals (28 scenes across four acts, 20 across five or six), Oberg's A/B/C scene ratios and Chase's "about 35 beats an episode" are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 2.

### 2.3 Converting Four Acts Into Five or Six

Three conversion methods (Douglas, Peter Blake of House, Miller) and the six-act tail design in which "Act Six is really the next episode's teaser" are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 2.

### 2.4 Teaser / Cold Open

- **Length**: the teaser lengths given by Douglas, Landau and Oberg are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 3.
- **Ten types** (Douglas's seven paradigms plus Landau's three) with names and examples are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 3.
- **The hard rule for beginners** (Douglas p0098): "Open with story, not with information… Many students… imagine that opening on a large thematic idea or an abstract philosophy will impress. **What they are actually doing is flinching from the challenge of a moving dramatic arc. The best teasers tend to be the most dramatized.**"
- **The teaser-flashback taboo** (Oberg): you may begin partway through, or even near the end of, a dramatic action (The Walking Dead, Breaking Bad), **but you must never answer the dramatic question inside the teaser**; if you take the material from the ending, take it from **before or during** the climax (Run All Night), not after (John Wick).
- **A teaser is not compulsory**: Landau 2e's and Oberg's arguments, plus the list of shows with no teaser and shows that use "a short Act One as the teaser," are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 3.
- **A cold open ≠ a teaser** (Miller): the Brooklyn Nine-Nine pilot's cold open does not touch the main line, it only launches the episode's theme, which is why that episode's point of attack falls inside Act One.

### 2.5 Invisible Acts: A Three-Part Test

Pay-cable and streaming scripts often carry neither act labels nor page breaks (Calvisi names **True Detective, House of Cards and Mad Men as unlabelled, though Mad Men puts FADE OUT where the conventional breaks would be**), and yet "a pilot script is almost always going to be structured in the conventional 4-6 act template." A three-part test, measured act by act from the Succession scripts:

① **Story-day or time-block markers** — **a new day is a new act**; ② **a change of location or of vehicle**; ③ **the moment the central question turns**. The decision rule: **every act must end on a transfer of information or of power**, not necessarily on a suspense hook. The Succession measurements behind each test (S3E9, S4E01, S4E05, S4E08, S4E10) are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 4.

The accompanying pattern (Succession season 4, ten episodes): **8 of 10 episodes compress the whole episode into a single container with a fixed procedure**, and **the procedure's checkpoints replace act breaks** — **that is, streaming writers are still writing acts, they have just disguised the acts as a schedule.** Per-episode containers and scene counts are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 4.

### 2.6 Whether to Print ACT on the Page — a Disagreement That Must Be Kept Side by Side

- **Five positions** (Landau 2e's "story over format," the showrunners who want to see the labels, Miller's market judgement, Mazzara's act conversion on TWD, Alex Pina's milestone moments) are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 2.
- **The test**: writing for a broadcast network / basic cable / AVOD → label the acts (AVOD, like broadcast, does not let viewers skip the ads); writing for premium / SVOD → break in four acts and delete the labels before delivery, or switch to story-day markers. If unsure, use Miller's test: which platform could this content actually air on?

### 2.7 Page Count and Episode Length

Calvisi's, Landau's, Miller's and Blum's pilot and episode page ranges, Oberg's per-platform episode lengths, Landau 2e's read on the trend, and the currency flags per publication year are all in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 2.

## 3. Act Out and Cliffhanger Taxonomies; the Four Information Tools

### 3.1 Definitions, Counter-Examples and Tests

- **Calvisi**: every act (ideally every scene) must make the reader want to know what happens next; the act ending is commonly called the **"act out."**
- **Landau 2e, chapter 12**: "A cliffhanger is a plot device that leaves one or more characters facing either a dangerous situation or a shocking revelation."
- **Oberg**: a cliffhanger is "simply an unresolved conflict" — **and its position is not only end of episode or end of season, but any point at which you leave a strand.**
- **The counter-example, schmuck bait** (Landau 2e): the heart monitor flatlines, the nurse shouts "Code blue!" (commercial) → "I'm getting a pulse."
- **Today's rule (the single most important line in Landau 2e)**: "**forcing a cliffhanger at the end of each act break is deadly**… Instead, act breaks can land on a **turning point that emerges from characters, not merely from plot**."
- **The test** (repeated in 1e Tip 19 and 2e chapter 12): a cliffhanger must **"grow out of character jeopardy, risk, or fear"**; a bad cliffhanger is a plot point reaching for shock value; "even a 'surprise' cliffhanger needs to be (subtly) set up so that the audience feels they **should have seen it coming—but didn't**."
- **Position and time limits**: an hour drama saves the climactic cliffhanger for the end of the episode; a multi-camera sitcom usually puts it at the midpoint (the ending has to hit the reset button back to normal); you may delay the full revelation, **but reveal it before the season ends** (the original wording is in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 5).

### 3.2 Ten Kinds of Cliffhanger (Landau)

The seven types in 2e (character in peril | ticking bomb | love is in the balance | surprise twist / aha moment | death of a character | discovery of a secret | life events) and the three further types listed in 1e (lack of closure | mini-cliffhangers | comedy cliffhangers), each defined with examples, plus the two most transferable techniques ("complete the reversal in a single word," "never go out on a laugh"), are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 5.

### 3.3 Eight Forms of Act Out and Three Universal Laws

Eight types measured across six West Wing episodes: **the suspense question / the reversal line / the emotional reveal / wordless action / a held prop / a repeated line inverted in meaning / a severed setup / a planted gesture paid off.** The accompanying rules: **give each act out to a different line**; **at least one act must go out on wordless action or a prop rather than all four on dialogue** — and a wordless act out must have had its grammar taught earlier. Examples of all eight types, the way Two Cathedrals teaches its own grammar, and Downton's four types are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 5.

**An act out can be purely an information event.** The two appendix episodes in Goldberg & Rabkin (Martial Law and seaQuest 2032's "Depths of Deceit") yield three universal laws: **① an act ends not on the biggest danger but on "the protagonist pushed into a position with no way back"; ② the Act Two break must change the nature of the story, not merely raise the danger; ③ the Act Three ending must turn the protagonist's victory into a new defeat.** Act-by-act comparison of both episodes is in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 5.

### 3.4 The Components of Suspense, and the Stakes Test

- **Douglas's rhythm formula** (p0139; in English, Anticipation — Expectation — Surprise): **anticipation → expectation → surprise**, "and then you begin a new suspense passage"; the beat-by-beat demonstration on the six-beat cold open of NYPD Blue's "Hearts and Souls" is in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 6.
- **Landau 2e**: Suspense = **Anticipation + Surprise**, best when unexpected, inevitable but not predictable.
- **The stakes test (the most usable single item here)**: ask whether "do nothing about the crisis" is a viable option for this character — "If your character is not compelled to solve a problem by the potential of losing something of value, then your stakes are insufficient."
- **The polarity model**: mark the positive or negative charge (+/−) of every episode, every scene, every act. What differs from the old practice is that **an act break can go either way** — "some acts will end on a win."

### 3.5 The Four Information Tools (Oberg, chapters 1–2)

A three-dimensional model: character is a point; add the main dramatic action (conscious want) → a line; add the main dramatic evolution (unconscious need) → a plane; **add managing information → a solid.** Managing conflict answers "Who wants or needs what and why?"; managing information answers "**Who knows what and when?**"

The four tools (**dramatic irony** / **surprise** / **mystery** / **suspense**), defined and tabulated for use, are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 6.

Four red lines (① do not let the protagonist know more than the audience for long stretches; ② prefer an "information bomb" for a cliffhanger; ③ closed mystery vs. open mystery; ④ "you can have some mystery over the nature of the antagonist, but not over the reality of the danger it represents") are unpacked in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 6.

Landau 2e's stitching line: "it's not only Who Does What When, but also **Who Knows What When**."

### 3.6 Acceleration: an Act Out Is Not a Fake Reversal Every Seven or Eight Minutes

Rabkin's comparison of 24 against Scandal, the original wording of "move the coolest thing at the end of Act Four up to the end of the teaser," the L+3 ratings logic, and "streaming lets you drop three things" are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 5.

## 4. Weaving and Proportioning A / B / C / Runner

### 4.1 Definitions and Ordering

Four sets of definitions and orderings (Douglas's "three independent stories" plus the runner / Calvisi's "letters ranked by screen time" plus the three elements of a story engine / Landau's A = franchise, B = the personal story, runners = C/D / Oberg's "at least A+B per episode, at most 4–5 strands, and only A and B need to be designed in three acts") are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 7.

### 4.2 The Hard Rules of Weaving

1. **The franchise (A line) runs through every act; the B line and runners come in as needed**, with generally **4–5 beats an act, ideally closing on the A line and on the protagonist's predicament** (Landau).
2. **Almost never end an act on the C line** — "If the story is worthy of an act break, then it's not your C story – it's probably more your A or B story." (Identical wording in Landau 1e and 2e; Oberg agrees.)
3. **But the act-end suspense need not be on the A line** (Douglas p0095: "it may be the turning point of the B story"). Measured on the West Wing pilot: both the teaser and the Act Three out are carried by the B line, Sam–Laurie.
4. **You do not have to alternate like a chessboard; follow the line of interest.** Douglas demonstrates on scene 8 of NYPD Blue's "Simone Says" (the old woman downstairs killed by a stray bullet): "on the chessboard of plot sequence it is not required, but for following the audience's line of interest it is essential."
5. **Cutting to another line creates ellipsis** — the impression that time has passed, letting the same characters reappear having "gone through something they could not actually have gone through that fast."
6. **No scene is written for exposition alone. "If you have to deliver facts, put the facts inside an emotional context."** (Douglas p0119)
7. **Plan the lines separately and only weave them when you write the teleplay** (Douglas's recommended order for beginners, which is the reverse of the order she herself uses when analysing the four-layer simultaneity of "Hearts and Souls").
8. **Lines cross most effectively when there is a thematic link** (Landau 1e), and family is the strongest link; **a runner can grow into a main line**; **a serial need not reach every character every episode**; examples of all three (the three-step promotion in Terriers, the rotation in Game of Thrones, Peggy's rat in Mad Men S6E11 and elliptical storytelling) are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 7.

### 4.3 The Procedural A/B Twin Track (Ann Donahue, CSI: Miami, Douglas chapter 3)

Ann Donahue's A = investigation / B = emotion twin track, "warm body by the end of Act One, within the first 17 pages," and three room orders (The Good Wife / CSI / House) are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 7.

### 4.4 Two Alternative Ways to Break Story (which must be kept alongside A/B/C)

- **Wendy West (Dexter)**: aired without acts, so the show effectively reverted to three; "**We break the beats by character and then do a weave.**" Every character on the whiteboard gets a beginning, middle and end; **you break by character, not by act**, starting with the lead's line — "The reversals tend to come where you'd expect — page 40 to 45 out of a 55-page script."
- **Glen Mazzara (The Walking Dead)**: **explicitly rejects A/B/C** — making Rick the A and someone else the B and interweaving them, "that doesn't always equal a theme. It just pushes the ball further on each one." He asks instead: **"What is this episode about?"** (the episode where the girl walks out of the barn, the episode where the herd breaks through the fence — each character has a place inside that larger story). **He states the cost himself: minor characters may stay minor forever.**
- **Oberg's middle position**: multiple lines do not automatically mean theme-led — if the characters on each line **share the same external problem and the same goal**, it is a plot-led co-protagonist structure (Stranger Things' A1 Joyce / A2 Hopper / A3 the boys / A4 Jonathan are really one A line).
- **Downton's extreme case (Fellowes)**: the ensemble specification of running 8–12 lines simultaneously in season 2, and "at least one line per episode unconnected to this episode's main crisis," are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 7.

### 4.5 Using the Grid to Analyse and to Plan (Douglas's grid)

The basic four-act grid, the three steps for taking an episode apart, reverse-engineering, the requirements of a log line, and Oberg's three-step alternative (Mapping → Sequencing → Weaving the Strands) are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 7.

## 6. The Five-Part Scene Structure and the Scene-Level Checklist

**The five parts** (Miller chapter 9, adapted from Lance Gentile, writer on ER and Third Watch and an emergency physician): **hero's want / obstacle / escalations / decision / resolution.**

- **The want and the obstacle are already fixed before the scene opens**; **the scene opens on the first escalation**; **the decision is the scene's climax**; **the resolution is the consequence of the decision**, and it carries the characters into the next scene.
- **Quantity**: at least three escalations, "but it's better to have more. More escalations give the scene time to build and breathe." **Prefer action** — "If all of your escalations are oral… **Your TV show turns into a radio show.**" (Escalation counts measured on one Hannibal scene and one HTGAWM scene are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 8.)
- **A scene has exactly one hero, and the hero can change from scene to scene** (the men's-room scene belongs to Will; the next Jack–Alana scene belongs to Jack). **In a one-person scene** the opposing want comes from the world or the environment — in OITNB's "The Chickening," Piper's want is to enjoy her Sunday-morning ritual and **the obstacle is prison**.
- **A moment or an image is not a scene**: "If there isn't a strong character want, it's not a scene. **A moment without a character want is simply a moment, an image.**" And a moment must not repeat an emotion already known; it has to show an emotion evolving.
- **The duty to end it**: "**You the writer need to decide how to end the scene.** And you the writer need to decide what emotions your characters are feeling."
- **Exposition through conflict**: in HTGAWM's woods scene, everyone's objection is reasonable, and the argument conveys both the difficulty and the stakes.

**Five additional specifications at the series scene level** (the 2-page length target, not describing appearance, "ANGLE" as a literary device, layering several strands inside one scene, not micromanaging actors) and **two ways stage directions carry structural work** (from the Succession scripts) are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 8.

## 8. Preserved Disagreements: What to Use When

| Point at issue | Position A | Position B | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Is an act structure or format** | Douglas / Calvisi / Goldberg & Rabkin: four/five/six acts are a structural skeleton with checkable page anchors | Oberg: acts are logistical, "just the size of the sausage"; design in dramatic three acts and cut at delivery | Delivering to a platform with ads, or proving structural competence → A; diagnosing "the middle sags" or "the structure feels formulaic" → B (cut sequences by subgoal). They stack: A as the working unit, B as the design tool |
| **Whether to print ACT on the page** | Landau: break story by act break, delete the labels in the final draft (story over format) | The counter-position in the same book: many showrunners want the labels as proof of structural command; Miller: if the content could air on a network, they are mandatory | Judge by target market (Miller's test): deletable for a streaming submission, mandatory for broadcast or basic cable |
| **Does the midpoint mean anything** | Calvisi: the midpoint is the Act Two out, must turn the A line and start a ticking clock | Oberg: "the midpoint can be safely ignored if you find it confusing"; the substitute is a **mid-act climax** | The A-line goal can span all of Act 2 → midpoint; the goal changes partway (heist films, the Occupied pilot) → mid-act climax |
| **Is the midpoint a false victory or a real success** | The Snyder school / Calvisi: choose between an illusion of victory and an illusion of defeat | Miller: it must be the hero succeeding—almost—"**It's not 'the hero fails'!**" | For a tragedy or an anti-hero episode use Miller's own variant: **the squeeze flips positive, the climax flips negative** |
| **Where the "worst case" sits** | Douglas: three-quarters in; Calvisi: DOW + All Is Lost at 4/5 | Oberg: "a series will be successful **despite** these formulaic crutches, not because of them." | First series script → use the anchors to self-check; solid sequence design already in place → no need to place them |
| **A/B/C lines vs. other ways of breaking story** | Douglas / Calvisi / Landau: A/B/C(/D) plus runners, letters ordered by screen time, act breaks on A or B | Mazzara: rejects A/B/C and asks "What is this episode about?"; Wendy West: break by character, not by act, then weave | Ensemble show where each episode should feel like one complete story → Mazzara; single-lead serial with reversals concentrated on the lead → West; procedurals and broadcast drama → A/B/C. Mazzara names the cost himself: minor characters may stay minor forever |
| **Must every act end on a cliffhanger** | Calvisi: every act (ideally every scene) should make the reader turn the page; Kelley (Revenge, six acts): "five or six cliffhangers an episode" | Landau 2e: "forcing a cliffhanger at the end of each act break is deadly"; measured on Sorkin: the Act One and Act Two outs are both light, and Act Four is a reset rather than a cliffhanger | Six-act broadcast drama (five ad slots to fill) → Kelley's quota; no ads or a strong authorial voice → Landau/Sorkin. **The test does not change**: it must grow out of character jeopardy, risk or fear |
| **Theme unifying multiple lines vs. theme as an emergent property** | Landau (Tip 14 / ch. 11): theme is the glue holding divergent story lines together (all four lines of ER's "Stuck on You" are about being stuck to each other) | Johannessen (Homeland/Dexter/24): the room slogan on 24 was "**We don't do themes.**"; "If you want something that looks and feels real, **throw out the writerly devices**"; the substitute is an **operational question**. Mazzara agrees: focus on story first, and only step back to see the theme | Hard realism or thriller priority → B (use "this episode's operational question" instead); ensemble with several lines that need stitching together → A. Note that Johannessen concedes the "Atonement" theme of Dexter season 5 was **emergent**; Landau likewise lists shows with no unifying theme (Breaking Bad; Parenthood and FNL "arranged asymmetrically") |
| **Whether to outline** | Landau 1e: "**No outline, no paycheck.**"; Calvisi: "the Story Map is the ultimate outline template."; Miller: grid, then outline, then script | Mazzara: "I don't use outlines… None of their notes are applicable to script." — his substitute is a two- or three-page story document plus a beat sheet of two or three words per beat ("Rick kills Shane."); Sorkin: "When I finish one script I have no idea what's going to happen in the next." | Writing an episode on someone else's staff, or collecting development payments → outline is mandatory (it is a contract milestone); being the showrunner and able to hold structure while drafting → Mazzara/Sorkin is viable. Mazzara draws his own boundary: "All script problems… come down to structure problems." See `sw-writers-room` |
| **Is a teaser required** | Calvisi / Douglas / Goldberg & Rabkin: the teaser is the first gate for holding an audience | Oberg: only if it serves a dramatic purpose, otherwise "just pointless"; Landau: many shows simply start with Act One | Ads, and a viewer holding a remote → a teaser is mandatory; strong serial or a binge platform → a short Act One with late main titles can replace it (Scandal, Modern Family) |

## 9. Diagnostic Checklist (run after finishing an episode)

1. Can you state this episode's **central question** in one sentence? (Mazzara: "What is this episode about?")
2. Does every line have a **log line hung on a series regular** (not a guest character)? Is it an actual story, or only a situation?
3. Does the A line run through every act? Do the act breaks all land on A or B? Does any act close on the C line — and if so, is that line actually your A or B?
4. Are there 4–5 beats and 5–7 scenes per act? Is the episode's scene count between 20 (five or six acts) and 28–30 (four acts)? **(Scene counts apply only to the American hour: half-hour comedy runs 15–20 scenes multi-camera or 40–50 single-camera, see `sw-sitcom-comedy` Section 4; a Chinese 45-minute episode runs 25–35 scenes, see `sw-chinese-series-practice`. The three figures differ by a factor of two or three — pick by "shooting method + length," do not scale.)**
5. **(This item applies only to the American hour; skip it for half-hours and Chinese series.)** Do the page anchors line up? (Four acts with a teaser: 17–18 / 30 / 45 / 60; Calvisi's five acts: teaser ≤10, Act Two out at 30, Act Three out at 40, Act Four out at 48; AOP at 37–40; the worst case at 4/5.)
6. Is any scene longer than 3 pages without being a complete beat? A four-page meeting scene is a red flag.
7. Is any scene there purely for exposition? Is any "moment with no character want" being used as a scene?
8. Does every scene have all five parts (want / obstacle / at least three escalations, action preferred / decision / resolution)? At the end, does the audience know where the hero stands emotionally?
9. Can you name the event at every act out? Does it grow out of the character's jeopardy, risk or fear? Is the "surprise" act out set up enough that the audience feels they should have seen it coming?
10. How many of the eight act-out forms did you use? **Does at least one act go out on wordless action or a held prop** — and was its grammar taught earlier?
11. Does the Act Two break change the nature of the story, or only raise the danger? Does the end of Act Three turn the protagonist's victory into a new defeat?
12. Stakes test: is "do nothing about the crisis" a viable option for this character? Have you marked each act's +/− polarity? Does any act end on a win?
13. Where is each of the four information tools used? Does the protagonist know more than the audience for long stretches (especially about backstory needed to understand their motive)? Will this season's central mystery be resolved by the finale?
14. Is the teaser story or information? If you used a teaser flashback, did it accidentally answer this episode's dramatic question?
15. Does the last act do nothing but pay off, introducing no new information? (**Does not apply to long-form Chinese series**: that system requires a big hook at the end of every episode, in direct conflict with this item. When writing a Chinese series, apply this only to the season finale and use the end-of-episode suspense rules in `sw-chinese-series-practice` for regular episodes.) Before the penultimate act ends, does the audience know exactly which debts remain unsettled? Does the ending have something to *discover* while **not** paying everything off?
16. Streaming script: with the ACT markers deleted, can the invisible acts still be drawn using the three-part test (story day / location change / turn of the central question)? Does every act end on a transfer of information or power?
17. Season shape: is this episode in the right position in the season (six-episode season: lay it out / the want / the exposure / the bottle episode / the false victory / the closed social occasion)? Does the finale turn around on something symmetrical with the pilot? Does each line's resolution simultaneously create debt for the next season?
18. Acceleration check: could the coolest thing at the end of Act Four be moved to the end of the teaser? Does this episode contain "the scene everyone talks about the next day"?
19. Production check: have you counted the interior/exterior and day/night ratios? Did you write only DAY / NIGHT? Have you inverted the production constraints into the three choices of a bottle episode?

## 10. Workflow: From an Episode Idea to a Delivered Draft

1. **Settle three things before writing** (Oberg): **series type** (procedural / serial / hybrid / limited / anthology / sitcom), **story type** (plot-led / character-led / theme-led / hybrid / exception) and **format** (episode length plus broad category). If any one is vague, do not start the outline. (Determining story type is in `sw-series-engine-bible`.)
2. **Pick a structural comp** (Calvisi and Landau agree): find a **successful** show close to yours in tone, genre, subject and audience, and use it as a structural template; if you cannot get the script, watch and write out a scene list. Ask four questions: **is there a teaser? how many act breaks? how many scenes an act? is there a tag or epilogue?** It doubles as your comp when you pitch. **A ready-made sample of 47 shows** (title | year | format | network/platform | act structure | premise/non-premise/hybrid pilot | showrunner; Landau 1e appendix, as of 2014) is in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 11 — you do not need to count them yourself.
3. **Take two or three episodes of the comp apart into the grid** (Douglas): name the A/B/C lines, write a one-sentence log line hung on a regular for each, and fill the letters in cell by cell.
4. **Settle this episode's central question**, and the **entry scene and exit scene of each line** (Downton's line table: 8–12 lines an episode is the ceiling, and 1–2 of them must be unconnected to the main crisis).
5. **Reverse-engineer** (Douglas): fill only three cells first — the opening, the "worst case" three-quarters in, and the ending; do only the A line, or A+B, leaving room for C; then work backwards from the cliffhanger to the preceding beats.
6. **Build landmarks or dramatic sequences for each line separately**: use Miller's six landmarks (in a pilot, "hero committed" has to kill two birds), or Oberg's inciting incident → subgoal → sequence → scene. **Keep the lines apart at this stage; do not weave.**
7. **Compress onto a one-page grid** (Miller chapter 5): a row of act-label cards across the top, a column of cards under each act, one card per scene, each carrying a scene heading plus the **character want**. For an ad-free show, break in **teaser + four acts** first and delete the markers in the final draft; for six acts, split acts three and four in half. Check at a glance: are the landmarks in place, are the act breaks hard enough, is the protagonist proactive, does this episode fit this season?
8. **Lay out the act outs**: give each to a different line; choose from the eight forms; guarantee at least one wordless or prop act out; keep the squeeze mid-act (it needs to be played out).
9. **Give every episode a "procedural line" as its metronome** (common to all six West Wing episodes): an external crisis (a military strike, a besieged embassy, the length of an operation, a vote count) supplies legitimate interruptions and an "one hour left" deadline, and in the end hands its numbers to the protagonist as a moral argument; it does not carry the act outs.
10. **Write the outline / beat sheet**: an hour drama uses a treatment or a step outline; **Blum gives a series treatment 5–10 pages, "the shorter the better," and it must state the act breaks** (a film treatment runs 10–20). Write each "beat" in the outline as that scene's log line. Room process is in `sw-writers-room`.
11. **Weave only when writing the teleplay**: layer several strands inside one scene, use ellipsis, cross-cut; now give every scene its five parts.
12. **Write long and compress, or hold the page count**: Succession's practice was 70+ pages an episode (leaving room to refocus in the edit); Calvisi's counter-warning is that a newcomer should not exceed 60. Which one applies depends on whether you are the showrunner and whether the show is already shooting.
13. **Make one formatting decision before delivery**: label the acts or delete them (per the test in 2.6); write the teaser out as `SMASH CUT TO: MAIN TITLES`, and end each act with `FADE OUT.` plus `END OF ACT N` (100% consistent across six West Wing episodes); **a pilot script carries no scene numbers** (Miller: "Pilot scripts do NOT have scene numbers.").
14. **Run the checklist in Section 9**, then finish the way Grace Paley finished (as relayed by Landau) — **"I look for the lies."**

## 11. Exercises

1. **Take an episode apart into the grid** (Douglas's classroom assignment): name the A/B/C lines, write a log line for each, fill the letters in cell by cell; mark which line owns each act-end suspense, which line does not start until later, and which line ends as early as Act Three.
2. **Goldberg & Rabkin's paired exercise** (p0033): ① watch an episode, stop at each act break, **write the act's narrative arc in one sentence and its final scene in one or two lines**; ② watch several episodes' **Act Two breaks** in a row and analyse how that moment changes the story's direction.
3. **Count the act outs**: take one episode, assign each of its five act outs to one of the eight types, and identify which one is schmuck bait.
4. **Calvisi's Story Map**: take a pilot apart with the Basic 17 columns plus the full map, marking which page the AOP lands on and whether the DOW sits at 4/5; then beat out your own pilot on the same form.
5. **Miller's landmark sheet**: write six lines for your own pilot; then take apart a pilot whose **structure** (not subject) resembles yours, with a breakdown containing scene headings, story beats, character wants, scene ins and outs, act breaks and landmarks.
6. **Escalation dissection**: pick two scenes of 1.5–2 minutes, count the escalations one by one (aim for more than ten), mark the decision and the resolution, and tally how many are action and how many are dialogue.
7. **Reverse-engineer invisible acts**: take an episode of a show with no ACT markers (Succession, Homeland, the Sopranos), draw the act positions with the three-part test, write "act-out event + basis for the division" for each, and compare against the Succession measurements in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 4.
8. **Information-management rewrite**: take an act out that cuts away mid-fight and write three versions — one surprise, one newly established dramatic irony, one mystery; judge which makes you most want the next episode.
9. **Act-count conversion**: take a four-act episode outline and convert it into five acts (split Act Four), six acts (split acts three and four), and no acts (story-day markers), each time finding a defensible event for every new act break.
10. **Acceleration exercise** (Rabkin): move the coolest thing at the end of your pilot's Act Four to the end of the teaser, rewrite the whole episode, and see whether the next sixty pages can hold the same speed.
11. **Budget-forced writing**: impose four bans on yourself (no guest stars, no exteriors, no new sets, no extras) and write a bottle episode — one night, one building — taking the chance to advance the season-arc secret you have been avoiding, and raising the line count from four to five.
12. **Six-episode season shape**: write six one-sentence episode summaries on the Fleabag skeleton, and nominate one object that **changes hands once per episode**; the final image of the finale resolves only the theme.
13. **Symmetry exercise** (Succession season 1): find a number, an object or a line in the first episode that the finale uses to complete a turnaround.
14. **Container-episode exercise**: write an episode in which "everyone is locked in one building with restricted communications," then one set on "a ritual day with a schedule" (list the fixed stages and stuff one conflict into each).

# Series Case Studies

**A library of worked examples**: each case gives "what this text is / structural data / transferable technique." The principles are in `sw-series-structure`, `sw-series-engine-bible`, `sw-writers-room` and `sw-sitcom-comedy`; Succession has its own skill, `succession-series-writing`.

## 1. The West Wing: the Anchor Text for the Broadcast Four Acts Plus Teaser

Sorkin's *The West Wing Script Book* (Newmarket, 2002) collects **six teleplays credited solely to Sorkin** (S1E01 Pilot, S1E03 A Proportional Response, S2E01–02 In the Shadow of Two Gunmen I/II, S2E18 17 People, S2E22 Two Cathedrals), each with a one-page author's preface. They are **shooting scripts**, carrying `TEASER` / `ACT ONE–FOUR` / `END OF SHOW` marks and scene numbers and page numbers — **the act boundaries are a fact on the page and need no inference.** It is therefore the only four-act sample from the 1999–2001 NBC one-hour (42–44 minutes of show) that can be quantified page by page.

- **Act-page table (PDF pages)**: Pilot **8 + 19/18/12/11 = 68**; A Proportional Response **4 + 14/14/18/19 = 69**; Two Gunmen I **7 + 21/14/11/14 = 67**; II **2 + 11/14/16/18 = 61**; 17 People **6 + 14/10/10/18 = 58**; Two Cathedrals **3 + 13/13/12/17 = 58**.
- **Teasers run 2–8 pages (median 5)**, with length inversely proportional to how much backstory the audience needs: the premise pilot's is longest, and the second half of a two-parter's is shortest (2 pages); the finale's is 3 pages with the slug marked `— CONTINUOUS`, picking up directly from the last scene of the previous episode.
- **Act lengths come in only two shapes**: front-loaded only in the Pilot (which spends two acts building the world); **the other five are back-loaded, with Act Four the longest or second longest.** Acts two and three compress most easily (17 People runs 10/10), and **Act Four is never under 11 pages**; total page counts fall season by season (68/69 → 67/61/58/58).
- **Four to seven story lines, with four the standard**; the bottle episode has five and the finale seven — **when there are no exteriors to shoot, the number of lines is the source of rhythm.**
- **Every episode has a "procedural line"** (the Cuban rafts / the retaliatory strike / the FAA terror threat / the Haitian embassy), which **carries no act out at all** and exists only to supply interruptions, deadlines, and a number handed to the protagonist at the end (the Pilot's Act Four: 1,200 set out / 700 turned back / 350 missing / 137 detained).
- **Eight act-out types**: the suspense question / the reversal line / the emotional reveal / wordless action / a held prop / a repeated line whose meaning is rewritten / a severed setup / a planted gesture paid off. The first two episodes use conventional hooks, and the later four lean further and further toward the emotional.
- **The bottle episode was forced by budget**: Warner ordered "no guest cast, no locations, no new sets, no extras, as little film as possible," and Sorkin's conversion was "then I get to write a play; and it was time for the President to come clean about his condition" — **a constraint is the trigger that says it is time to move the main line.**
- **A wordless act out must have its grammar taught first**: in Act Three a supporting character recites the tells item by item (hands in pockets / look away / smile = he has decided), and in Act Four at the press conference he performs the three actions again without a word, and `just as he smiles we FADE TO BLACK`. **The corridor walk-and-talk and the planted gesture paid off are the two most copyable things in these six scripts.**

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Start each episode with four lines** (A the main line / B comedy or relationship / C emotional / D planting people for the season arc); when resources are tight, **add lines rather than lengthen scenes**, and add a procedural line as the metronome.
2. **The corridor is a free set**: one passage of dialogue can run across three or four `INT. X — CONTINUOUS` slugs, laying in setup, planting a payoff and landing a joke all at once.
3. **Turn the secret into a countable number** (15 → 16 → **17**; "the **35th** in the last two hours"), so the audience has a progress bar.
4. **A button should say less**: the answer may go unsaid, the joke may go unfinished, the emotion may be only an action; **cut to black mid-action.**

> A disagreement to preserve: Sorkin writes explicitly, "I put no organization to the writing process. The writing is done on the fly." — which conflicts with the outline/bible school (see `sw-writers-room`). The irony: the only structure in the book that was planned in advance, the two-parter, is the tidiest.

## 2. The Sopranos: the Engine and a Pilot's Art of Leaving Blanks

Chase et al., *The Sopranos: Selected Scripts from Three Seasons* (Warner Books, 2002): five **final shooting scripts** (Pilot, College, The Happy Wanderer, The Knight in White Satin Armor, Pine Barrens) plus a four-page Chase foreword. HBO scripts carry **no ACT and no teaser marks**, so every "section" in this section is **an inference judged from the therapy-room frame and the turns**; the only facts on the page are the scene numbers (the Pilot is 67 pages, sc. 1–81).

- **About 35 beats an episode** (Chase's own words): "**thirty-five beats**—**thirteen** or so in the A and B stories, usually **five to six** in the C story, and sometimes a D story with just a few beats." **A and B carry equal weight, C is secondary, and D is usually a comic runner.**
- **The season's theme is not decided in advance**: at the top of a season they write only arcs and touchstones and are not bound by them; season one's theme (with the incapacitated mother) "organically emerged" only on looking back at the season's end.
- **The pilot carries twice the usual number of lines**: A (the attack → therapy → the ducks = the fear of losing the family) / B (the uncle wants to kill someone in a friend's restaurant) / C (the waste contract and a debt → insurance fraud) / D (mother–daughter conflict), plus E (the season arc's seed) and three runners.
- **The therapy room is a device, not an explainer**: the Pilot has **11 therapy scenes** (sc. 1/13/15/18/22/24/44/48/52 with an empty chair/70/74), functioning as a narrative frame, a source of V.O., a namer of the theme, and plot. **The audience always knows more than the therapist**: having just watched him run a man down and beat him, he says "Nothing. We had coffee."
- **The confessional device can change hands**: College has **no therapy scenes at all**, with the wife's confession to a priest substituting; Chase picked it because it is "self-contained" and could stand as a feature — and it is also the only one of the five with **just two lines, A and B.**
- **The pilot does not detonate at the end**: after the uncle's probing, the script writes "She says nothing… **He has her blessing.**", and then the family goes indoors and `PAN to the still and silent pool.` **It closes on an absence (no ducks), not on an event.**
- **A bottle episode is not a single-line episode**: Pine Barrens strands two men in the woods overnight (the A line) while **the B/C/D lines advance as usual**; three therapy scenes form the B line's skeleton and never intersect the woods at all; the Russian's fate is never explained in the entire series; and **the episode's emotional landing is given to the therapy room.**

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Proportion the lines to 35 beats**, listing beats line by line and then **cutting them up and reordering**; **name the theme by looking back at the end of the season**, and never build a story to fit a theme.
2. **The irony formula of a double life**: show the audience the violence first, then have the character tell someone a decent lie ("We had coffee." / "Talking helps.").
3. **Let the B line's wish be granted by the A line's disaster** (the daughter gets the solo because the classmate's father has been eaten by the protagonist) — find the joint where the audience is pleased and sickened at once.
4. **Refuse three times, then agree**, and when agreeing hand the responsibility back with a rule ("don't say short if you don't mean short").

## 3. Downton Abbey Season Two: Weaving 8–12 Lines in a British Ensemble

Fellowes, *Downton Abbey: The Complete Scripts, Season Two*: **the complete shooting scripts annotated with 419 author's footnotes**, Ep1–8 plus the Christmas special. It carries **ACT marks for ITV's commercial breaks**, with the act count varying: **six in Ep1, four in Ep2–5, five in Ep6, five in Ep7, six in Ep8 and seven in the special.** The footnotes are rarer than the scripts — this is the only text in the whole body of material where an author annotates himself.

- **Scene counts**: Ep1 Sc 1–71 / Ep3 1–73 / Ep4 1–60 / Ep5 1–61 / Ep6 1–64A / Ep7 1–56 / Ep8 1–77 / **the special 1–110.** The season's scripts were "too long," and ITV allowed 70–75 minutes, which meant one more commercial break and complaints from viewers, so **season three returned to a strict hour** (currency: the ITV schedule of 2011–12).
- **A grid of 19 story lines** (the full table is in reference.md), running simultaneously — ten in Ep1, twelve in Ep5, eleven in Ep6, eleven in Ep8, nine in the special.
- **The ensemble-scene specification (footnote 326)**: lunches, dinners and drawing-room scenes "always have to **advance about six or seven stories at the same time, often by no more than a line.**"
- **Only four act-out types**: a request made across a line (bribing a maid) / a hidden fact stated in one sentence ("He is standing.") / a Dowager-style button ("I have a cold.") / a physical fact (a corpse, handcuffs, a breakdown into tears, going up to read the lesson).
- **The rules for killing a character**: you cannot have no regular die; the one who dies must be familiar and loved — "**It's like a whodunit. The killer has to be someone who was there all the time.**"; and **deliberately misdirect the audience toward the wrong person.**
- **The resistance is supplied by institutions**: documentary evidence, an illegitimate child having no legal claim on the father, buying off a scandal, a Decree Nisi revoked for collusion, a brachial plexus injury (chosen because it is "serious but requires no special effects") — **the antagonist is merely whoever knows the rules best.**

**Transferable techniques**
1. **"The Downton dilemma = both sides are right"** (the most frequent item in the footnotes): "a typical Downton argument, where hopefully you're on both sides." **If one side is plainly wrong, that line fails.**
2. **Draw up a line table for each episode**, writing the **entry and exit scene numbers** of each; 8–12 is the ceiling, and 1–2 of them must be unconnected to the main crisis.
3. **Run the upstairs and downstairs emotional lines in parallel, at equal weight and cross-cut**, saying the same theme in two entirely different registers.

## 4. Fleabag: the Six-Episode Half-Hour Template, With the Device as the Season Arc

Waller-Bridge, *Fleabag: The Scriptures* (Sceptre, 2019): **12 shooting scripts across two seasons** (about 25 minutes each) plus an authorial memoir at the end. **A half-hour carries no ACT marks** — the division into movements is **an inference by location and time block**; the facts on the page are the fixed device and the roughly **2,900–3,600 lines** per S1 script. It is the only sample that treats the fourth wall **as a season-arc design.**

- **The per-episode skeleton**: `cold open (1–3 minutes, carrying its own joke or its own mystery) → TITLES: FLEABAG → 3–7 movements → button (≤1 page, usually wordless)`; **the one exception is S2E6**, which prints `TITLE CARD: THE WEDDING` instead.
- **The function of each of the six positions (strictly symmetrical across both seasons, and directly copyable)**: 1 lay out every character plus the season's suspense / 2 convert the emotion into a followable want / 3 a family occasion plus the first card played face up / 4 **the bottle episode** (the retreat / the confessional: a closed space plus one prohibition) / 5 the false victory at its peak / 6 recall everyone to one closed social occasion (an exhibition / a wedding) where every line closes.
- **Neither season is a premise pilot**: episode one does not use an event to push the protagonist into a new situation; the engine is **a web of relationships already busy hurting each other**, and at each character's first appearance the protagonist nails them down with a line to camera.
- **The device's five-stage inversion is the season arc**: in S1 the camera is an accomplice → in S1E6 **the camera chases her into a corner and forces the flashback out** → in S2E3 the Priest sees it for the first time ("What was that? Where did you just go?") → in S2E5 she shouts "STOP THAT" and pushes the camera away → in S2E6 she asks it not to follow, looks back from a distance and waves, and the final line is a stage direction, "Goodbye."
- **The season gives exactly one long monologue not addressed to camera** (the S2E4 confessional) = **the show's emotional peak.**
- **"Visual sentences,"** the image the director supplied: **Boo standing across the street with traffic sweeping past** — cut in while she is laughing, having sex, "feeling fine," so the audience knows **she isn't OK.**
- **The device was tested before it was written**: the dramaturg filmed her on a phone making tea and talking to the camera in her kitchen, and after five minutes put the phone down: "**It works.**" **The one test for another season**: "**If she has something else to learn, then we have more story to tell.**"

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Lay out a six-episode season as 1 lay it out / 2 the want / 3 the card face up / 4 the bottle episode / 5 the false victory / 6 the closed occasion where everything settles.**
2. **The device is itself the season arc**: in season two, let **one character see it** and escalate episode by episode (discovery → interrogation → an order to stop → she pushes it away → she asks it to leave). Once that journey is complete, the show should end.
3. **The protagonist tells the camera only jokes and people only the truth**; one long monologue not to camera per season, and only one.
4. **Write parts for specific actors and give the actors a veto** (the Godmother was created by "cracking open the pilot script and rewriting the ending" after meeting the actor; the closing "I love you too" was added at the actor's insistence).

> Joke mechanics (the two-part setup, the punch word last, the topper, the triple to camera plus a fourth beat inverted by someone else) belong to `sw-sitcom-comedy`.

## 5. Calvisi's Eight Minute-by-Minute Pilot Beat Sheets

> The eight pilots' beat-by-beat tables (beat name → minute mark) are in [reference-pilots.md](reference-pilots.md) §5.

The CASE STUDIES in Calvisi's *Story Maps: TV Drama*: beat sheets for eight pilots, in which **the number before each beat is a minute mark.** His own stated methodological limits must be cited with them: they were **transcribed beat by beat from the finished episodes** ("transcribed as I watched them"), assume **one minute ≈ one page**, and mostly record only signpost beats. So they are **measurements of the cut episode's structure, not facts of the script**; and in shows with no act marks, the act boundaries are his own **inference** — "I've inserted my own act breaks… in what feel like the natural positions."

**Three lateral rulers (aggregated across the eight, and directly checkable)**: the midpoint almost always lands at **24–30 minutes**; the Assumption of Power always lands at **36–48 (mostly 37–40)**; and the Declaration of War / All Is Lost always lands at **about 4/5 of the way through (43–52).**

- **Scandal (2012, 45′, teaser + 5, A/B/C/D)**: **the shortest of the eight**, "front-loaded, with both **Catalyst and Inciting Incident occurring in the six minute Teaser**," launching three lines in six minutes. In an earlier draft the bloodied man did not come through the door until page **12**, and the revision moved it up. **The theme does not tighten until minutes 41–42.** The four lines exit at staggered points: 39-40 (D) → 42 (A) → 43-44 (B) → 44-45 (C).
- **Mr. Robot (2015, 64′, teaser + 5, A/B/C)**: the closest fit to the template. **The midpoint is stretched into a whole section at 25–30**; the **AOP is "proposed and refused"** (42-48), with the real action saved for the DOW (50-54). The exits run 56-58 (B) → 60 (C) → **62 (A) last**, the reverse of Scandal.
- **True Detective (2014, 58′, only two lines, A/B)**: **no teaser**; his structural confession — "my original map was only four acts in total, but I chose to define a short **Act Four as a 'bridge act'** since this section feels like a separate movement." The A line is the relationship plus the 2012 interrogations, the B line the 1995 case; **the AOP splits into the B line at 40′ and the A line at 48′.** Four **long-range plants** are explicitly listed (the first victim / the killer's scars / the Yellow King / the falling-out).
- **The Walking Dead (2010, 66′ — an extended premiere slot, teaser + 5, A/B/C)**: **the model premise pilot**, whose Week-to-Week has **not yet been established** in this episode. **Two major characters do not appear again in the rest of the season**, and the author gives a three-part defence: they are the vehicle for conveying the world's rules / **the protagonist is in every scene, so a B line is needed to let the audience breathe** / and they stand in for the separated family. The broadcast act-design principle: **every act brings a new movement and its own engine, closes clearly at the act's end, and leaves only the hint that there is more.** Plants come in pairs: the walker mob at 43′ → 60′.
- **Game of Thrones (2011, 60′, teaser + 5, A/B/C/D)**: **multi-protagonist**, and of the three co-protagonists **only one makes a decision, and off screen**; the defence is that a pilot's job is **world-building**, selling "the promise of high adventure" rather than paying it off. **The inciting incident arrives late, at minute 18, in Act Two.** **Act heads are marked by location TITLE cards** (Winterfell / King's Landing / Pentos). **The beats run once per line**: three Shadow Showdowns (32 / 38-39 / 41-43), two AOPs and neither belonging to the protagonist (40 / 46). **The letters follow screen time**: Dany, who does not appear until Act Three, is the B line. Long plants: the dragon eggs planted at 53-54 and hatched only at the season's end; Bran climbing the tower planted at 23-24 and paid at 58-60.
- **Breaking Bad (2008, 57′, teaser + 5, A/B/C) — the golden reference for the template**: built out of a series of **2-minute scenes**, with **scene length itself as a rhythm tool**; several scenes were cut before broadcast, which made it "**a nearly perfect example of the template.**" **The AOP splits into two adjacent beats** (the A line at 36-37, the B line at 38-39); **Act Five closes the loop back to the teaser's image** (49-51 matching 1-3). **Positions established in a pilot can invert**: the brother-in-law is essentially an ally in the pilot, and the partner is the Shadow.
- **House of Cards (2013, 54′, A/B/C/D)**: the script is written as a feature with **no act marks**, and Calvisi inserts a teaser plus five. Three deviations: **the FT/FC lands in Act One (14-15), the only exception among the eight**; **Act Two has only three beats with a gap between 19′ and 25′**; and **the CLIMAX closes in the order C → B → A, with the A line saved for the last scene**, looping back to the teaser's dog through 52-54.
- **Mad Men (2007, 48′ as cut / 52 pages of script, four acts with no teaser — the fewest acts of the eight)**: the reverse-engineering process is worth copying: the script has only **three FADE OUTs**, and the cut episode's structure does not match the script, so he **iterated several times** before settling on "four acts, no teaser." **The A line closes at the end of Act Two** (27-32), leaving acts three and four to the character lines; **ALL IS LOST splits into one each for A/B/C**; and the **BIG ENDING is a single scene running on an information reveal.** **The pilot leads with the louder of two themes** ("It's a man's world" first, with the real theme taking over later).

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Move the catalyst and the inciting incident forward** (Scandal moved it from page 12 to minute 6 of the teaser).
2. **Give every line its own exit beat and stagger them**; by default **close the supporting lines first and leave the protagonist's hanging**, and only invert that deliberately.
3. **If the protagonist is in every scene, there must be a B line letting the audience breathe** — even if that line's characters never appear again this season.
4. **Pair every plant with a payoff and mark them as pairs**; and **keep a separate list of long-range plants to be recovered in later episodes** (as True Detective does with its four).

## 6. How to Read Landau's Structure Table

> The full seven-column table of 47 shows is in [reference-pilots.md](reference-pilots.md) §6.

The appendix **"Top Television Series by Structure"** in Landau's *The TV Showrunner's Roadmap* (first edition): **47 shows** in seven columns — title | year | format | network | structure | pilot type (premise / non-premise / hybrid) | showrunner. **A warning about the text's identity**: the scan of that page is rotated with garbled OCR, and the notes reconstruct only what is legible, so **a blank in the table means "could not be read," not "not in the book."** The full table is in reference-pilots.md.

- **The distribution of act counts**: `teaser + 4 acts` is the mode for the hour (Breaking Bad, CSI, Law & Order, Mad Men, The West Wing, The X-Files, ER and others); `teaser + 5 acts` (Friday Night Lights, Parenthood, Touch; Justified adds a tag); **6 acts with no teaser** (Grey's Anatomy, Once Upon a Time, Revenge, Rizzoli & Isles); **Scandal is "6 acts, with a short first act serving as the teaser, after which the title card plays"**; **Royal Pains has 7 acts** (the table's maximum); and premium cable and streaming show a great many "**no act breaks**" (Deadwood, Dexter, Downton Abbey, Game of Thrones, Homeland, House of Cards, Weeds, The Sopranos and others). Sitcoms: `cold open + 2 acts + tag` (The Big Bang Theory, Two and a Half Men), `cold open + 2 acts` (The Office), `4 acts with a short first act` (Modern Family).
- **Premise vs. non-premise is the more useful column**: those marked **premise** are mostly shows whose world has to be built from scratch (24, American Horror Story, Breaking Bad, Deadwood, Lost, Revenge, The Sopranos, The X-Files plus three sitcoms); those marked **non-premise** (the typical-episode kind) are mostly procedurals and ensembles (CSI, Dexter, **Downton Abbey**, Game of Thrones, Law & Order, Modern Family, The Office, Scandal, **The West Wing**); and **only two are marked hybrid**: Damages and The Walking Dead.
- **"No act breaks" does not mean no acts**: the same book's body text notes that HBO's *Deadwood* scripts have **clear four acts** while Showtime's *Homeland* scripts do **not** — writing act breaks "both grabs the audience at the plot level and fits into a given time slot."
- **Page-count norms (currency: around 2013)**: a half-hour multi-camera sitcom double-spaced runs about **50 pages**; a half-hour single-camera or dramedy about **30**; an hour drama **48–63**, with pilots often longer; Moonlighting, The West Wing and E.R., with their machine-gun dialogue, can reach **85+ pages** and still cut to an hour.
- **How to use it**: treat the table as **a sampler of shows of your type** — before writing a pilot, find the row whose tone and pace match what you want and ask, item by item: is there a teaser? how many act breaks? how many scenes an act? is there a tag? is the pilot premise or typical-episode? Then write to that row's specification.
- **The same show's act count changes**: The Walking Dead appears as `teaser + 4 acts` with a note that **Mazzara changed it to teaser + 5 on taking over** (AMC wanted another ad slot) — **act count is a function of ad slots** (this table reflects roughly the 2013 landscape; the platform map has changed completely, and while the causality still holds, check a given show's act count against the season in question).

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Set your specification from the row of a comparable show first** (teaser or not / act count / scenes per act / tag), and then write.
2. **Derive the act count from the ad slots**, and do not mistake streaming's "no act breaks" for "no structure" — the script can still carry clear four acts.
3. **Settle the pilot type first**: if the world must be built from scratch, write a premise pilot; procedurals and ensembles default to the typical-episode kind.

> A disagreement to preserve: Landau writes "**No outline, no paycheck.**" (studios and networks must sign off); in an interview in the same book Mazzara objects — "I don't use outlines… they are used to engage TV and studio executives… None of their notes are applicable to script." — substituting **a two- or three-page prose story document plus a beat sheet of two or three words a beat** (e.g. "Rick kills Shane."). **Which to use**: if it must pass a platform sign-off, or if it goes to a freelance writer (the Sopranos rule was that a freelancer had to follow the outline strictly), write the outline; if you are the showrunner and rewrite every episode yourself, a beat sheet alone will do. See `sw-writers-room`.

## 7. Miller: Two Scenes Taken Apart Beat by Beat, and Four Student Pilot Outlines

> The itemized escalation tables for the two scenes and the act/scene counts of the four student outlines are in [reference-pilots.md](reference-pilots.md) §7.

Chapter 9 of Miller's *The Hero Succeeds* gives the **five-part scene structure** (hero's want / obstacle / escalations / decision / resolution, adapted from ER writer Lance Gentile) with two itemized escalation breakdowns; the appendix collects **four physical student pilot outlines** (numbered scenes, standard scene headings, act marks, first appearances in all caps with a thumbnail, and almost no dialogue). The two scenes' durations and beat counts are **the author's own count while watching**; where the six landmarks fall inside the outlines is **an inference back from the model.**

- **The Hannibal pilot, the FBI men's-room scene (from about 16:06, 1 min 57 sec, 14 escalations)**: want = he wants to understand this killer who baffles him; obstacle = his boss wants to know **now.** The escalation chain (excerpted): asked what he is doing in the bathroom → he deflects with humour ("I like the smell of urine cake.") → the boss swats the humour away and shouts at an agent who comes in, "Use the ladies' room!" → asks whether he respects his own judgement → he admits he is baffled and says **the killer may not be a textbook psychopath** (new information) → pressed on what he is withholding → the boss produces the evidence (no semen, no saliva, the victim still a virgin).
- **Decision (= the scene's climax)**: he defends the killer as if he were the killer — "That isn't how the killer loves them. The killer kills them quickly and mercifully." **Resolution**: the boss believes him ("A sensitive psychopath"). In the next scene the boss goes to ask a psychiatrist to evaluate him (having just seen him break down and then break through), and in the scene after that he is in the evidence room **trying not to look at the body** — **scenes connect by emotional logic, not by plot points.**
- **The HTGAWM teaser's woods scene (about 31 seconds into the teaser, 1 min 40 sec, 11 escalations)**: want = retrieve the body and bury it; obstacle = two of them want to stay put. The escalation chain is entirely **reasonable objections**: take the weapon back → wipe it clean and "hide it in plain sight" → leave the body where it is → agree only to retrieve the weapon → "don't carry a body across campus on the busiest night of the year" → **the ground is frozen** → we have all night to dig → "this is murder, and none of us knows what we're talking about." **Decision**: 2–2, so they flip a coin. The teaching point is **exposition through conflict.**
- **Hard conditions**: a scene needs at least two characters and two wants, and **diametrically opposed is strongest**; **each scene still has only one hero, and "Your hero may change from scene to scene"**; **at least 3 escalations but more is better**; and **prefer action** — "If all of your escalations are oral… Your TV show turns into a radio show."
- **The scene counts of the four outlines**: *Jerusalem, TN* **33** (teaser 6 / 8 / 8 / 6 / 5); *Girl Out of Water* **44** (9 / 5 / 10 / 9 / 11); *Minotaur* **43** (3 / 9 / 7 / **16** / 8); *Exit Stage Left* (half-hour single-camera) **27** (8 / 6 / 6 / 4 / 3). **All four are teaser plus four acts**; scene count does not equal duration (comedy scenes run longer).
- **Head-and-tail mirroring written into the outline**: scene 25 of *Girl Out of Water* states it — "As in the first shot of the episode, Sierra is facedown in the water. **This time, she's not moving.**"; and **the B line's hero-succeeds at the end of Act Two sits in the same act as the A line's hero-fails** — "The juxtaposition of landmark scenes makes them pack more of an emotional punch."

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Count your escalations**: fewer than 3 and it is not a scene; make at least half of them action.
2. **Deliver setup through argument**: every objection must be reasonable, and the information hides inside the objections.
3. **Follow a key scene with two scenes of "emotional consequence,"** not with the next plot point.

## 8. Goldberg & Rabkin's Appendix Beat Sheets (the physical documents)

> Per-act scene tables for the three beat sheets are in [reference-pilots.md](reference-pilots.md) §8.

*Successful Television Writing*, appendices D–I: **four physical beat sheets plus two leave-behinds**, two of which print **the same episode's leave-behind and beat sheet side by side**, so you can read word for word how "a one-page story area" grows into a thirty-scene outline. The header wording is the draft stage: `Story Area` → `Rough Beat Sheet` → `First Draft Beat Sheet` → `Revised Outline`. Two industry facts (currency: the late 1990s to early 2000s): **a beat sheet has no standard format and follows the showrunner's habits**; and "you get paid whether the script is shot or not!"

**Martial Law's "Sammo Blammo" (an action buddy show; a textbook on constraint devices)**
- **Scene counts**: TEASE **2** / Act One **5** / Act Two **8** / Act Three **5** / Act Four **10** = about **30 scenes.** Against the same book's "6–7 scenes an act, four acts" and "fifteen to twenty-five scenes": **an action show's later acts fragment markedly.**
- **A constraint device's rules must be written out in full**: the bomb is strapped to the protagonist's chest and can only be disarmed by a single encrypted mini-disc; opening the casing, freezing, smashing, magnetizing and X-raying all detonate it; and there is a motion sensor — **if the protagonist does not move, it explodes**; as a "fair warning" it will **BEEP FOUR TIMES, and the fifth beep is KA-BLAMMO.**
- **A small nuisance planted in Act One is recovered in Act Four**: the street thug who was beaten off returns in bandages **at the worst possible moment** (just as the protagonist is about to storm the bank).
- **The Act Two out changes the nature of the story** (a sniper silences the witness first, turning "investigate an old case" into "race the clock to stay alive"); **the Act Three out is "it's all over"** (the case is solved, which makes things worse, because the villain only wants money).
- **Write two versions of the leave-behind**: for the network you need only write "the conflict that drives this episode" — "The network takes it on faith that the character conflicts will be spelled out in the beat sheet, **but a producer won't grant a freelancer the same benefit of the doubt.**"

**seaQuest 2032's "Depths of Deceit" (political science fiction, where every act out is an information event)**
- The floor of how rough a `Rough Beat Sheet` may be: **locations may be written `SOMEPLACE –` / `SOMEWHERE –`, with no scene numbers, in about 6 pages total** (the Martial Law one runs about 15 pages with complete slugs). Lock the order of power moves and information reveals first.

**Two Diagnosis Murder documents (a one-hour whodunit and a two-hour episode)**
- The one-hour: TEASE **1** scene plus Act One **6** / Act Two **7** / Act Three **6** / Act Four **6**, with no tag. **No body appears until the end of Act Two** — the first two acts are entirely devoted to building a web in which anyone might have a reason to kill anyone; and the Act One out is not a crisis but **a moral lapse.**
- **A procedural's licence to intervene written as one line of dialogue**: "if it doesn't impact on his medical condition, **it's none of our business**" — and in the end it is precisely the medical record that pulls the protagonist in.
- **The two-hour "The Last Laugh": eight acts, scene numbers running continuously across acts from 1–44, three lines.** The authors' definition: "The cliffhanger is essentially **a bigger version of the usual second-act break** in a one-hour episode… only on a larger scale." It has two functions: hold the audience for the second half; and **create a break strong enough that if it is ever split into two episodes for syndication, it brings the audience back the following week.**
- The first hour's closing cliffhanger (scene 27) does three things at once: it overturns every conclusion of the first hour (the "false solution" at the Act Two out was wrong); **the protagonist causes the disaster himself** (he personally lets the real killer go, and double jeopardy means they can never be prosecuted again); and the question changes from "who did it" to "**what now.**" Also: **a first-draft beat sheet may leave blanks** — scene 42 reads only "**Brilliant farce to be figured out.**" plus the outcome.

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Make a countdown audible as a warning** (four beeps, and the fifth is the explosion).
2. **Three hard laws**: the Act One ending is not the biggest crisis but "the protagonist pushed into a position with no way back"; the Act Two out must change the nature of the story; and the Act Three ending must turn the protagonist's victory into a new defeat.
3. **Self-check the constraint device**: substitute the constraint (a bomb, a deadline, an identity, a diagnosis, a prohibition) into each scene and ask "what new trouble does it create here"; **if you cannot answer for more than two scenes, it is only an opening gimmick.**
4. **Self-check the beat sheet**: write one sentence at each act ending saying "what the audience now thinks the story is about"; **if two consecutive acts give the same answer, you have not written the Act Two out.**
5. **The ending restores the engine**: a long-running show may not change its power structure, and only the understanding between characters changes; **the break in a two-hour or two-part episode is an enlarged Act Two out**, and it must be able to stand alone as an episode's ending.

## 9. Sakamoto Yuji, *I Fell in Love Like a Flower Bouquet*: Dialogue-Driven Relationship Drama

> The scene statistics, the five-year timeline and the examples are in [reference-asia.md](reference-asia.md) §9.

> **Note the medium: this is a feature screenplay** (released 2021, directed by Doi Nobuhiro; Chinese edition translated by Lei Ke, folio, December 2022). **There are no episodes, no acts and no act outs**, so it cannot be used as a model of series structure. The reason it is in this library: the author is one of Japan's most important television serial writers (*Tokyo Love Story*, *Mother*, *The Best Divorce*, *Quartet*, *Oomameda Towako and Her Three Ex-Husbands*), and this is the most concentrated and dissectible sample of his "dialogue-driven relationship drama." The "invisible three acts" below are **an inference from proportions.**

- **159 scenes, 1,822 effective lines, 35,000 characters, and about 124 minutes on screen**: an average of **11.5 lines a scene**; **72 scenes (45%) run ≤5 lines**; and only 2 scenes in the whole film exceed 100 lines.
- **The distribution across the year chapters** (seven handwritten date cards divide it): the 2020 prologue 2% / **2015 at 42% (73 scenes)** / 2016 6% / 2017 10% / 2018 12% / 2019 14% (only 12 scenes, almost all long ones) / the 2020 ending 2%. **The first year of the relationship takes 42% and the following four years take 42% between them — "remembering it in detail" is itself the form of the theme.**
- **Date precision = the freshness of the relationship**: 2015 is accurate to the day; 2016 keeps only 4/13 and 6/3; after 2017 there are only months; 2019 has just three markers. **Flashbacks are used only in the first quarter**: ten scenes are marked "recollection" in the whole film, and **not one appears after scene 42.**
- **All the weight rests on four or five long scenes**: scene 147, the break-up (**162 lines = 9% of the film**); scene 28, the cultural-checklist comparison on the night they meet (115 lines); scene 121, the big fight plus an angry proposal (71 lines); scene 52, the confession (54 lines); scene 103, the first quarrel (45 lines); and **the gaps between long scenes widen as the film goes on** (28 → 52 → 103 → 121 → 147).
- **Three audio tracks**: `X (monologue)` is the voice-over diary track, **151 entries** (Kinu 77 / Mugi 74); `X's voice` is a real off-screen voice inside the scene (love-making under a quilt, a phone call given only one side's picture, a replay in a dream); and there is a **sound bridge crossing over** (scenes 133–137, where the two leads' respective "advisers" interleave by voice, weaving five short scenes into one passage of double counsel).
- **Reusing locations instead of new settings**: only seven or eight locations recur (the couple's room 25 times, the family restaurant 3 times — the confession and the break-up in the same one), and **the second and third appearance of the same location is the entire explanation of a value turn**; `*` marks a subdivision inside a scene (32 of them distributed across 17 scenes, with scene 28 using 7 to break one night into 8 sections).
- **The dialogue budget is very unusual**: the two leads have **83%** of all the lines (Mugi 295 / Kinu 266); and **the supporting character with the most lines in the film is the "mirror couple" who appear only in the last scene (36 lines), seven times as many as the "romantic rival" (5)** — the budget goes almost entirely to the mirror and not to the obstacle.

**Disagreements to preserve**: ① **the turning points are not events but acts of consideration** — "while drying her hair he says I've decided to look for a job" (scene 85), "putting on the headphones he says turn the sound up and play" (scene 107); ② **there is no antagonist and no viable third party** (the rival has five lines, and no affair ever happens), with all the pressure coming from each lead's own kindness; ③ **death is not a turning point**, and the decision to break up happens at **a wedding**; ④ **no reconciliation is written** (neither quarrel has an apology scene).

**Transferable techniques (including the conversion to a series)**
1. **The "diary" form is a natural way to divide a series into episodes**: one episode = one stretch of time, with a handwritten date card at the top of the episode and 3–10 line micro-scenes stacked inside, **keeping only one long scene (10–15 minutes) per episode as its core.**
2. **Copy the proportion curve 42/6/10/12/14**, and **drop the date precision one grade per season (day → month → season → none)**, letting the precision itself replace a "time has passed" caption.
3. **Repeat the same line across years with its meaning inverted**: rock-paper-scissors three times (loneliness → being in step → "because I am a mature adult"); "even after reading Imamura Natsuko's *Picnic*, nothing stirs in me" is said by him to her, and four years later said back to him verbatim, and he can only fall silent.
4. **Use a recurring object rather than an event as the act out**: pick 3–4 objects a season (the headphones, the curtain, the seat, the games console), change their function at every appearance, and freeze the episode's end on the object.
5. **Write four mirror couples** (the one that broke first / the one that married first / the leads' own past / the leads' replacements), **giving the largest budget to "the leads' own past"**, and let that couple re-enact the leads' first episode on screen in the finale.

## 10. Noh Hee-kyung, *The Most Beautiful Goodbye*: the Family End-of-Life Drama

> **Note the text's identity**: what is in hand is the **novelization** of the Korean television writer Noh Hee-kyung's (노희경, *It's Okay, That's Love*, *The World They Live In*) drama of the same name (adapted by Lee Seong-suk, Chinese translation by Liu Yue, CITIC Press, April 2016, 244 pages of text; the copyright page reads "Copyright 2010 by NOH HEE KYONG"). **It is not a script**: no scene headings, no S# numbering, no action lines — it is third-person omniscient prose. **The book nowhere gives the broadcaster, the year or the episode count**, so any "mapping to N episodes" is an inference.
> The chapter-by-chapter structure table, the epigraph device, the four-episode mapping (inferred), the family conflict web and the examples are in [reference-asia.md](reference-asia.md) §10.

- **The length curve, 33 / 32 / 30 / 26 / 32 / 44 / 19 pages**: chapter four (announcing she is dying) is the shortest; chapter six (the family each preparing their goodbye) is the longest at 44; and chapter seven (the actual death) is the shortest at 19. → **The closer to death, the sparer the description; and the longest chapter is "preparation," not "dying."**
- **The epigraph device**: before the text of every chapter, one whole page prints dialogue only, no narration, quoting an exchange that will appear in full later in that chapter; it is always a refusal or a declaration between two or more people, and not one is lyrical. It gives the chapter's **value statement** rather than a plot hook — the audience already knows the ending, and the tension comes from "how will this line get said." Converted to a series, this is 30 seconds at the head of each episode playing only a piece of dialogue from later in the episode, with no picture.
- **Two clocks**: the visible clock is "move into the new house in Ilsan before deep winter arrives" (the in-story time from diagnosis to death is about five or six weeks, from November through the first snow to one clear morning); the hidden clock is the soybean-paste crock (chapter one, drying the paste → chapter six, paste fermented for thirty-odd years grows white mould for the first time → the father's "you will never eat that paste again"). The son's **university results day is a deliberately unfulfilled deadline** — the mother does not live to see it.
- **The patient learns it herself rather than being told**: the mother infers the conclusion from coughing blood and the mould in the crock (a folk omen), not because anyone tells her.

- **The character web (illness as a single detonator igniting six lines at once)**: the mother (called almost only "Mum" throughout, in her fifties, who wants the whole family moved into the new Ilsan house before winter) / the father (60, a doctor, retiring next year, who wants to complete that operation) / the grandmother (in her eighties, dementia plus a stroke, who wants not to be left behind) / the daughter (27, a retail display manager, who wants her love for a married man acknowledged) / the son (21, on his third attempt at the medical-school exam, who wants his mother to see the result) / the mother's brother (a gambler and a drunk, who wants money and to be recognized as not a waste of space) plus his wife (a market stallholder, who cannot have children).
- **One directly reusable writing discipline**: the characters in the book **never use the word "cancer"** — the mother "has still never once said the word 'cancer' aloud."
- **Law A: death is not a horror but "the last chance to become beautiful"** (afterword, item 10: both her parents died of cancer, and "death is not a frightening thing, it is the last chance to become beautiful"). The show's task is not to postpone death but to distribute that chance among every member of the family.
- **Law B: a real goodbye is flat.** The mother's death is "without a smile and without a painful struggle, she went very peacefully"; "**our parting was neither beautiful nor sad.**"; "I am not grateful that you said you loved me before you died, because I have *always* known how much you loved your youngest daughter." → **Wherever something would ordinarily be written as a climax (a deathbed declaration of love, the funeral, the diagnosis), push it down a grade.**
- **Law C: convert the grief into guilt.** "Before my mother died, I never thought she would become my regret. At the time, I was hers." — the engine of a family drama is not "whom I lost" but "**whose regret I was.**"
- **Law E: every family member's account of the same thing has been rewritten in self-protection.** The author puts her mother's telling of "the birth story" into the afterword, and her eldest sister then tells her it is not true; the conclusion is "**we simply cannot fully understand each other's minds**" — which directly explains why everyone in the drama conceals things from everyone else.

**Transferable techniques**
1. **One detonator plus six pre-existing lines**: do not invent conflicts for the illness; let the diagnosis ignite the want each person already had.
2. **Ban the name of the disease**: let nobody say the word, and the ladder of who knows first, who is kept from knowing and who pretends not to know will generate the tension by itself.
3. **Push the climax down a grade**: put the real detonation into a shabby meal, a cheap performance, or one line — "when will I ever get love like that?"

# Series Case Library: Episode Structure Tables, Beat Sheets and Source Examples

> Use alongside SKILL.md.

**Conventions**: quotations from English-language texts are kept verbatim, with a line of commentary underneath; quotations from Chinese, Japanese or Korean texts are given in English (Sakamoto Yuji's *I Fell in Love Like a Flower Bouquet* via the Lei Ke Chinese translation; Noh Hee-kyung's *The Most Beautiful Goodbye* via the Liu Yue translation of the novelization). Page references follow each set of notes: West Wing, Sopranos, Downton, Calvisi, Landau and Goldberg & Rabkin are PDF pages (`p00NN`); Fleabag is txt line numbers (`L`); Sakamoto is scene numbers; Noh Hee-kyung is PDF pages. **Wherever a script carries no act marks of its own and the division into sections or the mapping onto episodes was inferred by the analyst, the table says "inferred." Data the notes do not record is written "not recorded," and nothing is supplied on their behalf.**

## 1. The West Wing: Six Episodes' Act-Page Table and Act-Out List

Source: Aaron Sorkin, *The West Wing Script Book* (Newmarket, 2002), the shooting scripts of six teleplays credited solely to Sorkin. **The act boundaries are a fact on the page** (the scripts carry `TEASER` / `ACT ONE–FOUR` / `END OF SHOW`, scene numbers and page numbers) and need no inference. Page numbers are PDF pages (book page = PDF page − 10).

### 1.1 The Act-Page Table and Act-Out List

| Episode | Writer / director | Teaser | Act 1 | Act 2 | Act 3 | Act 4 | Total | Lines | Act outs (teaser / 1 / 2 / 3 / 4) and type |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Quantified patterns readable from these six:

- **Teasers run 2–8 pages, median 5**, inversely proportional to how much backstory the audience needs (the premise pilot's 8 is longest, the second half of the two-parter's 2 shortest).
- **Totals run 58–69 pages**, with S1 thicker than S2 (68 / 69 → 67 / 61 / 58 / 58).
- **Two act-length shapes**: front-loaded only in the Pilot (19 / 18 / 12 / 11); the other five back-loaded, with Act Four longest or second longest. Acts two and three compress most easily (17 People's 10 / 10), and **Act Four is never shorter than 11 pages.**
- **Four to seven lines, four standard**; the bottle episode has five and the finale seven.
- **The teaser out is `SMASH CUT TO: MAIN TITLES` 100% of the time**; every act out is `FADE OUT.` / `FADE TO BLACK` plus `END OF ACT N`. The format is fixed, and all the variation is in the content.
- **Every episode has at least one "procedural line"** (the Cuban rafts / the military strike / continuity of government / the FAA terror threat / the Haitian embassy): it carries no act out, and supplies interruptions, deadlines and the final moral data. The Pilot's Act Four raft numbers: 1,200 set out / 700 turned back / 350 missing / 137 detained.

### 1.2 The Eight Act-Out Forms and Where They Occur in This Book

| Form | Source |
|---|---|
| Suspense question | Pilot teaser, "Potus?"; 17 People teaser, "What's going on, Leo?" |
| Reversal line | A Proportional Response teaser, "Wow, are you stupid." |
| Emotional reveal | A Proportional Response Act Two (Charlie's mother killed on duty); Pilot Act One (Mandy's photograph) |
| Wordless action | Two Gunmen I Act Two (Donna sitting down); Two Gunmen II Act One (Josh pointing at his face through the glass) |
| Held prop | Two Cathedrals Act One (Mrs. Landingham's black hat); Two Gunmen II Act Three (the White House ID) |
| A repeated line rewritten in meaning | Two Gunmen II Act Two (Charlie's five "Okay."s plus Bartlet repeating "We don't know what the injury count is yet."); 17 People Act Three ("I feel fine by the way") |
| A severed setup | 17 People's episode button (the joke is never finished) |
| A planted gesture paid off | Two Cathedrals' episode button (the tell taught item by item by Delores in Act Three, paid off wordlessly in Act Four) |

### 1.3 Dialogue Examples

**Example 1 (S1E01 Pilot, Act One, p0033–p0037): an ensemble relay sentence**

```
TOBY  What to do when the Nina, the Pinta, and the Get Me the Hell
      Out Of Here hit Miami.
SAM   We can't send 'em back. They'll go to jail if they're lucky.
TOBY  We'll get whacked in--what--at least--
SAM   --three Congressional districts. Dade County--
TOBY  --those seats are gone.
JOSH  Not to mention the fact that it's wrong.
SAM   Plus that.
```

Commentary: one person starts and another finishes, with sentences cut into halves; the moral judgement comes after the political calculation and is pinned down by two words, "Plus that.", which completes the comedy and the position at once. The same scene's stage direction states, "Throughout this scene, LEO will drift in and out of participation... In other words, nothing going on here is an emergency."

**Example 2 (S1E01 Pilot, Act One, p0039–p0041): an unusual action instead of a line**

```
JOSH  And when was the last time you brought me a cup of coffee?
      (DONNA thinks...)
JOSH  It was never. You've never brought me a cup of coffee.
DONNA Well, if you're gonna make a big deal out of--
JOSH  Donna, if I get fired I get fired.
DONNA Do you think he's gonna do it?
JOSH  (beat) No.
```

Commentary: the first cup of coffee she has ever brought = "I am worried about you"; the concern is inferred by the audience and never said aloud.

**Example 3 (S1E03 Act Two, the Situation Room, p0113–p0115): the same question asked three times**

```
BARTLET      What is the virtue of a proportional response?
FITZWALLACE  I'm sorry?
BARTLET      What is the virtue of a proportional response? Why's it good?
             (An awkward silence... They're not geared up for these kinds
             of questions.)
BARTLET      They hit an airplane, so we hit a transmitter, right?
             ... They hit a barracks, we hit two transmitters.
FITZWALLACE  (pause) That's roughly it, yes sir.
```

Commentary: each repetition of the question carries more setup in front of it, so the military cannot answer; the answer is deferred to Act Three and given by Fitzwallace — "It isn't virtuous, Mr. President. It's all there is."

**Example 4 (S1E03 Act Two, p0110–p0111): returning the attack verbatim**

```
SAM   ...you don't have the guts or the strength or the courage to say
      "We know what's right from wrong and this is none of your damn business."
C.J.  Really.
SAM   Yes.
C.J.  Strength, guts or courage.
SAM   Yes.
C.J.  You just said three things that all mean the same thing.
```

Commentary: the argument closes at the rhetorical level — she does not rebut the content, she points out the flaw in his rhetoric.

**Example 5 (S2E02 Act Two, the pool, p0252–p0255): a recruitment scene in four steps**

```
TOBY  Jed Bartlet's very impressed with you. He likes the work you did
      with that girls' group with the stupid name.
C.J.  Emily's List?
...
C.J.  He's never heard of me, has he.
TOBY  No.
...
C.J.  How much does it pay?
TOBY  How much were you making before?
C.J.  550,000 a year.
TOBY  This pays six-hundred dollars a week.
C.J.  So this is less!
```

Commentary: flattery → puncture → a gap in the offer → the only real question ("Is Jed Bartlet a good man?"). The numbers themselves — $550,000 a year against $600 a week — are the character's decision.

**Example 6 (S2E18 Act Two, p0322–p0323): turning the secret into a countable list**

```
TOBY  How is it possible that this was kept a secret. First of all, who else knows?
LEO   You're the 16th person.
TOBY  Who else?
LEO   I'll tell you some of them, I won't tell you all of them.
...
TOBY  Nobody lied? Is that what you've been saying to yourself over and over again
      for a year?
```

Commentary: the number of people in the know, 15 → 16 → 17, is the audience's progress bar, and the episode title is that number (number 14 is Hoynes, number 15 the anaesthetist Dr. David Lee). Toby's correction in Act Four: "It's 17 people by the way. You knew. We weren't counting you."

**Example 7 (S2E22 Act Three, p0387–p0388): the grammar of a wordless act out has to be spoken aloud first**

```
DELORES  ...if you think we're right and you won't speak up 'cause you can't
         be bothered, then God, Jed, I don't even want to know you.
         (beat) Come on, I'll get you a triple-A.
JED      Miss Mueller only gets half as much to teach music as Mr. Ryan gets
         to coach crew?
DELORES  (pause) You're gonna do it.
JED      Now I didn't say that.
DELORES  Yes you did.
JED      When?
DELORES  Just then. You stuck your hands in your pockets, you looked away and
         smiled. That means you made up your mind.
...
DELORES  And looked away and smiled. We're in.
```

Commentary: the three constituent actions (hands in pockets / look away / smile) are named separately by a character, which is what makes Act Four's silent act out legible.

**Example 8 (S2E22 Act Four's button, p0411–p0412): cutting to black mid-action**

```
BARTLET   Yes. Sandy.
REPORTER  Mr. President, can you tell us right now if you'll be seeking a
          second term?
And BARTLET doesn't answer... the staff is getting curious...
BARTLET   (pause) I'm sorry, Sandy, there was a bit of noise there, could you
          repeat the question.
The staff is thinking "Please, God, come to me papa..."
SANDY     Can you tell us right now if you'll be seeking a second term?
Again BARTLET is silent...
LEO       (to TOBY) Watch this.
And BARTLET thinks... looks away... sticks his left hand in his pocket...
and just as he smiles we FADE TO BLACK.
```

Commentary: three layers — the character manufactures the delay himself, an insider cues the audience ("Watch this."), and the cut lands halfway through the completed action ("just as he smiles"). The answer is never spoken.

## 2. The Sopranos: Five Episodes' Structure Table (the sections inferred) and the Pilot's List of Blanks

Source: David Chase et al., *The Sopranos: Selected Scripts from Three Seasons* (Warner Books, 2002), five final shooting scripts plus Chase's foreword (p0009–p0012). **HBO scripts carry no ACT and no teaser marks. Every "section" in the table below is an inference judged from the therapy-room frame and the turns; the only facts on the page are the scene numbers, page counts and revision stages.**

### 2.1 The Five-Episode Table

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Pine Barrens (S3E11, S311, "formerly developed as S211") | Teleplay: Terence Winter; Story: Tim Van Patten & Terence Winter / Steve Buscemi | Production Draft 12/8/00 → 5th Revision (Goldenrod) 1/8/01 | sc. 1–71 / 64 pages | A the woods / B Gloria / C Meadow-Jackie Jr. / D the couples' therapy and the in-laws, plus the Slava runner | 3 (sc. 8 with both spouses, sc. 21, sc. 71) | "Tony's at a loss. Several beats, then he shakes his head. THE END" |

**The recipe from Chase's foreword (as written)**: "Each script has approximately **thirty-five beats**—**thirteen** or so in the A and B stories, usually **five to six** in the C story, and sometimes a D story with just a few beats." A and B carry equal weight, C is secondary and D is usually a comic runner. The process: list the beats line by line on a whiteboard → the assistant types them out in A/B/C columns → **cut them up with scissors and marry them into episode order with clear tape** (an A scene → a B scene → A → C…) → retype once satisfied, and that is the episode's outline. Freelance writers had to follow the outline strictly. The season's theme is not decided in advance: season one's "Tony and his incapacitated mother" only "organically emerged" on looking back at the season's end.

### 2.3 What the Pilot Establishes / What It Deliberately Refuses to Explain

**Established**

- The world: North Jersey; the waste business "was" the bread and butter; RICO and informants; "things are trending downward"; HMO fraud as a new revenue stream (p0049).
- The core family: Carmela (cash in the bean can, the AK-47, Father Phil, the "goomar"), Meadow (15, clever, lying, Aspen), AJ (13, the birthday).
- The work family: Christopher (desperate for recognition, reckless, wanting to sell his life to Hollywood), Big Pussy, Paulie, Silvio, Hesh (his father's Jewish adviser), Dick Barone; Artie and Charmaine as the mirror of the legitimate world.
- Therapy: Melfi's rule (she must report a murder, "Technically"), her curiosity and her fear, Tony's resistance (Gary Cooper) and dependence ("Talking helps").
- Livia: refusing every kindness, "girls take better care of their mothers than sons," reading concern as murder ("locked away in a nursing home I'll die faster").
- Junior: an uncle managed by his nephew, "Something may have to be done."
- The thematic images: the ducks (flying away = the family dispersing), the cathedral (something the ancestors built with their hands vs. today, when "I can't find two guys who can grout a tub"), the "sad clown."

**Deliberately not explained**

- Tony's formal position ("The guy who says how things go is who I think I am" — never boss or capo, with Jackie Aprile never appearing); how "waste management" actually works ("On this here also you don't need to know the details").
- The exact kinship between Christopher and Tony (he is called both nephew and "my cousin Anthony"); Christopher's drug habit is covered by one Paulie line.
- Who blew up Vesuvio — the audience sees Silvio walk away, Artie does not know, and **nobody in the show explains it.**
- Junior's "New York" structure ("He's got their okay on the hit"); how Tony's father died.
- What Junior and Livia's understanding will lead to — left entirely blank.
- How much Meadow actually knows (her "cynical look") — held for College.
- The two deleted scenes (Tony kissing Melfi, Meadow's fantasy of being burned at the stake) show that a pilot is also trial and error: a premature relationship advance and an over-stylized fantasy were both cut — **but the script collection prints both scenes**, so they can be compared.

### 2.4 Examples

**Example 1 (Pilot, p0025–p0026, sc. 18): establishing the patient–doctor complicity**

```
MELFI: ...if I was, for example, to hear that a... say a... murder?...
       was about to take place—
       ...I'm supposed to go to the authorities. Technically.
TONY (long beat): Oh.
MELFI: I don't know what happened with this Mahaffey fellow. I'm just saying.
TONY: I see. (beat) Nothing. We had coffee.
```

Commentary: the audience has just watched the hit-and-run and the beating; "We had coffee." is the irony formula of a double life, and every later therapy scene rests on that boundary between what can and cannot be said.

**Example 2 (Pilot, p0046, sc. 44): resistance and admission completed in one scene**

```
TONY: Whatever happened to Gary Cooper? The strong silent type. That was an
American. He wasn't in touch with his feelings. He just did what he had to
do... once they got Gary Cooper in touch with his feelings, they wouldn't be
able to shut him up! Dysfunction this! Dysfunction that!
MELFI: Do you feel depressed?
TONY: Since the ducks left, I guess.
```

Commentary: after a long anti-intellectual monologue, the most ordinary question knocks him back to himself; "since the ducks left" is the theme's first naming.

**Example 3 (College, p0091–p0092, sc. 3): denial and half-admission**

```
TONY: I'm in the waste management business. Everybody immediately assumes
you're mobbed up. It's a stereotype. And it's offensive.
...There is no mafia.
...Look, Mead'—you're right, you're almost a grown woman—some of my money,
yeah, comes from illegal gambling. And whatnot.
MEADOW: Well... at least you didn't keep denying it, Like Mom.
```

Commentary: three stages (the official line → flat denial → giving an inch); the price of the exchange is that the daughter then admits she has taken speed — a symmetrical structure of information exchange, and also the setup for the betrayal in sc. 60.

**Example 4 (College, p0123–p0124, sc. 37): the confessional device changes hands — the wife's confession replaces the therapy room**

```
CARMELA: I have forsaken what is right for what is easy. For a life of
comforts I have turned a deaf ear and blind eye, allowing what I know is
evil in my house... I have committed the sin of omission, because I wanted
things for them, a better life, good schools... I'm so ashamed.
...You know all about him Father Phil, you know! AND I'M THE SAME!
```

Commentary: this episode has no therapy scenes at all, and the confession (with the two of them seated back to back) carries the therapy room's entire function — the confessional device inside an engine can change person and change religion.

**Example 5 (College, p0137, sc. 59A): the execution**

```
PETERS: I could have killed you last night. Outside the motel. Your daughter
was drunk... I said to myself, 'He's just a dad this time.'
TONY: One thing about us wiseguys—the hustle never ends.
...Jimmy says hello from Hell.
```

Commentary: his fatherhood saved him the night before, and in this scene he voids it himself; after the strangling, "a few wild ducks in a V winging toward somewhere" — the Pilot's image recovered across episodes, wordlessly.

**Example 6 (The Happy Wanderer, p0173, sc. 13): giving in after three refusals**

```
TONY: David, don't say short if you don't mean short. All kidding aside.
      Do you understand what I'm sayin'?
DAVID: Of course. Hey, you don't have to explain business to me.
(sc. 14) TONY: Give him five boxes of ziti. (to David) Good luck.
```

Commentary: refusing three times lets the protagonist keep his moral covering, and when he agrees he uses a rule to transfer the responsibility to the victim.

**Example 7 (The Happy Wanderer, p0215–p0216, sc. 37): the B line's wish granted by the A line's disaster**

```
MALE VOICE: A program note—'Sun/Moon' to be performed by Eric Scatino and
Meadow Soprano has been canceled... Instead Miss Soprano will be singing
'My Heart Will Go On.'
CARMELA: Oh, that's a lucky break. I wonder what happened.
```

Commentary: the outcome is announced by the school's PA rather than by any character, and one ignorant "lucky break" completes the irony. The daughter gets the solo because the classmate's father has been devoured by hers.

**Example 8 (Pine Barrens, p0312–p0314, sc. 34–35): the chain of information decay**

```
TONY: He killed sixteen Chechen rebels single-handed!... He was with the
Interior Ministry. Guy's like a Russian green beret.
→ PAULIE: He killed sixteen Czechoslovakians. Guy was an interior decorator.
CHRISTOPHER: His house looked like shit.
```

Commentary: the key intelligence is corrupted through two relays plus phone noise (static, echo, crossed lines), and the characters act on the corrupted version — the comedy and the structural device of "everyone holds only half the information" are the same thing.

**Example 9 (Pine Barrens, p0343–p0345, sc. 69–71): an ending that does not answer**

```
TONY: You're a captain, what do you wanna do?
PAULIE: Fuck it, let's go home.
TONY: If this cocksucker ever crawls out from under a rock, this is your
      problem, not mine.
...(a long silence in the car) TONY: You got mayonnaise on your chin.
...(sc. 71) MELFI: And Irina before her? Depressive personality, unstable,
impossible to please... Does that remind you of any other woman?
```

Commentary: the Russian's fate is left unknown and the A line gives no answer; the episode's emotional landing is handed to one question in the therapy room.

## 3. Downton Abbey Season Two: a 19-Line × 9-Episode Grid and Fellowes's Rules

Source: Julian Fellowes, *Downton Abbey: The Complete Scripts, Season Two*, the complete shooting scripts with 419 author's footnotes, Ep1–8 plus the Christmas special (January 1920). **It carries ACT marks for ITV's commercial breaks, with varying act counts**: six in Ep1, four in Ep2–5, five in Ep6, five in Ep7, six in Ep8 and seven in the special. In this season Ep4 is co-written with Shelagh Stephenson and Ep6 with Tina Pepler.

**Scene counts (facts on the page)**: Ep1 Sc 1–71 / Ep2 1–64 / Ep3 1–73 / Ep4 1–60 / Ep5 1–61 / Ep6 1–64A / Ep7 1–56 / Ep8 1–77 / **the special 1–110.**

### 3.1 The Season's Line Grid

Key: **●** advanced this episode　**◎** turns this episode (the line changes direction)　**★** this episode is the line's high point　**✕** closes / the character leaves　**(blank)** not in this episode.

| # | Line (the characters bound to it) | E1 | E2 | E3 | E4 | E5 | E6 | E7 | E8 | CS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Mary–Matthew** (the season's main arc) | ● | ◎ the declaration abandoned | ● | ★ the singing | ★ paralysis plus "blub all you like" | ◎ feeling returns to his legs | ◎ he stands / a wedding date is announced | ★ the dance and the kiss | ★ the proposal in the snow ✕ |

How to read it: **lines running simultaneously** — ten in Ep1, twelve in Ep5, eleven in Ep6, eleven in Ep8, nine in the special. **Line lengths are tiered**: cross-season main lines (1, 2, 4) plus single-season arcs (6, 11, 13) plus guest lines of one to three episodes (16, 17, 19). Every episode carries at least one line entirely unconnected to the main crisis, to let the audience breathe (the Ethel line against the war in Ep3–5, the Bryant line against the influenza in Ep8). **The retention mechanism**: a popular regular never leaves, and anyone already pushed to the door is pulled back by "bankruptcy" (Thomas, Ep7), "a change of mind" (Carson, Ep7; Anna, CS) or "a commuted sentence" (Bates, CS); the ones who can actually be killed are the outsiders (Courtenay, Lang, William, Lavinia, Vera, Bryant).

**A note on currency**: this season's scripts were "too long," ITV allowed 70–75 minutes, which meant one more commercial break and complaints from viewers, and season three returned to a strict hour (the ITV schedule of 2011–12).

### 3.3 Fellowes's Rules (distilled from the 419 footnotes)

1. **"The Downton dilemma = both sides are right"** (the most frequent item in the footnotes): "a typical Downton argument, where hopefully you're on both sides." / "A Downton dilemma is when you see both sides." Applied to: Robert vs. Cora on whether to bring Matthew home; whether Clarkson should make an exception for the house; whether Ethel should hand over her child; whether Daisy should tell a dying man the truth; Strallan's self-veto vs. Edith's longing.
2. **No character is entirely unsympathetic**: "There are no entirely unsympathetic characters in Downton. But there is always a sub-text, or at least I try to suggest one." (O'Brien's atonement; Bryant "afraid of his own grief"; Carlisle, "he always tells the truth with Mary.")
3. **Open each episode with two or three lines restating the lines in play**: "never assume that they will remember every detail of an earlier episode, because they won't." (E.g. Ep7 Sc2–3 hooks two lines with "should we call him Mr or Captain now" and "any news about Bates?")
4. **The ensemble-scene specification (footnote 326)**: "the luncheons, the dinners, the drawing-room scenes… they always have to **advance about six or seven stories at the same time, often by no more than a line**. So the audience has to store it all and then put the plots together at the end."
5. **Let the audience know more than the characters**: "you engineer uncomfortable meetings, and then you let the audience fill in the detail." / "the audience always likes knowing things that the people on screen don't know."
6. **Once the audience knows, you need not show them**: Matthew is given no dialogue when he hears the truth; sex and violence are left to the audience's imagination, which "owes more to old Hollywood than modern television."
7. **Upstairs and downstairs emotional stories must carry equal weight**: "giving them both the same attention" (Ep5's Mary/Matthew paralleled with Daisy/William), with mirrored pairings so the same thing is said once in each register (Carson/Hughes against Violet/Isobel).
8. **Deliberately stage scenes between two people who would never otherwise meet**: "there is no greater distance at Downton than that between the Dowager Countess and Daisy" — for which a little institutional irregularity is permitted (making up the fire during the day is properly a footman's job).
9. **Staging techniques**: the whole company rising after dinner can recombine pairings; the running order of a shooting day (walking to the first drive, soup midway, lunch in the barn) "can bring characters together or push them apart"; and passage acting in the corridors runs several lines in parallel.
10. **The retention premise of a workplace drama**: "One of the basic questions of any drama is: why do these men and women stay?" — Downton's answer is that this is their livelihood, so every so often the audience must be reminded that this is a workplace with a budget, wages and ranks.
11. **"Truth reminders"**: plant, at intervals, a line of truth that punctures the warmth (the master does not know the kitchen maid's name: "Who is Daisy?"), so the ensemble does not become a love-in.
12. **The rules for killing a character**: "it wouldn't be right if no one from our running cast died" (footnote 120, discussed repeatedly by three producers); the one who dies must be familiar and loved — "It's like a whodunit. The killer has to be someone who was there all the time."; and **deliberately misdirect the audience toward Cora** (which also invites speculation about "finding Robert a new wife to produce a son").
13. **A season finale must give a reason to come back**: the last act is pure pay-off plus "streamers" — "we won't resolve this tonight, but if you keep watching, at some point we will."
14. **Judging when lovers unite**: "Any writer or producer of a series always dreads allowing lovers finally to come together… So you keep them apart and keep them apart, but… there comes a time when the audience thinks, oh, for Christ's sake, are they going to get together or not?"
15. **The logic drama requires outranks the logic of fact**: "drama requires a logic not always supplied by truth." — but history and medicine must be verified (a Decree Nisi revoked for collusion, spinal shock vs. transection, the brachial plexus, Spanish flu killing the young), and he prefers references that "look implausible and turn out to be exactly accurate." Footnote 306: Strallan's injury is engineered — it had to be serious but require no special effects (no missing limb, too expensive and troublesome), so a medical adviser supplied a brachial plexus injury; and that explanation was then cut, because it "undermined the truth of the situation and turned it into a sort of stage convention."
16. **Do not give period characters modern attitudes**: "It always slightly annoys me when characters in period drama, in order to make them sympathetic, are given modern attitudes and modern choices." (Sybil's views on chastity before marriage do not conflict with her politics.)
17. **Put it in without explaining it**: "the style of Downton… refer to people and events… without making a point of explaining them."
18. **Production reality rewrites the script**: rolling up Highclere's carpets requires the owner's approval; there is a ceiling on dining-room scenes (Carson and the footmen cannot stand that long); a convertible stage bedroom with no ceiling and movable walls is kept available (hence the overhead shots); actor availability → scenes cut or written out; and **when an actor plays it well enough, the dialogue is cut** (Thomas's motive, Jane's lines) — "A good actor's skill will always obviate some of the dialogue."
19. **Editing is a three-way consensus, and the author often loses and admits he was wrong**: "the consensus went against me… I think I was wrong and they were right." (ending on Vera's body); what goes is mostly expository dialogue and small actions adding "a new colour," and he records what was lost every time.
20. **American broadcast has no concept of a "special"**: the Christmas special had to "play either as the end of a series, or as a stand-alone."
21. **Write a character's opposition as an institution, not as malice**: Bryant demanding documentary evidence, an illegitimate child having no legal claim on the father, Carlisle buying off the scandal, the Decree Nisi revoked — the resistance is supplied by rules, and the antagonist is merely whoever knows the rules best.
22. **The joke and the exercise of power in the same line**: the Dowager's lines are frequently both a scene button and an exercise of power ("I hope it is not vulgar in me to suggest that you find some way to overcome your scruples." / "I have a cold.").

**Four act-out types (the season uses only these four)**: (a) someone making a request across a line (Carlisle bribing Anna); (b) a hidden fact stated in one sentence ("He is standing."); (c) a Dowager-style button ("Worse luck." / "I have a cold."); (d) a physical fact (a corpse, handcuffs, a breakdown into tears, going up to read the lesson).

### 3.4 Examples

**Example 1 (Ep5 Sc58): the Dowager button**

```
VIOLET: I have a cold.
```

Commentary: at the wedding she quietly wipes her eyes and Edith takes her arm, and she gives only those four words. Footnote 157 records that the source is a true story about the author's wife's grandmother, Lady Broome, and adds a judgement: crying at a funeral is "definitively middle class." One line is simultaneously a joke, a characterization, a statement of class and a scene button.

**Example 2 (Ep5 Sc41): a downstairs Downton dilemma — both sides are right**

```
DAISY: All the more reason. I can't lie to him at the end. Don't make me be
       false to a dying man.
MRS PATMORE: What matters now is the poor lad knows some peace and some
       happiness before he goes.
DAISY: I can't.
```

Commentary: one side is "I cannot lie to a dying man" and the other is "let his last days be peaceful" — the audience nods at both, and only then does the line qualify.

**Example 3 (Ep5 Sc52): the resistance is supplied by institutions**

```
VIOLET: Finally, I would point out your living is in Lord Grantham's gift,
your house is on Lord Grantham's land and the very flowers in your church
are from Lord Grantham's garden. I hope it is not vulgar in me to suggest
that you find some way to overcome your scruples.
```

Commentary: three concrete property rights (the living, the house, the flowers) come before the polite threat; the person applying pressure is merely the one who knows the rules best.

**Example 4 (Ep7 Sc22–23): an act out that states a hidden fact in one sentence**

```
LAVINIA: Heavens, that was a near thing.
She has brought her eyes up to Matthew. She stops.
LAVINIA (CONT'D): My God.
He is as stunned as she is. He is standing.
...(Sc23) ROBERT: Everyone, come at once! … Come and see this!
As Sybil and Edith also hurry down, he has run into the library, and now they
follow him, to find Matthew in his chair, which is a slight let-down.
```

Commentary: first the eyeline of the person seeing it, then the cause; the reveal is delivered by stage direction ("He is standing.") and no character explains a word of it. The ensemble reaction scene that follows first lets the family think it is something else (he is back in his chair, "a slight let-down") as a buffer.

**Example 5 (the Christmas special, Sc69 and Sc110): not letting the heroine romanticize her transgression / expressing forgiveness through "I have not forgiven you"**

```
MARY: It was lust, Matthew, or a need for excitement or something in him
that I — Oh, God, what difference does it make? I'm Tess of the
d'Urbervilles to your Angel Clare. I have fallen. I am impure.
MATTHEW: Don't joke. Don't make it little. Not when I'm trying to understand.
...(Sc110)
MATTHEW: I haven't forgiven you because I don't believe you need my
forgiveness. You've lived your life and I've lived mine. And now it's time
we lived them together.
MARY: You must say it properly. I won't answer unless you kneel down and
everything.
```

Commentary: what he has to forgive must be what actually happened, not the version he would rather hear; and after the largest possible gesture (the snow, the kneeling), the answer is compressed to its shortest.

## 4. Fleabag: the 12-Episode Table and Examples

Source: Phoebe Waller-Bridge, *Fleabag: The Scriptures* (Sceptre, 2019), the shooting scripts of 12 episodes across two seasons (about 25 minutes each), plus a short foreword and the closing essays "Who is Fleabag?" and "Post-script" (about 190 lines, sectioned by collaborator). The electronic edition has no page numbers, so locations use txt line numbers (`L`). **A half-hour carries no ACT marks — the "movements" in the table below are an inference by location and time block; the facts on the page are the fixed structural device and the roughly 2,900–3,600 lines per S1 script.**

The per-episode skeleton (strictly observed across both seasons): `cold open (1–3 minutes, carrying its own joke or its own mystery) → TITLES: FLEABAG → 3–7 movements → button (≤1 page, usually wordless)`. **The one exception is S2E6**, which replaces the titles with `TITLE CARD: THE WEDDING`.

### 4.2 The Two Seasons' Symmetrical Structure (a six-episode skeleton you can copy wholesale)

| Position | Series One | Series Two | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Episode 1 | Lays out every character plus the debt plus the mystery of Boo within one day (a typical-episode pilot, **not a premise pilot**) | One engagement dinner (a single location) lays out every character, the new characters and the season's suspense | The engine is displayed: **the web of relationships is itself the engine**, with no event required |
| Episode 2 | Recovering the ex (Harry) plus Hilary's arrival | The church / the café (two people alone) plus the counselling room | Converts episode one's emotion into a followable want |
| Episode 3 | A family occasion (Claire's birthday) plus the season arc's first card | A family occasion (an awards ceremony) plus the device being seen for the first time | Episode 3 plays the first card face up |
| Episode 4 | The bottle episode (the retreat: a closed space, silence enforced) | The bottle episode (the confessional: a closed space, a game of rules) | A two-hander deep dive away from the regular locations, with the episode's theme at its purest |
| Episode 5 | The false victory at its peak (the sisters' alliance, getting the money) | The false victory at its peak (sleeping together, reconciliation with the sister) | Gives "the best day," building potential energy for episode 6's collapse |
| Episode 6 | Recalls everyone into one space (the Sexhibition) plus the truth plus the camera chasing her | Recalls everyone into one space (the wedding) plus every object settled plus her dismissing the camera | The finale = one closed social occasion where every line closes |

Neither finale's button is plot: S1E6's is "People make mistakes." plus the pencil eraser; S2E6's is the fox, the statue and the wave. **The last image of a half-hour resolves the theme, not the plot.**

Four source lines from the closing essays (as writing tests):

- The moment the device passed its test (Vicky Jones, L43484): "When trying to crack the pilot episode, Vicky filmed me on her phone as I walked around my kitchen. I was making tea and experimenting talking to the camera. After five minutes she pulled the phone down and grinned: 'It works.'"
- Visual Sentences (Harry Bradbeer, L43507): "Harry described the image of Boo standing across the street with cars zooming past her. He said that was all we needed. … She has such a formidable armour of wit and self-awareness, but drop that in at any point – her laughing at a party, her during sex, her walking down the street feeling 'great' – and we'd know that she isn't OK."
- The one test for another season (L43511): "'If she has something else to learn, then we have more story to tell'. Harry was convinced that the greatest love story we had to tell was that between Fleabag and herself."
- The actors' veto and additions (Sian Clifford, L43537; Andrew Scott, L43562–43564): "There were a few times I tried to change the lines on set, and she would quietly stop me. 'It's good. Stop it.'" / "He even insisted on the character saying 'I love you too' to Fleabag at the end, and thank God he did."

**Example 1 (S1E4, the retreat, about L11900): a supporting character hands over a list of wants, and the protagonist answers in one line**

```
BANK MANAGER  They keep asking me. What do you want from this? What do you

BANK MANAGER  I want to move back home, I want to hug my wife, I want to
```

**Example 5 (S2E4, the confessional, L35811–36456): the show's only long monologue not addressed to camera**

```
FLEABAG  No, I want someone to tell me what to wear every morning … I want
         someone to tell me what to eat, what to like, what to hate, what to
         rage about, what to listen to, what band to like, what to buy tickets
         for, what to joke about, what not to joke about. I want someone to
         tell me what to believe in, who to vote for, who to love and how to …
         tell them.
         (beat)
         I just think I want someone to tell me how to live my life, Father,
         because so far, I think I've been getting it wrong. … Just fucking
         tell me what to do, Father.
Silence. She looks to the grate. She can't make out his expression. A long pause.
PRIEST (O.S.)  Kneel.
FLEABAG  What?
PRIEST (O.S.)  Kneel. … (gently) Just kneel.
```

Commentary: a list of twelve what/whos in parallel turns "I do not know how to live" into comic rhythm; the other party answers with a single word which is simultaneously a religious instruction and an erotic one; and the grille substitutes for a reaction shot. She cannot see his expression, and neither can the audience.

**Example 6 (S2E6, the bus-stop button, L43118–43434): a seven-beat anti-climax ending**

```
"You nailed it." → the display reads 46 minutes, "They always lie. It'll
magically come in a minute." → "It's God, isn't it?" "Yeah." → "That I
fucking love you. I love you." → "No no don't, let's just leave that out
there for a second on its own." → "I love you." → he takes her hand,
"It'll pass." → he leaves → "I love you too. (beat) Ok." → the bus display
reads Cancelled → the fox goes past → "He went that way." → she takes out
the statue → she gets up and walks, and the camera follows two steps → she
turns back and shakes her head almost imperceptibly, asking us not to follow
→ she looks back from a distance and waves → "Goodbye." / THE END
```

Commentary: the declaration is allowed three times (the second at her own request, to "leave that out there for a second on its own"), and the answer is two words; the ending completes the device's farewell not with a line but with **a shake of the head**, and the show's last line of dialogue is a stage direction written to the camera, "Goodbye."

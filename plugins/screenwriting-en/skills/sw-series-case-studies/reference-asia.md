## 9. Sakamoto Yuji, *I Fell in Love Like a Flower Bouquet*: Scene Statistics and a Five-Year Timeline

> **Note the medium: this is a feature screenplay** (released 29 January 2021, directed by Doi Nobuhiro, starring Suda Masaki and Arimura Kasumi; Chinese edition translated by Lei Ke, Guangxi Normal University Press / folio, December 2022, 35,000 characters of text). **There are no episodes, no acts and no act outs**, so it cannot serve as a model of series structure. The reason it is in this library is that the author is one of Japan's most important television serial writers (*Tokyo Love Story*, *Mother*, *The Best Divorce*, *Quartet*, *Oomameda Towako and Her Three Ex-Husbands*, *anone*, *First Love Devil*), and this is the most concentrated and dissectible sample of his "dialogue-driven relationship drama." The "invisible three acts" below are **an inference from proportions.**

Conventions: scene numbers (1–159) plus scene heading (location / time / "recollection") plus action description plus dialogue. The years divide it into chapters with cards reading "TITLE '2015' (handwritten)," seven title scenes in all: 1, 3, 76, 92, 112, 140, 153. Monologues are written `Kinu (monologue)` / `Mugi (monologue)` (voice-over). `*` is a section break within a scene.

### 9.1 Totals and the Longest Scenes

**Totals**: 159 scenes, **1,822 effective lines** of text (action description plus dialogue, excluding blank lines), 35,000 characters, about 124 minutes on screen — **an average of 11.5 lines a scene.** **Short scenes dominate: 72 scenes (45%) run ≤5 lines**; only 2 scenes in the whole film exceed 100 lines.

| 28 | Interior, the izakaya | **115** | The cultural checklist on the night they meet |
| 121 | April, the couple's room (night) | 71 | The big fight plus a proposal in anger |
| 52 | Interior, the chain family restaurant (night) | 54 | The confession |
| 103 | The couple's room (another night) | 45 | The first quarrel |

The conclusion: **all the weight rests on four or five long scenes (meeting, the confession, the first quarrel, the big fight, the break-up), and the other hundred-plus scenes are 3–10 line "diary entries"**; and the gaps between long scenes widen as the film goes on (28 → 52 → 103 → 121 → 147).

### 9.2 The Distribution Across the Year Chapters

| Chapter | Scenes | Count | Effective lines | Share |

| 2020 prologue | 1–2 | 2 | 38 | 2% |

**The first year of the relationship takes 42%, and the following four years take 42% between them**; 2019 has only 12 scenes yet 14% of the lines (almost all of them long scenes). **The distribution is itself the theme**: "remembering it in detail" has been written as form.

Diary precision decreases (= the freshness of the relationship): 2015 is accurate to the day (29 October, 1 November, 24 December, 29 December, New Year's Eve); 2016 keeps only 4/13 and 6/3; after 2017 only months; 2019 has just three markers. **Recollections are used only in the first quarter**: ten scenes in the film are marked "recollection," and **not one appears after scene 42.**

**The invisible three acts (inferred)**: Act One = January–March 2015 (scenes 4–57, meeting to the height of the affair, about a third of the film, with the diary at its densest); Act Two = June 2015 – July 2018 (scenes 58–124, cohabiting → employment → falling out of step, with the diary thinning year by year and the month markers growing sparser); Act Three = December 2018 – 2020 (scenes 125–159, Kaito's death → the break-up → afterwards).

### 9.4 Three Audio Tracks and Reused Locations

- `X (monologue)` = the voice-over diary track, **151 entries** in all (Kinu 77 / Mugi 74).
- `X's voice` = a real off-screen voice inside the scene (the love-making under the quilt, a phone call given only one side's picture, a replay in a dream).
- **A sound bridge crossing over**: scenes 133–137, where the two leads' respective "advisers" (Nana, Kaji) interleave by voice, weaving five short scenes into one passage of double counsel.

| Location | Appearances | The inversion of meaning between the first and last |
|---|---|---|
| The Tamagawa-side apartment, the couple's room | **25** | 66, "wow" on the balcony → 139, the "indescribable loneliness and emptiness" at dawn → 150, living together after the break-up → 152, taking down the curtains |

There are **32 `*` section breaks distributed across 17 scenes**; scene 28 uses 7 of them to break one night into 8 sections. **The dialogue budget**: the two leads have **83%** of all lines (Mugi 295 / Kinu 266); and the supporting character with the most lines in the film is **the "mirror couple" who appear only in the last scene (36 lines)**, seven times as many as the romantic rival Kaji (5).

### 9.5 Examples (in English rendering)

**Example 1 (scene 2): parallel dialogue — two tables speaking one passage between them**

```
MUGI  You can't split love in half.
KINU  Love is one each.
MUGI  One each. Those two really don't get it. Maybe I should go over and explain.
  Mugi and Kinu stand up at the same moment.
  They notice each other, their eyes meet, and in the end they both sit back down.
AKANE  Never mind them. Mugi, did I leave an earring at yours last night? It might
       be on your bed.
MUGI  I didn't see it… (a little absent)
TOMOKI  Kinu, my parents keep nagging me to bring you over and introduce you.
        What should I do?
KINU  Mm, right, I think… (a little absent)
  Kinu and Mugi each sit facing their companion, with their minds somewhere else.
```

Commentary: each of them finishes the other's sentence; the new partners' lines are all about practical matters (an earring, meeting the parents), setting off these two people's "cultural matters."

**Example 2 (scenes 47–52, excerpted): alternating countdowns in two monologues**

```
MUGI (monologue)  They say if you eat together three times without confessing,
                  you can only ever be friends.
KINU (monologue)  I'm starting to panic.
……
KINU (monologue)  Eight hours until the last train.
MUGI (monologue)  From now on I have to change the mood.
……
KINU (monologue)  Why does the conversation keep drifting further away?
MUGI (monologue)  Three hours until the last train.
……
MUGI (monologue)  No, no! It keeps drifting.
KINU (monologue)  Two hours left.
```

Commentary: the two monologue tracks share one clock; the suspense comes from both parties counting down without knowing the other is.

**Example 3 (scene 28): a list of proper nouns as a heartbeat**

```
  Each takes a paperback out of their bag and shyly exchanges it.
KINU  (opening Mugi's paperback) I've read nearly all of Homura Hiroshi.
MUGI  (opening Kinu's paperback) I've read nearly all of Nagashima Yu too.
      I never had money, so I always waited for the paperback.
KINU  Me too, or I borrowed them from the library.
MUGI  Which other writers do you like?
KINU  Nothing unusual, really. Ishii Shinji, Horie Toshiyuki, Shibasaki Tomoka,
      Oyamada Hiroko, Imamura Natsuko, Enjoe Toh. And of course Ogawa Yoko,
      Tawada Yoko, Maijo Otaro, Sato Aki.
  Mugi listens, nodding along. He notices a used cinema ticket stub tucked into
  Kinu's paperback — the film is Hill of Freedom.
MUGI  Ah, Yatagai-san, you…
  The stub Kinu has found is Venus in Fur.
KINU  Yamane-san, you use cinema tickets as bookmarks too.
MUGI  Yes. I'm a cinema-ticket bookmark person.
```

Commentary: the script explains none of the names; "nothing unusual, really" followed by ten writers is both self-deprecation and self-positioning; and it lands on an object (the ticket stub), which is stronger than the list.

**Example 4 (scenes 13 → 31 → 150): one line, three years, three meanings**

```
[January 2015, scene 13]
MUGI (monologue)  Rock beats scissors, scissors beat paper — those two I get.
                  Paper beats rock? Why? Paper would obviously tear.
[the same month, scene 31]
KINU  How can paper beat rock? It would just tear.
MUGI (monologue)  Now I know there's one other person who thinks the same thing.
MUGI  (quietly delighted) You're right, actually!
[May 2019, after the break-up, scene 150]
  Kinu and Mugi play rock-paper-scissors. Kinu throws rock; Mugi throws paper.
KINU  Why did you throw paper?
MUGI  (smiling) Because I'm a mature adult.
```

Commentary: the first time is loneliness, the second is being in step, the third is "I have accepted the rules" — and with no explanation at all, the audience completes the meaning. The same technique appears as "the line returned verbatim": in 2015 scene 65 Mugi curses the interviewer on Kinu's behalf with "however senior he is, a man like that wouldn't feel a thing even after reading Imamura Natsuko's *Picnic*"; in 2018 scene 121 Kinu says the same line back to Mugi, and Mugi can only answer, "I don't feel anything any more."

**Example 5 (scene 147): the core argument of the break-up — "lowering the floor"**

```
KINU  It's only because today was a happy day that you think that. It'll go back
      to normal soon.
MUGI  I don't mind going back to normal.
  Kinu shakes her head.
MUGI  Isn't that what married couples are, even when the feeling of being in love
      is gone… (realizing he has said the wrong thing)
KINU  ……
MUGI  (and yet going on) Plenty of people stay married, don't they? … You and I
      could too…
KINU  You want to lower the floor even further?
MUGI  ……
KINU  Lowering the floor of what you feel again and again, living together,
      telling yourself that's just how it is — do you really think that's good?
MUGI  Yes.
KINU  Really?
MUGI  If my feelings have cooled, doesn't that mean I'm ready to enter a calm
      married life?
```

Commentary: the film's longest scene (162 lines) has one argument — whether to lower the floor of what you feel; neither apologizes and there is no reconciliation. The same scene uses the 21-year-old couple at the next table (Rin and Wataru) as a mirroring interruption: "We finally met. / We finally met." — "Opposite them, Kinu and Mugi gaze at a past they can no longer reach."

**Example 6 (scenes 11 → 149): the epitaph completed by the other person**

```
[January 2015, scene 11, Kinu's monologue]
KINU (monologue)  At the 2014 World Cup, the hosts Brazil lost to Germany and
                  conceded seven… However bad I have it, I'm better off than the
                  Brazilians were then. Yes, I'm much happier. Much happier.
[February 2019, the night of the break-up, scene 149]
KINU  I always think — however bad I have it, it's not as bad as Brazil were then.
MUGI  Ah… do you know what the Brazil captain Júlio César said in the interview
      after the game?
KINU  No, I don't.
MUGI  After the worst defeat in their history he said, "The journey we have made
      until now was beautiful. We were only one step short."
  They turn the words over, then set off again, with quiet smiles on their faces.
```

Commentary: the heroine's private line of comfort is completed by the hero four years later; the film's verdict on the relationship is delegated to a quotation, and the characters never state it themselves.

**Example 7 (scenes 155, 159): the ending — a synchrony nobody sees**

```
[Scene 155, outside the café]
  Kinu and Tomoki come out. Mugi and Akane come out. They walk off in opposite
  directions.
  Kinu quietly raises a hand behind her back and waves.
  Mugi quietly raises a hand behind his back and waves.
  Neither sees the other's gesture; they just keep waving, walking away.
[Scene 159, the Waseda apartment, Mugi's room]
MUGI (monologue)  …I wanted the yakisoba bread from there, so I searched — and
                  six years later, the miracle happened again.
  On the computer screen, Street View. On an ordinary residential street near the
  Tamagawa-side apartment, a man and a woman are clearly visible, carrying a
  bouquet and a pack of toilet rolls.
  Their faces are blurred, but you can tell it is Kinu and Mugi.
  The two figures side by side slowly turn into Mugi's illustration.
  THE END
```

Commentary: the ending has no dialogue at all; two actions "only the audience sees" (waving behind their backs, the Street View photograph) complete the emotional settlement; and the title object (the bouquet) appears only at the last moment.

**The turning points are not events but acts of consideration** (the author's departure from convention): scene 85, "while drying her hair he says I've decided to look for a job"; scene 107, "he puts on headphones and says turn the sound up and play." The film has **no antagonist and no viable third party** (the rival Kaji has 5 lines, and no affair ever happens), and all the pressure comes from each lead's own kindness; **death is not a turning point** (Kaito dies at scene 125, and the decision to break up happens at a **wedding**, scene 142); and **neither quarrel has a scene of apology.**

## 10. Noh Hee-kyung, *The Most Beautiful Goodbye*: the Seven-Chapter Structure Table, a Four-Episode Mapping (inferred), and the Family Conflict Web

> **Note the text's identity**: what is in hand is the **novelization** of the Korean television writer Noh Hee-kyung's (노희경, *It's Okay, That's Love*, *The World They Live In*) drama of the same name (adapted by Lee Seong-suk, Chinese translation by Liu Yue, CITIC Press, April 2016, 244 pages of text; the copyright page reads "Copyright 2010 by NOH HEE KYONG"). **It is not a script**: no scene headings, no S# numbering, no action lines — it is third-person omniscient prose. **The book nowhere gives the broadcaster, the year or the episode count**, so any "mapping to N episodes" is an inference.

### 10.2 The Chapter-Epigraph Device (a directly reusable cold-open method)

Before the text of every chapter there is a full page printing **only dialogue and no narration at all** (p0008 / p0045 / p0079 / p0111 / p0139 / p0173 / p0219), always quoting **an exchange that will appear in full later in that chapter.**

| Chapter | Epigraph page | Epigraph (excerpt) | The scene it quotes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | p0008 | "I want to go home with you when I'm done today." "Why?" "Trouble passing water is never good…" "Just take something for it. Why do you have to go to a hospital?" | p0014 |
| 2 | p0045 | "We can't operate." "Why not?" "It's too late." "Is that any way for a doctor to talk, boy! Operate. … She hasn't even said it hurts." "It hurts. She's just bearing it. It obviously hurts." | p0066–p0068 |
| 3 | p0079 | "So what's wrong?" "Everything." "Nothing even hurts. How can everything be wrong?" "It's uterine cancer."… "…Doesn't it hurt?" "And if it hurts, will you hurt for me?" | p0092–p0094 |
| 4 | p0111 | "Yeon-su, your mother doesn't have long. She's going to die." "What are you saying? She's had the operation. Why would she die?" "…The operation didn't happen." "Then how long does she have?" | p0127–p0128 |
| 5 | p0139 | "Father, I can't just give up on Mum. … I want to do a child's duty, just once. Father, please…" | p0165 |
| 6 | p0173 | "Mum, die with me! How am I supposed to live after you're gone?…" "I shouldn't say this, but… Mum, when you're lucid, even if you have to bite your tongue, come and find me." | p0215 / p0218 |
| 7 | p0219 | "Jeong-su… even if you forget me, forget what your mother looked like… don't forget that your mother gave birth to you." "Yeon-su, Mum loves you, you know that? You are me…" "You come and find me quickly, don't leave me bored on my own. Husband, if you think I'm pretty, give me a kiss." "In-hee, thank you. Truly." | p0230–p0238 |

The pattern: ① the epigraph is **always dialogue, always between two or more people, always a refusal or a declaration**, and not one is lyrical; ② what it gives is the chapter's **value statement** rather than a plot hook — the audience already knows the ending, and the tension comes from "how will this line get said"; ③ chapter seven's epigraph is **four farewells to four different people juxtaposed**, effectively announcing in advance the four-scene structure of the final episode; ④ converted into a series device: 30 seconds at the head of each episode playing only a piece of dialogue from later in the episode (white on black, or a flash-forward), with no picture.

Chapter one's internal units (to demonstrate the level of detail, p0011–p0044): ① p0011–p0012, Grandmother shouting "aren't you going to feed me, you wretched girl" while Mum sits on the toilet in pain — **the show's first image: the protagonist in the lavatory, in pain, being abused**; ② p0013–p0015, Dad at the mirror (the lines on his forehead, half his hair gone, the private hospital lost eight years ago to a malpractice case), Grandmother dragging Mum's hair and throwing her to the floor while Dad glances, sighs and goes out; ③ p0016–p0018, the coloured-ball memory exercises → carrying Grandmother out to dry the soybean paste, singing a lullaby; ④ p0019–p0020, the contribution-club day as the one reason to go out, and the two wishes attached to the new Ilsan house; ⑤ p0020–p0028, **the way into Yeon-su's line** (running into Yeong-seok in the manager's office, the reconciliation on the stairs, the origin a year ago on the night of the lilacs, In-cheol witnessing it); ⑥ p0028–p0032, Mum collects ten million won; she is refused a bowl of udon by her daughter in the mall lobby because "I have to work"; ⑦ p0033–p0034, **the way into Geun-deok's line** (the sounds of domestic violence down the phone and "give me my money"); ⑧ p0035–p0037, Dr. Yun's ultrasound (the doctor's face changes and the patient notices nothing); walking home side by side with Dad on a rush-hour subway; ⑨ p0038–p0042, **the Act One out** (being told "it is malignant" in the little park by the car park, banging on the door for the keys, the light box reading "cancer cells blooming like flowers," the long self-accusation in the bar, weeping under a tree by the road); ⑩ p0043–p0044, juxtaposing the happiness of two people who do not know.

Time and season markers (directly usable as an episode clock for a series): it opens in **November** (p0057, "though it is already November"); the show's goal is to "**move into the new Ilsan house before deep winter arrives**" (p0019); and the in-story time from diagnosis to death is about **five or six weeks** (the announcement of "a month… two months" at p0128 → discharge → **the first snow** at p0170, "the first snow, a little later than usual" → dying on a clear morning at p0239, "bright sunlight filled the bedroom"). Jeong-su's **university results day** is a deliberately **unfulfilled** deadline ("can't Mum live until my university results come out?" p0163; "just over a month more to hold on" p0163; **Mum does not live to see the results**). **The soybean-paste crock is the second clock**: drying the paste in chapter one (p0018) → in chapter six, paste fermented for thirty-odd years has never grown white mould before (p0166–p0167) → Dad in the bar, "you'll never eat that paste again" (p0078) → and Dad's final list of when he will miss her includes "when I drink bad soybean soup / when I drink good soybean soup" (p0237).

One more pivot: **the patient learns it herself rather than being told** — at p0170–p0172 Mum infers the conclusion from **coughing blood and the mould in the crock** (a folk omen).

### 10.4 The Four-Episode Mapping (**everything below is inferred; the book carries no episode or act marks**)

The basis for the inference: ① the original drama was a four-episode short form (outside knowledge, not in the book); ② the seven chapters' value turns fall naturally on four break points; ③ each chapter's epigraph works exactly as an episode's cold open; ④ a Korean episode runs about 60–70 minutes, and the book's 244 pages divided in four gives about 61 pages an episode.

**Three things the mapping must move (still inference)**: ① **chapter six is too long and one or two units must be pushed into EP3** — move "Dad finds a job at the health centre" (p0194–p0196) and "father and daughter furnish the new house" (p0198–p0202) to the end of EP3, leaving EP4 with only the farewell; ② **Grandmother's "nailed door" and "smothering" scenes must be split across two episodes** (both are in chapter six, p0206–p0209 / p0212–p0215), because in one episode they cancel each other out — put the nailed door at the end of EP3 (the peak of Dad losing control) and the smothering at the start of EP4 (the peak of Mum overstepping); ③ **Yeon-su's affair must end in EP3**, or any want of her own in EP4 will dilute the farewell (which is exactly how the book handles it, with the break-up at p0168–p0170).

### 10.5 The Family Conflict Web

The configuration method in one sentence: **give every family member a problem already burning that has nothing whatever to do with the mother's illness, and then let the illness burn into all six at once.**

At the start six people each have an independent predicament, and **not one of them is caused by Mum's illness**: Dad is at odds with the young hospital director and about to retire (p0013); Yeon-su has resumed an affair with a married man (p0020); Jeong-su is waiting for results after three years of retaking exams (p0012); Geun-deok has gambled away the money for a car and beats his wife (p0033, p0056); Grandmother has dementia plus a stroke (p0011); and Mum only wants to move into the Ilsan house before winter (p0019). **The function of the illness is not to create conflict but to push every existing conflict to the point where it must be resolved**: Dad is dismissed (p0141), Yeon-su breaks it off (p0168), Jeong-su makes an impossible wish (p0163), Geun-deok starts driving a taxi (p0217), Grandmother is nailed into her room and then smothered (p0206, p0212), and Mum goes from cared-for to presiding (p0176–p0188).

### 10.6 How the Farewells Are Distributed (chapter seven, p0216–p0239)

| Order | To | Form | Length | Core action / prop |

Five rules of the distribution: ① **the longest scene goes to the most estranged person** (a wife who has not been cared for in thirty years and a husband who does not know how to care); ② **the shortest goes to the closest** (Grandmother's "I'm… going" / "Mm… go on" is two lines); ③ **one person must be absent** (Geun-deok), and that absent scene produces the biggest weeping of all; ④ **each scene must hand over an object**: a ring (the son), a kiss (the daughter), new clothes (the mother-in-law), a charge (the sister-in-law), walnut cakes (the brother, delivered in reverse), a "thank you" (the husband); ⑤ **the last line is not a dying wish but a thank-you, and it is spoken by the husband to the wife** ("In-hee, thank you, truly," p0238) — the deathbed line is given to the one who stays, not the one who goes. The death itself takes five lines (p0239).

```
"Jeong-su, even if you forget me, forget what your mother looked like, forget her
 smile… don't forget that your mother gave birth to you."
(Mum takes the ring off her hand and gives it to her son.) "Give this to your
 wife, one day."
"I'm giving it to you because I'm afraid you'll forget me. However hard I look,
 this is all I have to give you. I'm sorry."
(Jeong-su gets out of the car.)
"Yeon-su, I think Mum is about to lose consciousness. I keep drifting."
"Mum loves you very much, you know that?"
"Yes, I… love… Mum too."
"Mm, I love you. Very, very much."
"You… are me, and Mum is Yeon-su."
(Mum puts her arms around Yeon-su's neck from behind.) "My good daughter…"
(And kisses Yeon-su's cheek with lips wet with tears.)
```

Commentary: two farewells are completed back to back in one car, with extremely economical blocking — the son in the back seat, face to face; the daughter in the driver's seat, **with her back turned** (her face need never be shot). The son gets an object (the ring) plus a last instruction in the negative; the daughter gets a kiss plus an assertion of identity; and the son's lines are compressed into one repeated word — **the strongest emotion gets the fewest words.**

**Example 6 (p0237–p0239): the night in Ilsan — the last conversation and the death**

```
"You… you'll be all right without me?"… "Nobody nagging you any more. That's good,
isn't it?"
"Will you miss me?"
(Dad can no longer look at her; he only nods.)
"When? In what situations?"
"All of it."
"When is all of it?"
"When I'm knotting my tie before work." "And?" "When I drink bad soybean soup."
"And?" "When I drink good soybean soup." "And?"
"When I'm drinking, when I'm sobering up, when I'm going to sleep, when I want to
 be nagged at, when Mother is confused, when Yeon-su gets married, when Jeong-su
 goes to university and when he graduates, when we fry pancakes at New Year, when
 we make songpyeon at Chuseok, when I'm ill, when I'm lonely…"
"Come and find me soon. Don't leave me bored on my own."
"Husband, if you think I'm pretty, give me a kiss."
(Dad takes her face in both hands and kisses her deeply.)
"In-hee, thank you. Truly…"
—— The next morning —— "Wife." (The right arm he is holding has no warmth left in
it at all.)
"Wife…" "In-hee!"
```

Commentary: the question-and-answer loop of "will you miss me" → "all of it" → four repetitions of "and?" forces the declaration from one word into a list of twelve items, **every one of them a household task or a seasonal festival, not one of them abstract love**; the joking repetition "when I drink bad soybean soup / when I drink good soybean soup" closes the story's soybean-crock line; the last words are not "I love you" but "thank you," and they are spoken by the one who stays; and the death is one clause, "the right arm has no warmth left in it" — no breathing, no heartbeat, no doctor, no cries. The book's last image is "cold moisture had gathered at the corners of the eyes of the deeply sleeping mother" (p0239): **do not write the death, write the temperature and the humidity.**

The four rules as stated in the afterword (all from the author's afterword, p0240–p0253):

- **Law A**: both her parents died of cancer, and "death is not a frightening thing, it is the last chance to become beautiful."
- **Law B**: her mother died "without a smile and without a painful struggle, she went very peacefully"; "**our parting was neither beautiful nor sad.**"; "I am not grateful that you said you loved me before you died, because I have *always* known how much you loved your youngest daughter."
- **Law C**: "Before my mother died, I never thought she would become my regret. At the time, I was hers."
- **Law E**: the author puts her mother's telling of "the birth story" into the afterword, and her eldest sister then tells her it is not true; the conclusion is "**we simply cannot fully understand each other's minds**" — which directly explains why everyone in the drama conceals things from everyone else.

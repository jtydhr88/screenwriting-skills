## 5. Calvisi's Eight Minute-by-Minute Pilot Beat Sheets

Source: the CASE STUDIES in Dan Calvisi's *Story Maps: TV Drama*. **The methodological limits must be cited with them**: these beat sheets combine with each show's Basic Story Map to form eight Full Story Maps; "most have been **trimmed to focus on the 'signpost' beats** that form the bones of the narrative"; "All of these beat sheets have been made from the completed episodes, **transcribed as I watched them**, either on DVD or streaming"; "The numbers at the start of each beat represent the **time in minutes** the beat occurred, which correspond to the page numbers in a properly formatted script." (one minute ≈ one page); "if you see overlap in numbers, that means that a scene began within the same minute that the previous scene ended."

**So these eight tables are measurements of the cut episodes' structure, not facts of the scripts**; and in shows with no act marks, the act boundaries are the author's own **inference** — "I've inserted my own act breaks… in what feel like the natural positions."

**Three lateral rulers**: the midpoint almost always lands at **24–30 minutes**; the AOP always lands at **36–48 (mostly 37–40)**; and the DOW / All Is Lost always lands at **about 4/5 of the way through (43–52).**

| ACT FOUR | 50 | AFTERMATH: a CB broadcast is picked up by survivors in the woods; Shane is among them |

| | 54 | The car runs out of fuel; a couple at a country house who shot themselves; he finds a horse |
| | 57 | **NEW BEGINNING / DECLARATION OF WAR**: on horseback, entirely an old-West sheriff, he rides into the ghost city of Atlanta |

The author gives a three-part defence for "letting the audience invest in two characters and then losing them" (Morgan and Duane do not appear again in the rest of the season, and Morgan does not return until season three): they are the vehicle for the world's rules / **the protagonist is in every scene, so a B line is needed to let the audience breathe** / and they stand in for the separated family. The broadcast act-design principle: **every act brings a new movement and its own engine, closes clearly at the act's end, and leaves only the hint that there is more.** The typical mark of a premise pilot: the Week-to-Week **has not yet been established** in this episode.

The passage at the end of Act Three that the author quotes in full (noting that it states the theme and foreshadows at once):

```
MAESTER LUWIN: If this news is true and the Lannisters conspire against the
  throne, who but you can protect the throne?
CATELYN STARK: They murdered the last Hand, now you want Ned to take the job?
MAESTER LUWIN: The king rode for a month to ask Lord Stark's help. He's the
  only one he trusts. You swore the king an oath, my Lord.
CATELYN STARK: He spent half his life fighting Robert's wars. He owes him
  nothing. Your father and brother rode south once, on a king's demand.
MAESTER LUWIN: A different time. A different king.
```

Commentary: the author's note — "(This discussion touches on **the theme of the first season, the damage caused by the unquenchable thirst for power**, and it **foreshadows how Ned's sense of honor and tradition will do nothing to save him from treachery**.)"

How it violates the template (as the author lists it himself): ① **multi-protagonist**, with two of the three co-protagonists (Ned Stark, Jon Snow, Daenerys) passive, justified on the grounds that a pilot's job is world-building and that it sells "the promise of high adventure" rather than paying it off; ② **the inciting incident arrives late** (minute 18, in Act Two); ③ **the beats run once per line** (FIRST TRIAL / FIRST CASUALTY once on the A line at 25-28 / 30-31 and once on the B line at 33-35; three SHADOW SHOWDOWNs at 32, 38-39 and 41-43; two AOPs, neither belonging to the protagonist, at 40 on the C line and 46 on the D); ④ **act heads are marked by location TITLE cards** (Winterfell / King's Landing / Pentos, with cross-cutting starting in Act Three); ⑤ **Act Five has only two beats**, and the A line's "decision" happens off screen and is dispatched in one line; ⑥ extremely long plants: the dragon eggs planted at 53-54 and hatched at the season's end; Bran climbing the tower planted at 23-24 and paid at 58-60; Jaime and Ned's exchange planted at 46 and paid in episode 5. The letters follow screen time rather than order of appearance: Dany, who does not appear until Act Three, is the B line ("because she has the second most screen time behind Ned Stark").

Page-count norms (The Teleplay: Basic Guidelines; currency around 2013):

- Half-hour multi-camera sitcom: **double-spaced sitcom format, about 50 pages.**
- Half-hour single-camera comedy / dramedy: **single-spaced feature format, about 30 pages.**
- Hour drama: **48–63 pages**; pilots are often longer and trimmed later. Moonlighting, The West Wing and E.R., with their pace and machine-gun dialogue, can reach **85+ pages** and still cut into an hour slot.
- "Aaron Sorkin gets to break the rules… because he's won Emmys and Oscars and is a genius."

Two disagreements to keep side by side:

- Landau (the p0200 sidebar): "Writing a teleplay without an outline is like going on a road trip without a map… it's also **compulsory** (for the studio and network to sign off on the script). **No outline, no paycheck.**"
- Mazzara (an interview in the same book): "I don't use outlines… I can't shoot an outline… outlines take up a lot of valuable writing time and they are used to engage TV and studio executives… None of their notes are applicable to script." His substitute is **a two- or three-page prose story document for the network plus a beat sheet of his own with two or three words a beat** (e.g. "Rick kills Shane."). "All script problems, for me on The Walking Dead, come down to **structure problems**… And a beat sheet for me is a piece of scrap paper with ten words."
- Act count is a function of ad slots: The Walking Dead's pilot had no act breaks (standard script format) and later became teaser + 5 acts because AMC wanted another ad slot (it had been 4). Mazzara's positive view of act breaks: "those act breaks are an important part of the viewing process… It forces you to make choices. **It's about compression.**" And: "I do find that TV shows that embrace the TV form embrace the act break." — rebutting "It's not TV, it's HBO" with "No, it's TV and it's very well done."
- "No act breaks" does not mean no acts: the same book's body text notes that HBO's *Deadwood* scripts have **clear four acts** while Showtime's *Homeland* scripts do not; writing act breaks "both grabs the audience at the plot level and fits into a given time slot."

## 7. Miller: Two Itemized Escalation Tables and Four Student Pilot Outlines

Source: chapter 9 of Kam Miller's *The Hero Succeeds* (the five-part scene structure, adapted from ER / Third Watch writer and emergency physician Lance Gentile) plus the four physical pilot outlines in the appendix (the Outlines subsection, L9624–11367). **The two scenes' durations and beat counts are the author's own count while watching; where the six landmarks fall inside the outlines is an inference back from the model.**

The five components of scene structure (as written): **hero's want / obstacle / escalations / decision / resolution.** The definition of an escalation: "An escalation escalates; it increases friction, tension, flirtation—whatever's appropriate for the scene—and pushes the story forward." The want and the obstacle are fixed before the scene opens; "The first escalation begins the scene." On quantity: "You can have as few as three escalations in a scene before the hero makes a decision, but it's better to have more." Prefer action — "If all of your escalations are oral, your audience doesn't have to watch your show. **Your TV show turns into a radio show.**"

### 7.1 The Hannibal Pilot, the FBI Men's-Room Scene (from about 16:06, 1 min 57 sec, **14 escalations**)

The opening image sets the emotion first: Will submerges his face in a basin and blood seeps into the water; he lifts his head, dripping, and wipes his face — "His mind is flooded by the blood of the victim, Elise Nichols. It's confusing, obscuring, uncomfortable."

- **Hero's want**: Will wants to understand this killer who baffles him.
- **Obstacle**: Jack wants to know what Will knows **now.**

| # | Escalation |
|---|---|
| 1 | Jack wants to know what Will is doing in the bathroom (implying "you are wasting time") |
| 2 | Will tries to deflect with humour — "I like the smell of urine cake." |
| 3 | Jack swats the humour away and turns to shout at an agent who has come in and is undoing his belt, "Use the ladies' room!" — to let Will know how angry he is |
| 4 | Jack asks whether Will respects his own judgement (Will cannot say no) |
| 5 | Will says he does |
| 6 | Jack wants Will to know that with his gift they are likelier to catch the killer (subtext: use your damned gift, now) |
| 7 | Will half-apologetically admits he is working on it and is baffled |
| 8 | Will says he has never met a psychopath like this, has never read of one, and is not even sure the killer is a psychopath (**new information: the killer may not be a textbook case**) |
| 9 | Jack presses on what Will is withholding (because Will said putting Elise back in bed was "an apology") |
| 10 | Will soothes Jack: the killer wants to memorialize and honour the victim |
| 11 | Jack wants to know what kind of madness this is |
| 12 | Will gives Jack what he wants: the killer wants to prove to her that he loves her |
| 13 | Jack pushes back: meaning what? |
| 14 | Will asks Jack to stop pressing: the killer loves one of the victims and through her loves them all |
| 15 | Jack produces the evidence: there is no semen or saliva on the victims, and Elise Nichols was a virgin when she died |

**※ The book counts 14 escalations while the notes transcribe 15 items. Cite "14" as the book's figure, use the table above for the itemized list, and note the discrepancy rather than quietly merging entries.**

- **Decision (= the scene's climax)**: Will reacts — he **defends the killer as if he were the killer**, offended: "That isn't how the killer loves them. The killer kills them quickly and mercifully."
- **Resolution**: Jack believes him — "A sensitive psychopath"; Will knows the killer will take another girl soon, because he knows he will eventually be caught.
- **The emotional logic between scenes**: in the next scene Jack goes to Dr. Alana Blum to have Will psychologically evaluated (having just seen him break down and then break through); in the one after, Will is in the evidence room **trying not to look at the body** (want: to control his own emotions), because one look puts him inside the killer's mind. The author's comment: "The Jack-and-Alana-Blum scene and Will-in-the-crime-lab scene are perfect evolutions from the emotional logic and story logic of the men's restroom scene."

### 7.2 The How to Get Away with Murder Teaser's Woods Scene (about 31 seconds into the teaser, 1 min 40 sec, **11 escalations**)

- **Hero's want**: Wes wants to retrieve the body and bury it so they can escape blame.
- **Obstacle**: Connor and Michaela want to leave the body where it is (Laurel is on Wes's side).
- The principle for many people with many wants: **each scene still has only one hero**, and "Your hero may change from scene to scene" (in the men's room the hero is Will; in the Jack–Alana scene the hero is Jack).

| # | Escalation |
|---|---|
| 1 | Wes meets the other three in the woods carrying the weapon |
| 2 | Michaela wants Wes to put the weapon back |
| 3 | Laurel wants to wipe the weapon clean and, after burying the body, **hide it in plain sight** |
| 4 | Michaela wants to leave the body where it is |
| 5 | Connor agrees to retrieve the weapon but not the body |
| 6 | Laurel argues the body will get them caught and must be buried |
| 7 | Michaela wants a plan that does not require "carrying a body across campus on the busiest night of the year" (Middleton University is holding a frenzied pep rally for the Warriors football team tonight) |
| 8 | Connor points out that even if they could move it unseen, **the ground is frozen** |
| 9 | Laurel says they have **all night** to dig |
| 10 | Connor asks Laurel what she is talking about |
| 11 | Laurel concedes: this is murder, and **none of them knows what they are talking about** |

- **Decision**: Wes tells them to be quiet — it is 2–2, so they will flip a coin; if anyone has a better idea, say it now, and nobody does.
- **Resolution**: Wes flips the coin.
- The teaching point: every objection is **reasonable**, and the difficulty and the stakes are both conveyed inside the argument — "Peter Norwalk, the creator, is conveying **exposition through conflict**, which is one of the best, most compelling ways to do so."
- The test (repeated throughout the book): "If there isn't a strong character want, it's not a scene. **A moment without a character want is simply a moment, an image.**" How a one-person scene works: the opposing want comes from the world or the environment (the opening of Orange Is the New Black's "The Chickening": Piper's want is to enjoy her Sunday-morning ritual, and the obstacle is prison).
- The structural summary: "The spine of a scene's structure is the hero's want. The obstacle opposes or interferes with the hero's want… Each escalation makes it harder and harder for the hero to achieve his want until finally the hero makes a decision."

## 8. Goldberg & Rabkin's Physical Beat Sheets

Source: appendices D–I of Lee Goldberg & William Rabkin's *Successful Television Writing*: four physical beat sheets plus two leave-behinds, two of which print **the same episode's leave-behind and beat sheet side by side.** The header wording is the draft stage: `Story Area` → `Rough Beat Sheet` → `First Draft Beat Sheet` → `Revised Outline`. Two industry facts (currency: the late 1990s to early 2000s): **a beat sheet has no standard format and follows the showrunner's habits**; and "you get paid whether the script is shot or not!"

**The eight-act two-hour model (directly copyable)**: Act 1 = lay in three lines plus the murder (the murder at the end of Act One, not in a teaser) / Act 2 = **the false solution** (arrest the wrong person on a handsome medical or environmental clue, and let the protagonist look pleased with himself) / Act 3 = the investigation needed to overturn the false solution plus the subplots worsening / Act 4 = **the big cliffhanger: the protagonist discovers he has freed the real killer** / Act 5 = the real killer admits it to his face and the law cannot touch her (double jeopardy), and a new victim appears / Act 6 = the protagonist is attacked in turn (the killer using the same method) / Act 7 = a new chain of evidence assembled from rubbish, a yearbook and a lab test / Act 8 = **a trap set with the killer's own tool**, completing the arrest at the burial site.

**Symmetrical planting of clues**: the rose allergy (used in Act 2) / the tide and the surfers (background noise in Act 1, crucial in Act 4) / the morphing software (planted in Act 1, used in Act 8). **Every clue is written off as an irrelevant detail at its first appearance.**

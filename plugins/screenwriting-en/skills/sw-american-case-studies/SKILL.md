# American Case Studies (美国电影剧作案例)

This is an **exemplar library**: each case study gives a one-line premise, structural key points, and transferable craft techniques. Primary sources are Mei Feng, *The Screenwriter's Self-Study Course: Reading American Screenplays (Chinese original, no English edition)* (Western / Screwball Comedy / Wilder / Hitchcock / *Adaptation* / *Mildred Pierce* / five Oscar-winning original screenplays); Richard Walter, *Essentials of Screenwriting* (firsthand account of *American Graffiti*, the *Creature Comforts* exercise); Liu Dapeng, editor, *Story Creation Masterclass, Hollywood Volume (Chinese original, no English edition)* (*Bourne*, *Guardians of the Galaxy*, *Zootopia*, *Thor*, *Big Daddy*, *41 Days*); Julian Hoxter, *The Creative Screenwriter: 12 Rules to Follow and Break to Unlock Your Screenwriting Potential* (*Winter's Bone*, *Jojo Rabbit*); Blake Snyder, *Save the Cat!* (*Miss Congeniality*); Syd Field, *Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting* (*Chinatown*, *Network*); and Robert McKee, *Story* (*Casablanca*, *Chinatown*). For fundamental theory, consult the dedicated skills; this document keeps only "how these films were constructed." For further examples and breakdown sheets, see [reference.md](reference.md).

## 1. Genre Study: The Hollywood Western (Mei Feng)

- **Genre** = a body of standardized industrial products with distinct cultural characteristics; "genre is not a formula, but an archetype." Studying it means mapping a **system of conventions alongside a system of variations**; genres die for two reasons: failing to uphold their rules, or losing the pulse of their era.
- **Value Pairs**: Every genre is anchored by enduring value pairs—"the core questions remain unchanged; only the perspectives on those questions shift"; Western = civilization vs. savagery. Values must be translated into dramatic events, permeating character design, relationships, scenes, and plot points.
- **Close Reading of *The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance***: Dual protagonists Tom (the cowboy: "out here, a man settles his own problems") vs. Ransom (the Eastern lawyer); their opposition is restated repeatedly (physical strength, dress, attitudes toward firearms, attitudes toward law enforcement, romantic rivalry); the gun = a passport (learning to shoot = overcoming inner resistance; losing the gun = defeat); the supporting character Link acts as a catalyst, reappearing in pivotal scenes, essentially unchanged; opposing values can live within one character (Hallie's illiteracy); the restaurant scene cycles the same value pair through multiple shifts; every appearance of the cactus rose marks an essential thematic value; "characters in classic genre films carry stable value categories, but their specific value alignments shift continuously across the narrative"; the film's final thematic stance is decided by whichever value prevails at the ending (the community discards the authentic frontier lawman for the Eastern outsider).
- **Standard Flow**: The frontiersman (an outsider with a mysterious past and specialized skills) enters a community without acceptance → drawn in against his will (friends and family threatened) → villainous forces prove vastly superior → tries to avoid involvement but is pulled in → conflict concludes → accepted into society or departs. In the 1950s triptych (*Broken Arrow*, *High Noon*, *Johnny Guitar*), the journey starts inside the community, and **the townspeople themselves become the primary antagonist**.
- **Four Fundamental Oppositions**: Frontiersman vs. community; community vs. criminal forces; frontiersman vs. criminal forces; frontiersman vs. the external world—each pairing can push the protagonist in opposing directions.
- The Western hero evolves from classic hero → anti-hero → alternative hero: "standing before villainy, he embodies justice; standing before governmental order, he is an anarchist." The climax often hinges on a predicament where relationships with both factions turn tense at once; Native Americans evolved from formulaic villains to their portrayal in *Dances with Wolves*; female characters are the catalysts who "humanize" the frontiersman (agents of civilization / captives to be rescued / temptresses); the community shifts from an innocent frontier town to a hypocritical, corrupt, entrenched obstacle.
- The Dual Rider Paradigm (*Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid*): Moving from division toward unity, ending in joint resistance against an insurmountable obstacle; the star system ensures both characters are "already established in the cultural imagination before the story unfolds on screen."

## 2. Screwball Comedy Paradigm (Mei Feng)

- Born of the Great Depression, with *It Happened One Night* setting the benchmark; "a sex comedy devoid of overt sex."
- **Social Identity**: Dramatic tension is built on a battle of the sexes between figures of disparate social standing; narrative weight rests on scenes of sparring, barbs, and wits; the finale executes "the dissolution of class antagonisms alongside an ideological reconciliation"—to keep this reconciliation earned, both leads step outside their social classes (Peter loses his job, Ellie flees home); the true engine of their union is shared values (stubborn conviction, candor, love of liberty). Frank Capra's trilogy pivoted toward grass-roots populism; *Sullivan's Travels* compressed the gender clash to a minimum ("There's always a girl in the picture").
- **Characterization**: The female protagonist must be formidable and refuse to yield, never naive from start to finish (sometimes oscillating between virtuous and devious); the male lead is naive and at a disadvantage in romantic dynamics; **the father/elder figure is decisive**: the catalyst for conflict and the unraveling of complications are both negotiated through the father (Andrews shifts from opposition to urging Ellie to run from the altar; Harry: "Well, did she look pretty?"). A common shortfall across the genre—apart from *It Happened One Night*—is that the father's psychological pivot rarely achieves airtight logic.
- **Plot Points = Character Motivation**: Three motivational shifts in *It Happened One Night* (16 min: Peter decides to tag along; 29 min: Ellie agrees to travel together, erecting the Wall of Jericho; 75 min: shared transformation → misunderstanding; 94 min: Peter demands his thirty-six dollars and ninety cents from her father; the runaway bride climax). The universal attribute of classic Hollywood protagonists: **desire** (characters in European art cinema often lack clear awareness of their pursuits, which in mainstream Hollywood is an unforgivable technical flaw).
- **Dual Protagonist Formats**: Buddy mode (shared objective, effectively splitting a single protagonist into two complementary halves) vs. adversary mode (vast social differences, distinct personal burdens, forging intimacy across the journey).
- **Dialogue**: Extreme reliance on fast verbal delivery, loaded with veiled erotic Subtext, accelerated by rapid-fire editing; lines double as exposition and foreshadowing ("Washington and Valley Forge," "Napoleon and Josephine," "I need him like the ax needs the turkey").
- Key auteurs: Robert Riskin (collaborating with Capra for two decades); Preston Sturges (selling *The Great McGinty* in 1939 for one dollar to secure directorship).

## 3. Billy Wilder (Mei Feng)

- A childhood discovery on his doorstep—a letter revealing his father's illegitimate child, brushed aside with "it must have been delivered to the wrong address"—**sparked a lifelong fascination with human secrecy and deception across his whole body of work**; his early career as a journalist served as a lifelong reservoir of dramatic material; his chosen epitaph: "I'm a writer, but then, nobody's perfect."
- **Disguise** figures in nearly every film (a showgirl posing as a baroness, a twenty-four-year-old posing as a twelve-year-old, Neff concealing the truth from Keyes, Joe hiding being kept, Baxter feigning ignorance). **Disguise gives an omniscient audience dramatic pleasure in watching characters being duped; when the audience's knowledge diverges from multiple characters at once, dramatic possibilities multiply.** "A good screenwriter grabs the audience by the throat, chokes them tighter and tighter, and only lets go at the very end."
- **The Lubitsch Touch**: "Give the audience two and two, and let them add it up to four"—the sleeping car sequence in *Some Like It Hot*: the audience knows Jerry is a man (the first two), knows Sugar treats him as a female confidante (the second two), and deduces his excruciating inner torment unprompted.
- **The Ultimate Purpose of Disguise**: When the mask shatters, characters confront their authentic inner truth (*Love in the Afternoon*: "Throw her back in the river"; *Some Like It Hot*: Joe kissing Sugar while dressed as Josephine; Keyes: "the insurance investigator persona is an unwitting disguise," "closer than that," "I love you too").
- **The Meet-Cute**: The leads must never meet through ordinary circumstance; Wilder kept a dedicated notebook of inventive meet-cute premises (purchasing only the pajama top); complementary pairings (sheet music on the back staircase, lyrics on the front staircase), yet both leads remain in sharp opposition (*Midnight*'s bar sequence: "If you want peace of mind, get yourself a cab"—"No woman ever found peace in a taxicab"; *Ninotchka*: "I kissed that Polish cavalryman too—just before he died," sliding effortlessly into Act Two). Scripts tailored to their stars (Greta Garbo laughing; rewriting *Sabrina* when Cary Grant declined); the friction-laden chemistry between Jack Lemmon and Walter Matthau.
- **Realism**: "Seize a slice of reality like a newsreel documentary, then develop it dramaturgically"; Wilder studied human nature not as a European observing America, but from a universal vantage on the human condition: "People are rarely as good as they think they are, nor as cowardly as they fear."

## 4. Hitchcock and His Writers (Mei Feng Citing Leitch)

- Of fifty-three feature films, only ten were original screenplays; weeks of intensive, day-and-night brainstorming with writers preceded drafting; "design every single shot in advance—the moment the script is finished, the thrill of discovery is over."
- The economics of rights acquisitions (purchased for two hundred and fifty pounds, flipped for twice as much; using intermediaries to suppress bidding wars); did the true value of source material lie in the narrative itself, or in its potential to become "a Hitchcock picture"?
- Fraught credit arbitration (collaborative treatment outlines made individual contributions impossible to delineate; a preference for nurturing inexperienced, compliant writers over seasoned dramatists; Evan Hunter dismissed for refusing to write a rape sequence; Raymond Chandler's pages reputedly held at arm's length, nose pinched, before being tossed into the wastebasket).
- **"Every filmmaker is an adapter"**: Auteurship emerges as a collaborative ecosystem rather than the sole dominion of an autocratic director; Hitchcock's most enduring creative collaborator was his wife, Alma Reville ("working with two pairs of hands, one of which was Alma's")—"his auteur identity in the auteurist sense was inherently collaborative by nature."

## 5. *Adaptation* (Mei Feng Citing Tomasulo)

- Andre Bazin: "Adapt for cinema," not "adapt for the spectator." *The Orchid Thief* contained "no conventional plot engine, no dramatic crisis"; Charlie Kaufman fused his own neuroses with Susan Orlean's existential self-inquiry, writing his own creative paralysis into the screenplay.
- **McKee or Not McKee**: Charlie trashes McKee's commandments yet finally surrenders to them (genre hybridization; "can't you introduce an unexpected crisis?"; "you must leave the audience stunned in the final act"; "a protagonist without desire is impossible"); Act Three deliberately inserts gunfights, sex, illicit drugs, and trite life lessons—a meta-critique dramatizing how the screenwriter is forced to abandon his artistic purity and compromise step by step; yet defying the rule "do not use voiceover," the entire film relies on it.
- Charlie and Donald = the two halves of a single psyche (serious modernism vs. postmodern commercialism); Donald's script *The Three* comments directly on the movie itself; "in reality, this film possesses only one true protagonist."
- The Double Meaning of the Title: Adapting a book for the screen / biological adaptation and evolutionary survival (cross-pollination, wild orchids bound to the swamp, specialized pollination); using a line from page forty-one as the "book's ending" because the actual ending (retreating to civilization) left no room for a high-stakes car chase; the song "Happy Together" recurring three times; the blank wall vs. the kitchen window opened wide.

## 6. Eight Drafts of *Mildred Pierce* (Mei Feng Citing LaValley)

- Seven deviations from novel to screen: introduced murder; deployed flashbacks; compressed narrative chronology; shifted from third-person to first-person framing; excised Veda's opera career; heightened visual glamour; transformed episodic structure into "relentless momentum toward a decisive confrontation."
- **Producer Jerry Wald** drove creative direction (adopting *Double Indemnity*'s flashback format, injecting murder, shifting the climax to the homicide), for whom "realism" meant only provocative, boundary-pushing candor about sex and social taboos.
- Catherine Turney Draft: A domestic melodrama with three mother-daughter confrontations, emotionally plausible yet far from Wald's vision; **Albert Maltz's 62-page critique** raised three critical questions: is it too episodic and biographical? Is Veda believable enough that Mildred's blindness doesn't make her seem an idiot? Does their falling out stem from psychological collapse rather than blackmail letters?
- Margaret Gruen and William Faulkner Draft: Baroque indulgence and southern Gothic atmospherics (dark mascara rings, rat poison, a "house built on sand").
- **Ranald MacDougall** solved the fusion of woman's picture and film noir: murder in the present tense, domestic striving in flashback; a single setting (the beach house) staging both romantic swimming and lethal betrayal; the revolver carried from the office to the crime scene; dual thematic tracks (Wally's trembling seductions alongside parental ambition); defining the lead via supporting mirrors (Ida = pragmatic ideal and cautionary tale; Veda = repressed shadow; Wally = comic relief and spurned sexuality); radio drama craftsmanship—instant suspense, accelerated conflict, clear storytelling within strict running times; homage to *Citizen Kane* (circular narrative framing, the missing visual angle). "If Mildred embodies a new female ideal, she equally exposes its precarious vulnerability—destined to face retribution and rebuilding at the hands of the women within her own household."

## 7. *Thelma & Louise* (Mei Feng)

- **The "But" Method**: Two women set out for a weekend fishing trip, BUT an attempted rape forces a lethal shooting; they secure emergency cash, BUT a lover absconds with it; they decide to hold up a convenience store, BUT their path is blocked; surrounded by law enforcement, BUT they choose a defiant alternative exit. Plot points must sustain active dramatic momentum and yield irreversible functional shifts.
- Three Motivational Shifts (29 min: shared trajectory diverges; 70 min: "I heard the call—the call of the wild," realigning them; 96 min: past trauma is laid bare, launching the climax); from 70 to 96 minutes, threads split into fugitive escape vs. police pursuit, where detectives eating burgers while monitoring surveillance tapes cast women as passive spectacles of the male gaze; climax and resolution merge into a single beat (plunging into the Grand Canyon sidesteps the impossible legal realities established earlier).
- **Mirrored Dual Protagonists**: The opening cross-cuts packing luggage (meticulous order vs. chaotic impulse); as the journey unfolds, they gradually adopt each other's traits (Thelma supplants Louise to propose the final leap); physical motifs of transformation—cigarettes (Thelma exhaling her second cigarette mirroring Louise's gesture), the driver's seat (Thelma taking the wheel only twice), retrieving chocolate from the motel mini-bar three times (the third time refusing to put it back); details that "show without over-explaining"; Louise's Texas backstory is **acknowledged but never unpacked**, dramatized strictly through behavior and guarded dialogue.

## 8. *Fargo* (Mei Feng)

- **Character First**: Across the opening thirty-three minutes (before Marge enters), Jerry's economic trap and uneasy dynamic with the kidnappers are fully delineated through mundane behavioral sketches before characters precipitate narrative events; the state trooper's intervention is a coincidental Inciting Incident, plausible because character psychology is already firmly established; "dramatic contrivances do not happen to characters after the plot starts moving; rather, characters drive events out of fully realized temperaments." Jerry is tied continuously to automobiles (the automobile = the American Dream).
- Supporting characters with single-note eccentricities delight the audience, but a lead cannot rely on this device alone (the paradox of dramatic archetypes); Marge is the sole grounded presence (the Columbo archetype); the story ends with an ordinary, domestic exchange about postage stamps.
- **Revealing the Criminals Up Front**: Suspense pivots from a whodunit to "how rapidly the scheme will unravel"; the procedural detective work is handled with casual efficiency (never the core narrative focus).
- Quasi-documentary visual textures ("This is a true story" title card, regional location headings, Upper Midwest dialect, idiosyncratic speech ticks, static compositions, protracted long takes) set against an absurd narrative—"did not create a visual style but developed a new way of pairing"; the clash of deadpan humor and grotesque brutality (the wood chipper); high-density dialogue setting tonal rhythm rather than transmitting raw plot data; small mundane obstacles compounding into tragic absurdity; original sin tied to monetary greed, murder demanding retribution, grace reserved for moral decency; "compliant reformers working within the Hollywood system."

## 9. *Good Will Hunting* (Mei Feng)

- Kristin Thompson's Five-Part Structure: Setup (the inciting spark at the midpoint of Act One; the protagonist's goals dictated not by himself but by Professor Lambeau) → Complicating Action (half the running time explores Sean, sustaining the suspense of "how an unglamorous therapist can reach Will") → Development (**Sean reframes the central objective**: "You don't know what you want"; Chuckie's subplot monologue—"the ten seconds before I knock on your door"—is an advance rehearsal for the emotional climax) → Climax (**the true antagonist surfaces only here**—Lambeau exposed as an arrogant intellectual bully; the subplot is subtly referenced moments before the climax) → Epilogue (three distinct threads converge, each ending with Will out on the open highway).
- Actors Writing for Actors: Humor reliant on physicality and verbal cadence; the pivotal line "It's not your fault," repeated three times, requires performance delivery to carry the full tension of the climax.
- **Director Revisions Restoring Classic Conventions**: Cut Sean's expository intro and moved it to a classic "introducing the newcomer" beat; deleted the primary bar scene, replacing it with the supporting character's debut reading heavy books in his room; excised Chuckie's mother and the parish priest (extraneous realism); inserted b-roll cutaways of the commuter train; added the "picking Will up" scene to give Chuckie's monologue weight; added a single dialogue cue to synthesize two competing goals; adjusted depth of field to direct narrative focus. The tradeoff of this streamlining was "escapism" (only the genius from the gutter is deemed worthy of rescue); a three-way push-and-pull (writers, director, studio) forged the final film.

## 10. *American Beauty* (Mei Feng)

- "Look Closer"; the protagonist is a deceased narrator, opening with voiceover from beyond the grave (the screenplay's original courtroom frame was replaced with an omniscient bird's-eye perspective).
- **Point-of-View Strategy**: Camera framing builds a visual panopticon (high-angle shots, through window frames, car interiors, glowing computer monitors); the cheerleading performance—a zoom turns objective vantage into subjective reverie (overhead spotlight, high-speed photography, four zipper cutaways, crimson rose petals) before snapping back to reality; Carolyn denied solo compositions during group dynamics, blurred in shallow focus = visually erased; Ricky's camcorder perspective injects a sudden hush after frantic motion, using his lens to observe reality and lay bare hidden truths; the sequence where Ricky and Jane film each other across three screens; point of view carefully noted in the script.
- **Quasi-Multi-Protagonist Structure**: An ensemble network where every segment serves a precise dramatic function: charting Lester's spiritual awakening and demise (an umbrella-spoke architecture); the opening seven minutes set an elegiac tone, introducing each character's core affliction, which then ebbs and flows; three classic movements (numb awakening at 36 min / outward revolt at 84 min / transcendent peace); two contrasting dining room sequences (the script specifies "the camera moves in a slow circle around them"); cross-cutting at the finale misdirects the killer's identity, blunting simplistic moral judgment—"Lester, having unmoored himself from the collective unconscious, cannot ultimately be permitted to survive."
- **Genre Hybridization**: Domestic melodrama fused with pitch-black satire; neighboring scenes assembled strictly by contrast; within a single scene, contrasting necktie angles and shot scales; **the Discipline of Red**—crimson appears only in three strategic elements (the roses, the front door, the title graphics), rigorously excluded elsewhere to protect symbolic resonance.
- Studio Packaging: A five-million-dollar production grossing one hundred and thirty million; DreamWorks orchestrating a four-week, seven-hundred-and-seventy-thousand-dollar awards campaign.

## 11. *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* (Mei Feng)

- Opens with the death of an affair and ends where that same romance was born; the three-act paradigm stretched to **1 (18 min) : 5 (72 min) : 1 (17 min)**; the opening and closing segments form one continuous timeline, while the middle act unfolds as an inverted regression through the memory-erasure process; splitting the post-erasure reality between start and finish preserves the integrity of the psychological middle.
- Fractured chronology held together by recurring **visual motifs and emotional triggers** (the frozen Charles River, Montauk beach, the necklace, skeleton doodles, the crumbling beach cottage); the Montauk cottage sequence (87–92 min) is the true emotional climax, making their later real-world reunion an uneasy truce; Kaufman originally favored a bleaker ending showing Clementine undergoing erasures repeatedly over decades; the final shot repeats three times in loop-like cadence.
- **A Solitary Protagonist Acting Within the Confines of Consciousness**: His dramatic objective pivots at minute fifty-two (gazing at stars on the ice, ducking under blankets into childhood), locking in an active spine; repetitive memory sweeps are invigorated by the Lacuna tech crew subplots and varied visual metaphors for memory deletion; the introverted lead does not mutate through external plot shocks; his personality stays steady, wrestling mainly with his own emotional walls; voiceover mingles with direct dialogue as he addresses memories to rewrite them; supporting characters are streamlined so dramatic weight falls on Joel alone.
- Cinematic Texture: Expressive practical lighting, extreme shifts in shot scale, pre-lapped audio cues, dialogue hooks bridging cuts, hollow reverberations of metallic plumbing; "the most startling visual effects were executed entirely in-camera."

## 12. *American Graffiti* Experience (Walter)

- A 1971 phone call from Gary Kurtz offering a ten-thousand-dollar contract; an initial title hated by all; first draft knocked out in nine frantic days (street cruising vs. high-stakes drag racing; "the stark contradiction between aimless surface wandering and desperate internal searching"); George Lucas returned from travel without a word of feedback—"since then, I have become thoroughly seasoned in the gentle art of rejection"; agent advice: "nine days of work for seventy-five hundred in your pocket—take the check and move on"; a second draft was shelved; Walter failed to submit supporting materials for Writers Guild arbitration and received no screen credit; retained a 1.67 percent net profit stake yielding over a million dollars; later crowned a premier authority on coming-of-age scripts.
- Hard Lessons: Never write a solo first draft before the director returns to confer; guard your contractual terms while remembering "net profit" is usually phantom math; always file your paperwork during credit arbitrations; write without attachment to the outcome—write purely for the craft itself.

## 13. The *Creature Comforts* Concept Exercise (Walter)

- Checking into a hotel with no luggage → claiming "American Airlines lost it" → the fabricated suitcase actually arrives at the front desk—**what rests inside that luggage is the single most critical structural decision** (plane tickets and passports / skeleton keys / a corpse / black-market optical discs / poached rhino horns / vintage clothes / bulk condoms / narcotics "an absolute cliché to avoid" / an empty case / a locked box / a Twilight Zone metaphysical twist); Walter's chosen inventory: bespoke garments, a tape recorder, a .45 automatic, one million dollars in cash, a coded contract itinerary, and a manuscript titled *Creature Comforts* missing its final forty pages.
- Craft Mechanics: A character refuses an action → smash cut to the next scene showing him doing it; unifying a narrative through recurring textures (a Brooklyn accent, awkward social fumbling, vocal mannerisms); the Rule of the Cautious Protagonist (skips a shower but uses the toilet, inspects the weapon, handles the luggage cart); injecting minor friction into every scene (tight underwear, a Russian driver, bidding for subway tokens); omitting connective tissue (as in *Parenthood*, cutting straight to the collision); plausible improbabilities (a dropped recorder clicking into playback) without breaking the narrative spell; treating printed documents with visual shorthand—action lines, quick inserts, pithy dialogue; handling love scenes via discreet pans toward the ceiling; building the narrative backwards from the ending (a prisoner / the Son of Sam law / pseudonyms / identity theft / silencing loose ends); thematic identity—an imposed identity moving from fierce defiance to willing embrace.

## 14. Masterclass Case Studies (Hollywood Volume)

- ***The Bourne Identity*** (Banks's Parallel Arcs): The thematic question "Who am I?" is planted on page six; page seven introduces "Ivy League Ollie North" in four words; page ten introduces the film in his hip → launching the spine "Find Jason Bourne" (fusing theme and dramatic drive); page twenty-nine marks the Act One break at the embassy; page forty-five reveals the first shock: he is an assassin; pages sixty-five to sixty-seven feature the apartment shootout as a midpoint turning point (shifting from discovering identity to surviving pursuit); page eighty-one hits the low point; page eighty-four sees him altering his appearance, running from rather than chasing his past; pages ninety-three to ninety-five bring moral resistance; the concluding irony: surviving requires acting like the killer he despises. The litmus test: strip away the thematic layer—does the physical spine of action points still hold?
- ***Big Daddy***: The theme of adult responsibility; page one establishes "thirty-two years old and still living like a child"; page ten delivers the failed marriage proposal; page twenty-six relies on clumsy third-party exposition, an amateur misstep; page forty-four reinforces the theme when his ex-girlfriend pairs off with an older, dependable man; the sequence spanning pages twenty-six to forty-four could stand alone as a short film; the midpoint at page sixty-one pivots the protagonist's drive when he falls for Layla.
- ***Bruce Almighty*** / ***The American President***: When the dramatic spine is weak, the screenplay degrades into disconnected sketches; "the function of dramatic drive is to propel the narrative forward while imposing rigid narrative constraints."
- ***Zootopia***, ***Guardians of the Galaxy***, ***The Little Mermaid*** (Weiss's Relationship-Driven Arcs): Opening hook → page ten setup → page thirty Act One turn (the authentic core concept takes over); the Act Two dramatic question ("Can they crack Zootopia's greatest mystery?" / "Can they save their people?" / "Can they keep the orb out of Ronan's hands?"); obstacles between pages thirty and sixty use world-specific weaponry (sea urchins); minute forty-five introduces secondary threads (the claw-marked vehicle, night howler flowers); the midpoint bottoms out in a savage interpersonal clash (Nick laying bare his childhood trauma; "killing me won't solve your problem"); "audiences connect more deeply with the evolution of a relationship than with the solitary transformation of a single character."
- ***Thor*** (Stentz): Lock in wardrobe logic early (the ceremonial helmet reserved for coronation rituals); eliminate the alter-ego Dr. Donald Blake (keeping the name as an Easter egg on a fake passport); "don't over-explain world-building mechanics" (never justify how the hammer flies); "your ancestors called it magic, you call it science" knits the mythology into a sci-fi universe in one line; bridge the cosmic and the earthly (strip his godly powers, exile him to Earth to learn humility and sacrifice); substitute S.H.I.E.L.D. to delay the brotherly conflict; frame the first installment as Shakespearean tragedy, saving space-opera comedy for the third; Loki "always believes he is the good guy."
- ***41 Days*** (Richards): Single-location claustrophobia drawing on *Buried* and *127 Hours*; the opening ten pages cross-cut between a pizza parlor, a victim's face lit by matches, and a holding room to dismantle the expectation of "just a single pipe"; minute three unleashes a shootout; minute four uses a match cut on a face to pivot into backstory; minute eleven locks the protagonist inside the conduit; the tactical importance of the overcoat; the lead remains mostly reactive, with genuine transformation arriving long after escape; using a large four-track corkboard; bookending the narrative with matching structural visuals.
- ***Winter's Bone*** (Hoxter): An extended Debate beat (facing rejection across a succession of neighboring homesteads); the rhythm of cause and effect—painstaking efforts often yield nothing, and lucky breaks emerge without direct exposition. For the nine-beat breakdown of ***Jojo Rabbit***, see `sw-story-structure`.
- ***Miss Congeniality*** (Snyder's beat-by-beat Save the Cat! breakdown); ***Chinatown***'s opening ten pages (Field) and Act Two climax (McKee); the linen merchant scene in ***Casablanca*** (McKee)—see the reference materials in `sw-story-structure` and `sw-scene-craft`.

## I. Three Motivation Shifts in *It Happened One Night* (Minutes)

| Minute | Event | Motivation Shift |
|---|---|---|
| 16 | Peter discovers Ellie's identity and decides to exploit the scoop by following her all the way | Male First |
| 29 | Ellie is coerced and enticed into agreeing to share a room and travel together to New York; the Wall of Jericho is erected | Female First |
| 75 | Ellie confesses her feelings; Peter drives overnight back to the newspaper office to get his advance and propose (Central Turning Point) | Mutual Transformation |
| — | Misunderstanding: Peter does not return in time; Ellie is evicted from the room, appeals to her father, and agrees to marry King | |
| 94 | Peter visits Ellie's house to see her father and ask for the $36.90 he spent on her—the father recognizes his decency and genuine love | Male Third |
| Ending | At the wedding, the father convinces her to run away | Female Third |

The father Andrew's shift lacks logic; the writer depicts the aviator King as a scheming cad, and Andrew opposes him from the start, ensuring consistency of motivation.

## II. Opposition Matrix in *The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance*

| Dimension | Tom (The Cowboy) | Ransom (The Lawyer) |
|---|---|---|
| Physical Strength | Lifts him with ease | Is lifted |
| Attire | Cowboy hat, hip holster; later black suit | Tailored suit; later white apron |
| Attitude Toward Guns | "Out here, a man settles his own problems" | "I don't need a gun, but I'm going to put him in jail" |
| Attitude Toward Marshal Link | Banters and mocks | Respects |
| Romantic Rival | Hallie behaves coldly toward him | Hallie is attentive |

The meaning of the gun: learning to use a gun = overcoming inner obstacles; losing the gun = defeat (in *Unforgiven*, the Schofield Kid's poor eyesight, Ned unable to aim—"the saddest thing for a cowboy"). The supporting character Link is gluttonous and cowardly to serve comedic sequences, reflecting the director's attitude toward ordinary Western folk.

## III. Wilder Dialogue Examples

**The sleeper berth in *Some Like It Hot***: Jerry self-hypnotizes: "I'm a girl, I'm a girl"; Sugar climbs up in the middle of the night: "Thank you for saving my life, real sisters are supposed to help each other out," "If there is anything I can do to help," "There is plenty you can do to help me"; "When I was little I used to share a bed with my sister, we'd crawl under the sheets and pretend we were lost in a cave trying to find a way out," "You're trembling all over," "How could that be," "Your head is burning hot too," "Your feet are freezing," "Let me warm them up for you" ... "I'm a sick girl," "Not that kind of sick," "Have some whiskey"—hunting for paper cups, "Turn on the light," "No, I don't want anyone to know we're having a party," "I'll spill the liquor," "Then spill it, the more you spill the merrier, maybe we can make it a surprise party," "What surprise?" "It's not time to tell you yet."

**The bar in *Midnight***: "They're beating up the guy who hailed the cab," "Because he doesn't need a cab," "Then why did he hail one?" "Because I paid him five francs"; "I need forty francs a day, I earn forty francs a day, double on rainy days ... If you want peace of mind, get yourself a cab," "No woman ever found peace in a cab, I'm looking for a limousine," "They drive the same," "That's better than the subway"; "I wish you'd thrown the money in her face!" "How could I throw away a whole handful of money?"; "That's what you get for wanting something for nothing," "You can't just fall into a cornucopia, you have to jump into it yourself," "Now you're the one talking like a fool."

**The traffic island in *Ninotchka***: "How long must we wait here?" "Until the policeman blows his whistle again," "What is the interval between whistles?" "I never thought about it before ... I've spent a great deal of my life waiting for whistles," "In other words, you don't know?"; "How long does it take for a person to jump down?" "Last time I jumped, I forgot to time it"; "I am only interested in the shortest distance between two points. Must you flirt?" "Not necessarily, but it feels more natural"; "I kissed that Polish cavalryman too—right before he died."

**Keyes in *Double Indemnity***: "A claims investigator is a surgeon, the desk is an operating table, the pencil is a scalpel and a bone chisel ... He can be a doctor, detective, policeman, judge, jury, and father confessor all rolled into one"; "I didn't pick you because you're smart, but because you're less stupid than the rest of the company"; "No one has ever committed suicide by jumping off the back of a train going fifteen miles an hour"; "Closer than that," "I love you too."

## IV. Eight-Draft Script Timeline of *Mildred Pierce*

| Date | Draft | Characteristics |

| Spring–Summer 1944 | Turney 1st and 2nd drafts | Realist domestic melodrama; three mother-daughter confrontations; 147 pages stopping at two-thirds of the novel; no film noir style |

## VI. *Good Will Hunting* Cut and Add Comparison

| Original Scene | Director Treatment | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| South Boston L Street Bar & Grill (pp. 70–72, Will joking with friends) | Cut; replaced with Will's first entrance reading thick book in his room | Blocks out non-mainstream elements, highlights genius |
| South Boston rooftop (pp. 73–74, Sean exposition) | Moved after Lambeau suggests bringing Sean in | "Triggers perspective shift or introduces new entrance" |
| Chuckie's apartment/street (pp. 121–123, Chuckie with Skylar, mother, priest) | Cut | Omits realist preoccupation; Sean is more vital |
| Trolley cutaways | Added | Expresses mood |
| Chuckie picking up Will (pp. 103–104) | Multiple scenes added | Gives weight to the parking lot monologue |
| "If we talk about your life, who you are, then I'm fascinated" | Single line added | Merges two goals into one |
| First meeting between Sean and Will | Focus kept on Sean, Will turned away | Lowers identification with protagonist, elevates supporting character |

Climactic clash of values: Lambeau: "I am where I am today because I was pushed, because I learned to push myself!"—Sean: "He's not you!"

## X. *Creature Comforts* Suitcase Option Pros and Cons

| Option | Pros | Cons / Caveats |
|---|---|---|
| Airline ticket and passport | Advances plot instantly (to Paris); passport name/photo reaches back into past | |
| Keys | Finding the lock becomes the next destination | |
| Corpse or body parts | Propels to climax instantly, romance flips instantly to murder | "Creative works saturated with sadistic deviants" |
| Photos / CDs | Owner must become real in the story; CD must inevitably be played at the end | |
| Rhino horn / tiger penis | Smuggled exotic treasure con | |
| Clothing (wedding gown / diving gear) | Delayed explanation (wedding album); finally worn to infiltrate quarry | Clumsy scattered dialogue exposition |
| Condoms / fingernails | Directly connects to grave themes; DNA testing | |
| Drugs | | "Avoid at all costs" |
| Empty suitcase | Luggage itself is the theme (cart smuggling) | |
| Cannot be opened | Metaphor; opened by another character | |
| *Twilight Zone* approach | Changes every time it opens (Herb is a writer making it up) | Must obey logic, cannot be "woke up and it was all a dream" |

Author's version: luxury clothing, tape recorder, .45 pistol, one million dollars, contract itinerary, manuscript missing forty pages titled *Creature Comforts*. Reverse-engineered ending: convict author—Berkowitz ("Son of Sam") law—pseudonym Peggy Turner—publisher exploitation—impostor silenced—warden's accent—theme of identity.

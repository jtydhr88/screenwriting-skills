# The Writers' Room and Production Reality

Television is not written by one person and handed in. It is **a group of people in a room producing one episode a week on a fixed schedule and a fixed budget**, and that production process rewrites your script back at you. This skill covers: how the room works, what documents are delivered at what length and in what order, how a story gets "cracked" collectively, how to take notes, how to climb the ladder, and how to rewrite when money, actors, weather or locations veto you — without destroying the episode.

Schedules, physical documents and interview quotations are in [reference.md](reference.md) in the same directory.

Three premises (consensus across the books): ① **"the entire ladder of television is built on writers' titles"** (Douglas, chapter 5), fundamentally unlike film, where "creative authority belongs to the director and financiers can buy titles" (Blum chapter 19) — learning to write series television is learning to be a writer-producer. ② **"An episode is a product made every week in a specified number of days for a certain price and delivered at a guaranteed running time on an inflexible deadline."** (Goldberg & Rabkin, chapter 1) ③ **How you perform in the room matters more than your ideas, and even more than the script you eventually write** — "crafting stories is much harder than actually writing scripts" (Goldberg).

**Not included**: act structure and page anchors, season shape (→ `sw-series-structure`); series design, engine, bible and pitch-document fields (→ `sw-series-engine-bible`); joke mechanics and comedy-room punch-up (→ `sw-sitcom-comedy`). **The general treatment of American contracts, options, credit arbitration and the agency system is already in `sw-industry-business` sections 5 and 6; this skill covers only the step deal and staff pay specific to series. The mainland-Chinese production chain, filing and censorship, the three stages of creation and the episode-outline system are in `sw-chinese-series-practice`.** General dialogue, scene and character method is in `sw-dialogue`, `sw-scene-craft` and `sw-character-conflict`.

**[reference.md](reference.md) holds the physical documents, schedules and long cases**: the master table of seven ways to break story (§1), Prebble's four room rules (§2), leave-behind specifications in the flesh (§3), outline format and the hard annotations (§4), the 14-day method (§5), coloured pages and cut-outs (§6), the rules for picking a show to spec (§7), main-title shot-by-shot analysis and the stasis question (§8), the full etiquette of a live pitch (§9), four practical pieces on notes (§10), the rank table and the step deal (§11), the seven deadly sins in full (§12), the ledger of locations and budget (§13), bottle episodes (§14), actors (§15), alts and salvaging material (§16), the episode that cannot be saved (§17), a comparison table of five processes (§18), Wells's ER room transcribed plus Souders's and West's rooms (§19), a four-book comparison of ranks and money (§20), the multi-camera week and the freelance timetable (§21), the professional six-week schedule and the 14-day method (§22), six physical beat sheets and leave-behinds (§23), Fellowes's 419 footnotes distilled into rules (§24), and the chain of cut-scene migration in Succession (§25).

## Sources and How They Relate

- **Goldberg & Rabkin, *Successful Television Writing* (Wiley, 2003), gives the skeleton**: the two authors went from a Spenser spec to showrunning Diagnosis Murder and Martial Law; the book is a process manual for "writing, selling and rewriting one episode of an existing series," with real leave-behinds and beat sheets in the appendices. Currency: "one-hour drama is always four acts" and the $27,000 figure are 2003 data.
- **Douglas, *Writing the TV Drama Series* (3rd ed. 2011), gives the physics of the room and the ladder**: chapter 5 is the only treatment that writes the room **as a place with office locations, a culture and a politics**; chapter 4 gives the outline format and the 14-day method; chapters 6–7 give the spec rules and fourteen years of follow-up interviews.
- **Landau, *The TV Showrunner's Roadmap* 1e (2014) / 2e (2022), gives first-hand craft decisions from contemporary showrunners**, and is also **the source of most of the disagreements** in this skill (Lindelof vs. Mazzara, Hillian vs. Levinson).
- **Smith, *Writing Television Sitcoms*, chapters 14–19, gives live detail on pitch meetings and the roundtable**; **Blum, chapters 18–19, gives the 1999–2001 market structure and the working habits of four showrunners** (the numbers are dated, the structural judgements hold).
- **Script collections and annotations give "what actually happened"**: Chase's Sopranos foreword (35 beats, scissors and tape), Armstrong's and Prebble's Succession forewords and footnotes (the mega-chart, the "maybe" culture, alts, the freebie take, writing long), Sorkin's West Wing prefaces (the budget forcing a bottle episode), Fellowes's Downton footnotes (editing by committee, cutting dialogue when an actor plays it well), Waller-Bridge's Fleabag commentary (a single author's substitute for a room).
- **Miller, *The Hero Succeeds*, chapters 9–10, gives draft discipline and two rounds of readers**: the draft name = the draft number, warm read / cold read, three rules for filtering notes.

## 1. What a Writers' Room Is: the Showrunner's Duties and Powers

**The definition** (Armstrong, Succession S4 foreword): a showrunner is "essentially a writer. But **a writer empowered to protect the scripts through the rooms where they are conceived and written and rewritten, performed, captured and edited**." — those four verbs are the scope of the job.

- **Make a decision and communicate it** (Goldberg chapter 15): "there's really only one skill an executive producer needs to succeed: he has to be able to **make a decision and communicate it**." The counter-example is Ferd: today "a serial killer is killing nuns," tomorrow "nuns are killing a serial killer," wipe the board and start again; because he would not decide, scripts, casting and scouting all stalled, money burned daily, and it did not survive a year.
- **Be the show's single voice** (Mazzara, Landau 1e Tip 13): "I have several scripts this year that I've written every word of and **have not put my name on them**" — if an episode does not come out in that voice, producers, actors and crew all feel it is wrong.
- **Get the director a preppable script on day one of prep** (Hart Hanson, Bones, Landau 1e Tip 17): "**I think that's the first responsibility**" — you cannot have a director spend two or three days working off an outline that may still change.
- **Hold final cut**: Robert King (Landau 1e): a "TV showrunner has final cut… twenty-two to twenty-three little movies every year." Chase's division of labour (Sopranos foreword): the editor cuts first → the director's cut (changing shots but generally not reordering or deleting scenes) → the showrunner takes it through several versions to final, **with the power to reorder and to cut scenes.**
- **Do not hand the crucial things to a committee** (Bays, HIMYM, Landau 1e Tip 21): the room rewrites heavily, "but that mostly just applies to **the comedy**. **The dramatic moments I try to not mess with or punch up in the room. Drama needs to feel personal, and can't be written by committee.**"
- **Once you are up there the job is mostly management**: Goldberg — "**80 percent of the job is management**"; Souders, showrunning Smallville (Douglas 3rd ed., chapter 7, 2011) — **"writing is now 15% of my job"**, the rest being budget, management and endless phone calls: "I miss it. That's what I got into this for."
- **Set the room's atmosphere** (Prebble, Succession S3 foreword): Armstrong compares running a room to **throwing a party** — "you're responsible for the guests and the atmosphere and ensuring people want to come."

**"Of all the EPs, only one is the showrunner."** The executive producers in the end credits are three kinds of people (Douglas): the one running physical production / the **head writer (running content, including directing, casting and editing)** / an honorary title for a star. The way to tell is **to see who makes the key decisions**: the ER pilot was written by Michael Crichton, who carried an EP credit all season without working on the show, and "only one person — John Wells — made the key decisions"; the reverse case is an action show whose four EPs "could write a script fine but could not run a show at all," where the person actually running it was a semi-retired veteran credited as **Creative Consultant.** → **The first thing to do on joining is to find out who is really running the show, and report to that person.**

**Your own identity in the room**: Goldberg's staff philosophy — "**It's your show. Tell us what you want, and we will give it to you.**"; Wendy West (Dexter co-EP, 2011) — defensiveness comes from wanting to protect your own voice, "**and you are not on a show to protect your voice** … **When you're in this room it's not about your voice. Make your showrunner look like a star.**"; with the companion "chameleon" rule, "you have to adapt to the style of the show you're writing" (but "That doesn't mean losing your voice").

## 2. Breaking Story: How a Room Cracks an Episode

Goldberg calls it the **story meeting**, or **"cracking the story"**: first a loose discussion of the roads the story could take, then, once there is consensus, moving to the **big whiteboard**, beat by beat, scene by scene, act by act — "this lively and arduous give-and-take that is **the soul of television writing**" (chapter 11). Seven real methods; **pick one and do not mix them.** Full processes and schedules are in reference.md §1 and §2.

The master comparison table of the seven methods (source, one-line method, and the quantitative anchors of headcount / weeks / beat count) is in reference.md §1; Prebble's four room rules (the pitch opener, two ordering rules, the "maybe" culture, and "the room's output is what this show is *not*") are in §2. **The physical configuration of the ER room (ten chairs, a whiteboard for 12 episodes, the four headings Big / Serious / Humorous / Other), the six-week June season lay-in and the eight-week single-episode timetable, a step-by-step transcript of one real notes meeting, plus Smallville's anonymous script readings and Dexter's February camp → reference.md §19.**

**The strict definition of the worst case** (Douglas chapter 4, the item most often got wrong): **the worst thing is "what opposes the protagonist's goal / the antagonist's victory," not a generically bad event** — a doctor fears his wife no longer loves him, has a patient to operate on and it is raining outside; the worst case is his wife refusing him, **not** the patient dying or a flood. The four accompanying adjudication rules (the student Sonny's list): **every beat is an action** (a character "realizing" something is not a scene); **the antagonist must be as strong and as motivated as the protagonist**; aim for **a nearly balanced moral equation**; **anchor the worst case three-quarters in.** And one more: **when you read the cards, watch them like a screen — if you are bored, so is the audience.**

**The dissent: refuse the outline, refuse A/B/C** (Mazzara, The Walking Dead, Landau 1e Tip 13). "I don't use outlines… **I can't shoot an outline**… they are used to engage TV and studio executives… None of their notes are applicable to script." The substitute: **a two- or three-page story document for the platform** (in prose, saying what this episode has to achieve and what the main set piece is); for himself, only a **beat sheet of two or three words a beat** ("Rick kills Shane."). He does **not** use A/B/C either — "That doesn't always equal a theme. It just pushes the ball further on each one." — asking instead **"What is this episode about?"**; the cost, which he concedes, is that **minor characters may stay minor forever.** On the same side: Gilligan (Breaking Bad), six people in the room all day, no A/B/C, episode by episode "brick by brick," with the mantra "Where is Walt's head at? What does he want right now? What is he afraid of?"
→ **How to choose**: if it must be approved by the platform, or the line producer needs it to scout and budget, or you are a freelancer or a newcomer → **you must write an outline**; if you are the showrunner and can carry structure yourself → a story document plus a ten-word beat sheet will do, **but that is a by-product of power, not methodological superiority.** Multi-line ensemble with lines that must interweave → A/B/C plus coloured cards; a single-lead pressure drama → the one question, "what is this episode about."

**General room rules (consensus across the books)**
- **Sprint to the act outs and the ending first, then go back and make it good** (J.J. Abrams, quoted in Douglas chapter 5): "get to the act breaks and the end quickly, so you can reverse and make it better."
- **Solve one problem at a time** (Matt Williams, Smith): never mind the Act Two blow, change gear when you get there; the young writer's disease is to "chase everything in the script at once."
- **Your job is not to identify the problem, it is to offer something better** (Gurvitz, Smith): "your job is to **pitch a better joke or story fix**." **The dissent, preserved**: Kalish quoting Norman Lear — "**Don't be afraid to put your finger on something that's wrong, even if you don't know how to fix it.**" → The middle way: say plainly "I have no fix, but I do not believe this," and take responsibility for the next ten minutes of discussion.
- **Stop after the same proposal is rejected three times** (Sandy Frank, Smith); worse still is pitching it down the ladder (EP → co-EP → supervising producer → story editor) until only the junior writers agree with you.
- **Put the showrunner in the position of asking for help** (Craig Thomas, Landau 1e Tip 21): "I'm trying to figure out the story and these guys are helping me do it" works better than "What do we as a hive mind think this story should be?" **A room needs different kinds of people** (Lloyd): "encourage quieter, more thoughtful writers to come forward, and sometimes need to **butterfly net** the more aggressive ones."
- **The best episodes come from real events** (Bays & Thomas): "The best episodes **always** come from real stories. Like, 100 percent of the time, without fail." **An institutional prohibition can rescue a room that has gone off** (Johannessen taking over Dexter season 5): he instituted a **no-metaphors rule** — "Because everyone came up with metaphors and **nobody came up with an actual story**."
- **Price every change** (Miller chapter 9, quoting room practice): "**What does this change buy us?** … If it costs us more than it buys us, we think of another direction."

## 5. Taking and Giving Notes

**5.1 Seven rules for taking notes** (Goldberg chapter 13): ① **The most important thing is to listen. Do not argue, do not explain, do not defend** — "we think it's a cliché and we want you to change it. **Say 'got it' and move on.**" ② **But nodding is not enough**: if a note would create more problems than it solves, **ask what problem the note is trying to fix** ("everyone giving a note is trying to help, they just usually give you a solution instead of the problem"). ③ **Distinguish two kinds of note**: **overall** (tone, style, characterization, structure — the most serious) and **line/page**; overall notes divide further into those that give a road map and **"the killer"** (which says only that they do not like it and offers no fix) — with the latter, **do not just write it down and leave**, you must throw out a suggestion on the spot to start a discussion: "it's in no one's best interest to let you leave the room without an idea for a fix." ④ **Do all the line notes**, do not pick and choose (whatever annoyed them in the first draft will annoy them more on a second sighting). ⑤ **Write the notes down** — writers who say they have a good memory almost never include the notes they were given in the rewrite. ⑥ **Got a note you hate? "Do the notes. And do them well."** There is exactly one reason: **"Whether you do it or not, the note is going to get done."** ⑦ **Leave your ego outside the room** — Hasburgh's response to the first draft of seaQuest was "Hated it. Hated it. Hated it."; the author and his partner vented for a night, then opened the next day with "**We know you hate the script. So forget it. Don't bother giving us notes on this draft**," dropped the script in the bin, and said "let's go back to the outline and figure out how to make this a script you'll love" — turning it into an efficient working meeting.

> **First, what is actually useful in this section.** "Dare to refuse a wrong note" needs no skill to teach — when someone says "cut this character," not complying and offering an alternative is common sense. The value here is entirely in **wording and mechanism**: diagnosing "this line is diffuse" as "the orchestration is homogeneous" (so you know to change the units of exchange rather than cut people), translating "we cannot shoot this" into "this draft advances by changing location," and the ordering rule below. **Use it to argue with, not to steel yourself with.**
>
> **The ordering of ⑥ against ② / 5.3 / diagnostic 26 (this rule is added by this pack; Goldberg does not supply it).** On their face, ⑥ ("do even the note you hate, and do it well") and ② ("if it will create more problems, first ask what it is trying to fix") contradict each other. Draw the line by each one's own example:
> - **⑥ governs notes you merely dislike** — differences of taste, feeling it is corny, feeling it is not elevated enough. Always do these, and do them well. The reason still stands: "whether you do it or not, the note is going to get done."
> - **② / 5.3 / diagnostic 26 govern notes that will break something else** — notes that violate settled conditions (the character list, what has already aired, the episode count, the budget), or that would create more problems than they solve. **Do not execute these literally**: translate the "solution" you were handed back into the problem, then offer an alternative that solves the same problem.
> - **The test is one question**: if you do not comply, whose taste goes unsatisfied; if you do comply, what structural thing breaks? **If the latter has an answer, go with ②.**
> - Either way, **what you do in the room is identical**: no arguing, no explaining, say "got it," write it down. The divergence happens after you leave, not at the table.

The telephone code table (what "Nice work" / "Really good first draft" / "Thanks for getting it done so fast" actually mean) → reference.md §10.

**5.2 Use a recorder, not a laptop** (Douglas chapter 5, three reasons): ① you are not fast enough to catch everything, and cannot tell which of several contradictory opinions is worth keeping; ② what you type on a laptop under pressure "will have all the detail and nuance of a tweet"; ③ **with your head in the screen you are not in the discussion**, and you are permanently a beat behind. (Smith likewise advises a small recorder — "after you sell an idea, the producer may dictate a five-page story outline.")

- **5.3 Metabolize: digest before executing** — Le Bureau's six pages of notes and Éric Rochant's four-step handling process → reference.md §10.
- **5.4 Find your own readers first: warm read → cold read** — how to use the two kinds of reader, the question you must ask, the three rules for filtering notes, reading aloud, and fixing one line in isolation → reference.md §10.

## 7. Production Reality as a Creative Trigger

The core principle in one sentence (Goldberg chapter 16): **"Know what your script is about and you'll know what you can lose, and what you have to keep."** The long cases are in reference.md §13–17.

- **7.1 Everything costs money, and locations are the first thing changed**: the line-item ledger of turning a cat into a dog, the page economics of shooting 7–12 pages a day and the losses to company moves, and the case where West Wing's "The Drop-In" made it work → reference.md §13.

**7.2 Translate the "solution" back into the problem (the most transferable item here).** When you hear "move scenes 18 and 34 onto standing sets and cut the car chase," **go and ask what problem they are facing.** The truth may be: the standing sets are eight pages over schedule, and the stunt is $5,000 over budget. So you offer a better solution: move that four-page walk-and-talk at Fred's Bar onto a standing set (gaining a whole extra stage day and saving a location fee); keep the chase, and cut two one-line day players plus the Act One bar fight (the bar is gone anyway), which covers the $5,000. **"They won't object, as long as you come up with a better one."** The accompanying posture: **do not go into bunker mentality** — "If you try to hold on to everything you wrote, you'll lose it all."

**7.3 The bottle episode forced by budget**: the deficit-financing model behind West Wing's "17 People," Warner's five prohibitions, Sorkin's "I got to write a play" in the original wording, and how the six-night teaser is written → reference.md §14.

→ **When you are told "this episode cannot cost anything," do not think "which scenes do I cut"; think "which two characters have a confrontation that should have happened long ago, and which secret in the season arc should now move one step forward."** The accompanying data: that episode is completed in one night in one building; its teaser is only 6 pages yet spans **six nights** (using `TITLE: The Same Night / Two Nights Later / …` plus the recurring sound of a ball bouncing off a wall as the transition, each segment keeping only one or two exchanges) — **the cheapest way to write a teaser: one room, one motif of action, six escalating repetitions.** Same family: the Sopranos' "Pine Barrens." Where a bottle episode sits in the season shape is in `sw-series-structure`.

- **7.4 Actors: vetoes, availability, bodies — and writing for actors**: the three rewrites of "Sammo Blammo," the parameters in the Diagnosis Murder writers' guidelines, and the Fleabag and Succession cases → reference.md §15; **the 22 rules distilled from Fellowes's 419 footnotes, and the decision type each belongs to (editing trade-offs / historical research / rewrites caused by actors / production cost), are in §24.**
- **7.5 Alts, the freebie take, and the economics of "writing long"**: Armstrong's full list of seven reasons the script differs from the finished episode, plus the record of recycled cut material → reference.md §16; **the migration chains of cut scenes in the Succession footnotes (S2E10 → S3E1 / S3E2, the dimmed planting in S3E3, the S3E8 nanny line, the S3E9 Marcia–Shiv scene) are in §25.**

| # | Disagreement | Side A | Side B | How to choose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Whether to write an outline | Landau 1e Tip 13: **"No outline, no paycheck."** | Mazzara: no outline, just a 2–3 page story document plus a ten-word beat sheet | Needs approval / needs budgeting / you are new → write it. You are the showrunner and can carry structure → you may skip it, but that is **a by-product of power** |
| 2 | A/B/C lines | Chase, Douglas and Landau all use them | Mazzara and Gilligan refuse, asking only "what is this episode about" | Multi-line ensemble → A/B/C; single-lead pressure drama → the one question (cost: minor characters stay minor forever, as Mazzara concedes) |
| 3 | Whether to know the finale before the season starts | Armstrong walks into the room with a clear season end; West: "We know what's in episode twelve" | Gould has no master plan; Feldman did not know Steve would die at the end of S1 (it was pitched by a co-EP in the room's first month) | Highly serialized, endgame-shaped → fix the finale first; a relationship show where actor chemistry is the asset → allow it to arrive later |
| 4 | Should a newcomer speak up | Lapiduss: "almost better to kind of not speak up too much"; Konner: "**You haven't yet earned the right to show disrespect**" | Kalish quoting Norman Lear: "**Don't be afraid to put your finger on something that's wrong, even if you don't know how to fix it**" | **Read the showrunner's signals**; the middle way is to say "I do not believe this" while taking responsibility for the discussion — never report a problem without engaging |
| 5 | Do not spec the show you plan to pitch | Douglas rule 2: do not (an outsider does not know the show's minefields) | Her own "fairy tale": breaking in by repeatedly sending specs to the same show | Normally A; B holds only with the stamina to trade three scripts for one note |
| 6 | How many to pitch and how long each | Goldberg: 3 developed plus half a dozen half-formed | Smith: 10–15 springboards; Blum: 3–5 sentences each, six at most | Take the number from Smith, the length from Blum, the delivery from Goldberg |
| 7 | Write long or write short | Armstrong: 70+ pages, "I rewrite long" — writing long means not having to choose too early | Kelley: "if I can say a thing in four words, I will never use five" | Hold final cut and believe in the economies of scale of dialogue pages → write long; submission or spec stage → write short ("The fat ones are always the last to be picked") |
| 8 | Write fast or revise long | Kelley: "**I usually write a script in four or five days. If a script takes me a week or more, it usually isn't very good**" | Armstrong: about 15 drafts on the pilot, and **at least 30 and usually 50** on every episode after | On staff, weekly, racing a table read → the Kelley model (pressure is part of the quality); in development, with final cut, and dense subtext → the Armstrong model |
| 9 | Whether to discuss theme/tone in the room | Hillian: thematic unity is central, and once the story is settled you go back and fold A/B/C into the season theme | McNamara: "**I'm all about the characters**"; Levinson refuses an overarching theme; Berg has never discussed tone | Theme may come before or after, but **check it at least once after the story is broken**; if tone is the buyer's biggest question, answer it with footage |
| 10 | Can performance and editing substitute for writing | Fellowes: "A good actor's skill will always obviate some of the dialogue"; Dollner: "**Editing is essentially the final draft of the script**" | Armstrong: "the final draft of the show isn't the script. **It's the version we air.**" | No conflict: **treat the edit as the final draft, but give the editor enough choices on the page first** — which is the reason for "writing long" and shooting alts |

## 10. Diagnostic Checklist

**Before entering the room**　1 How many episodes have you watched? Have you taken the main title apart shot by shot? Can you state the franchise in ≤4 lines? Could your story be moved to another show (if yes, it is wrong)?　2 How many springboards did you bring? Can each be told in 3–5 sentences? How many came from "what a regular would throw up while in stasis," and how many are guesses at this year's big arc (delete all of the latter)?　3 Did you pre-pitch? Does the person you are pitching have hiring power (do not pitch to a story editor)?　4 Are you ready to have your subject or your killer changed on the spot? Can you tell which change genuinely breaks everything, and say why?　5 Do you know what kind of room this is (one that argues / one that is quiet), and who is really running the show?　6 Have you listed the standing sets and locations with their exact spellings, character-name spellings, and dialogue-header conventions?

**While breaking story**　7 How many beats does each line have? A line 12–16? B line 8–12? C line 3–8? About thirty-five in total?　8 Are the three tent poles up? **Is the worst case "what opposes the protagonist's goal / the antagonist's victory," or merely a bad event?**　9 Is every beat an action (a "realization" is not a scene)? Are you bored reading the cards yourself?　10 Is the room solving **one** problem, or chasing four at once?　11 For each change: "What does this change buy us?" — does it buy more than it costs?　12 Is the pitch opener "This is shit but –" (delete) or "This is the trashy version" (keep)? Has the same proposal been rejected more than three times?

**Before delivering a document**　13 Is the leave-behind one page in three paragraphs with a beginning, middle and end? Did you write both the platform version and the show version?　14 Does the beat sheet run 6–7 scenes an act and 6–20 pages? **Does every scene state a location and a filmable behaviour?**　15 Does the outline contain anything invisible on screen (the protagonist's fear of heights, a character's inner awareness)? If so, convert it into something playable, or cut it.　16 What day of the story is this? Do day and night line up? Are the characters at work when they should be at work?　17 Did you transcribe the lines the showrunner said aloud in the room and put them in verbatim? Did you leave bad temp dialogue in?　18 Did you finish a day early? **Let it cool for a day, then polish, then deliver on time** (do not deliver early).

**After taking notes**　19 Did you argue, explain or defend? Did you say "got it"? Did you record it or write every note down?　20 Which are overall notes and which are line notes? Was there a "killer"? Did you throw out a fix on the spot?　21 Did you go through every word and every layer (without rebutting), metabolize it (without transcribing), and go away with your own question?　22 Do several readers' notes overlap? Is any of them a weakness you already knew about? Is any of them making you deeply uncomfortable (that one is most likely right)?　23 Can the cold reader say where this story is going? Did you do **all** the line notes?

**Revision and production**　**23a Did you reread the diagnostic table item by item before delivering?** (Every item must answer "where in the revised draft was this resolved?" The commonest failure is not missing the diagnosis but **diagnosing it and only half-solving it** — the problem goes into the diagnostic table and the revised draft commits it anyway. Anything you cannot locate has not been fixed.)　**23b Did you cross-check item by item against episodes already aired or already locked?** (Have the hooks planted in earlier episodes been paid here? Does this episode pick up from the last episode's final image? Has this episode spent early something a later episode is already committed to using — a location, a prop, a diagnosis, a price increase?) This item appears in no other diagnostic checklist anywhere, and it is the highest-frequency error when revising a show already on air.　24 What is this episode actually about? Given that, what can go and what must stay?　25 Are these locations central to the meaning of the scene, or decorative? How many scenes could be folded into standing sets?　26 Did someone hand you a "solution"? Did you translate it back into the problem (how many pages over, how much over budget) and offer a better solution?　27 Has the budget compressed this episode into one location with no guest cast? Then **which two characters have a confrontation that should have happened long ago, and which secret in the season arc should move forward one step?**　28 How many alts did you prepare (0–15)? How many colleagues did you ask for alts (3–5)? Did you keep a freebie take?　29 For every cut, did you write down what was lost? Should it be recovered in a later episode?　30 Is this episode already unsalvageable? (If so, say so, and put the effort into the next one.)

**Career self-check**　31 Is my office the furthest and quietest on the floor? Did I retreat home to write this week? If so, what happened on the show this week that I do not know about?　32 Is my writing better or worse than when I joined? If worse, have I talked to the showrunner?　33 Do I have a second writing income, a non-writing job, or someone to share expenses with?

## 11. Workflow: One Episode From Assignment to Delivery

| Step | Action | Passing standard |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Confirm the process**: break story collectively or separately? How full must the outline be? Who gives notes, and in what order? Ask the production assistant for past outlines and scripts as formatting templates | All three questions answered |
| 1 | **Write this show's rules on one page**: the franchise in one sentence; three tone rules reverse-engineered from the main title; standing sets and locations plus how they are written; character names and headers; page count and the star's maximum appearance page; shooting days | The page is full |
| 2 | **Break the story**: log line (one sentence per line) → the two tests → the three tent poles → work backwards to the middle beats → beat counts land inside the ratios → physically sort the slips or cards once | The worst case is "the antagonist's victory"; every beat is an action |
| 3 | **The leave-behind** (if approval is needed): one page, three paragraphs; two versions; conspiracy lines written as "What we will discover is that…" / "What X doesn't know is that…" | Both versions written |
| 4 | **Outline / beat sheet**: 6–7 scenes an act, roughly a page an act; every step a scene with a protagonist, a goal and an opposition; complete slug lines; day numbers marked; the showrunner's own words inserted verbatim; no bad temp dialogue | Delivered inside 7 working days |
| 5 | **Take the first round of notes and revise the outline**: listen, record, write it down, say got it; ask what problem sits behind each overall note; throw out a fix for a killer note on the spot | The outline is **locked** |
| 6 | **First draft (the 14-day method)**: 28 beats ÷ 2 scenes a day; each day, read and revise yesterday's → write today's first scene → stop → go away → reread and revise → write the second scene → stop. When you drift far from the approved beats, **make the phone call** | Finish a day early, let it cool a day, **deliver on time** |
| 7 | **The notes meeting and the second draft**: sort the notes; do all the line notes; metabolize an overall note before acting on it; a polish may follow the second draft | Every note is findable in the draft |
| 8 | **Prep and shooting**: the script becomes coloured pages (pink → blue → green…, changes marked with asterisks). Have three things ready: ① one page saying what this episode is about; ② a list of scenes that could be folded into standing sets, with page counts; ③ the habit of translating "solution notes" back into problems | If told one location and no guest cast, go back to 7.3 |
| 9 | **Alts and set**: pick 0–15 lines, send to 3–5 colleagues for alts, filter to 5–10 to take to set; after the scripted version is shot, keep one freebie take; treat an actor's paraphrase as an implied note | You have alts in your pocket |
| 10 | **Editing and review**: treat the edit as the final draft; for every cut, write down **what was lost** and judge whether to recover it later; file cut material in the cut-outs document; keep every draft | The loss list is complete |
| 11 | **The next one**: track new shows; stay in touch with producers who bought a script from you; **record the pilot and an episode or two of every new show, think of two stories for each, and have your agent set the showrunner meeting**; tell your agent what you can and cannot write; **write another spec in a different genre** | "If it's easy, you're not stretching." (Douglas) |

## 12. Exercises

1. **Reverse-engineer a main title** (Goldberg chapter 9 exercise): record the main titles of three shows and take them apart shot by shot, writing one sentence per shot on "why it is in there and what it is meant to convey"; then write three things the show permits and three it forbids.
2. **Beat-sheet reverse engineering** (chapter 11 exercise): record an episode of your favourite show and break it into a beat sheet scene by scene and act by act (with real slug lines). "You're holding a snapshot of the series."
3. **The budget-note drill** (chapter 12 exercise): take the beat sheet above, pretend you wrote it, and receive a note — **cut three exteriors for budget and find four more scenes to move onto standing sets.** Rewrite without changing the plot.
4. **The actor-falls-ill drill** (chapter 16 exercise): with the same beat sheet, the second lead has the flu — **write them out of the episode and make the story work.** How much can you keep? Which conflicts have to be remade or abandoned?
5. **The notes-meeting drill** (chapter 13 exercise): give a story idea to a partner, a close friend and a "sworn enemy," listen without arguing, write down every note and say thank you; look at it again a day later. Then do something television rarely lets you do: **take only the useful ones.**
6. **The two-minute pitch** (chapter 8 exercise): tell a friend about the film you saw last weekend and watch when they drift and when they want to escape; polish the telling accordingly. Then use the same method to pitch three stories you have for a show currently on air.
7. **The trashy-version exercise** (Prebble): write the pulpiest, most over-the-top version of the episode in front of you, then pull it back three notches. Record what each notch loses and what it keeps.
8. **The stasis list** (Douglas rule 3): pick a show on air and list "five impulses or issues that would come up for the regulars while behaving normally," writing one sentence after each on why they do it. This year's big arc is barred.
9. **Fix one line in isolation** (Miller): take one of your own scripts, pull every scene of the B line into a separate document, fix it until it works, then put it back. Compare page count and clarity before and after.
10. **Ten chairs** (Wells): assemble a temporary room of 3–5 people and break one episode of a show on air in six hours: a four-heading whiteboard (Big / Serious / Humorous / Other) → list beats line by line for A/B/C → cut up and reorder → assign one person to write the treatment → the group gives notes → they revise → the group gives notes again. Record how long each step took.

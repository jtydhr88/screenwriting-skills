## 1. Master Table of the Seven Ways to Break Story (source, method, and anchors for headcount / weeks / beat count)

| Method | Source | The method in one sentence | Quantitative anchors |
|---|---|---|---|
| **35 beats on a whiteboard plus scissors and tape** | Chase, Sopranos foreword | List the beats line by line on the board → the assistant types them out sorted into A/B/C columns → **cut them up and "marry" them into episode order with clear tape** → retype, and that is the outline | About **35 beats** an episode: roughly 13 each for A and B, 5–6 for C, a few for D; about three months from starting to write to starting to shoot |
| **Ten chairs plus a six-week season lay-in** | Wells (ER), Douglas chapter 5 | From the first week of June, the whole staff spends six weeks pounding out the season's turning points and where the act outs land → send one writer off to write a treatment → group notes → revise → **another round of notes** → only then write the script | **10 chairs** at the table = 4 senior writers + 4 staff writers + 1 researcher + Wells; **about eight weeks an episode**; notes meetings Monday, Wednesday and Friday, four to five hours each |
| **A four-act grid plus coloured index cards** | Douglas chapter 4 | Log line → the two tests (credibility / rooting interest) → three tent poles → work backwards → **one colour of card per line**, laid out separately and then merged | About 28 scenes an hour: **A line 12–16 beats, B line 8–12, C line 3–8**; 40 cards on the A line means the pieces are too small or the story will not fit in one episode |
| **A mini boot camp plus a five-episode pipeline** | Lindelof (Lost), Douglas | **About 9 people spend a full 3 weeks on nothing but "blue sky"** to produce the blueprint; during shooting they meet for 4 hours every morning before going to set | **Five episodes are always in the pipeline**: one being analysed / one in synopsis / one in script / one shooting; a **dedicated bible keeper** (Gregg Nations) answers "we said that in season three, it is hard to overturn" |
| **Dividing a season into movements** | Katims (FNL), Landau 1e Tip 13 | At the top of the season settle only the tent poles and **write nothing down**; cut the season into stretches of 4–6 episodes, each driven by an answerable question; answer it and raise a new one | The first movement of FNL season 1 = "Will the town of Dillon survive the loss of Jason Street?", closing on the rivalry game in episode 5. Same school: Hillian treats a 10-episode season as three acts (E1 like a pilot / 2–4 / 5–7 / 8–10) |
| **Break the emotional line first** | West (Dexter), Douglas 3rd ed. chapter 7 | **"The first thing we break is where is his emotional journey? Not so much what are the plot twists but what does this episode have to accomplish in the emotional journey of twelve episodes."** | **The whole of February is eight writers talking**; "We know what's in episode twelve and then everything has to work to that" |
| **The mega-chart plus a "maybe" culture** | Prebble, Succession S3 foreword | Collect ideas on large sheets headed by **character name** (under Roman, "Afraid of sharks?"; under Kendall, "Goes Beyond") → surviving entries go onto the **mega-chart (characters across, episodes down)** | **8–12 writers, 5–6 hours a day, for months**; British and American mixed, comedy and tragedy backgrounds mixed |

## 2. Prebble's Four Room Rules (in the Succession room's own words)

**Prebble's four room rules (the most directly copyable material here)**: ① **The pitch opener** — never say "This is shit but –" (which tells people not to listen and demands reassurance first), and say instead **"This is the trashy version" / "I know this is vivid, but…" / "Not this, but maybe – Something like this."**, because Succession is a show that withholds, and **only by starting from the pulpiest version and pulling back do you keep the drama in it.** ② **Two ordering rules** (settled by the argument over whether Logan would sell the company): **"An appeal to reality over the mythic was always the most powerful pitch in the room, only occasionally losing out to whatever was the most funny."** ③ **The "maybe" culture**: "stay open until you absolutely have to close down the narrative," so as not to miss "that last possible moment of magic"; a "Maybe Connor does this" left in the script is an invitation to the director and the actors. ④ **The room's real output is what the show is not** — the generation chain of E7 runs "suicide in public" → "performance art faking a suicide" → finally "he decides not to go on stage, not to be crucified, not to sing"; **"Pitching is the way in which you work out what the show isn't, as well as what it is."**

## 3. The Physical Specification of a Leave-Behind, and Its Two Versions

**3.1 The leave-behind (story area / treatment / one-pager).** The specification (Goldberg chapter 8, as written): **"Not the whole story, one page tops, three paragraphs with the beginning, middle, and end, like jacket copy on a book. Punchy, well written."** Its function: to let them re-experience the excitement of the pitch afterwards; and the producer will use it to pitch the platform a second time. **Write two versions of the same story** (the introduction to appendix D, the most overlooked item): **"The network takes it on faith that the character conflicts will be spelled out in the beat sheet, but a producer won't grant a freelancer the same benefit of the doubt."** → **For the platform or the financier, write "this episode's conflict plus the hook"; for the show or the showrunner, write "this episode's conflict plus which thread in the regulars' relationships it touches."** A conspiracy show writes it as **the structure of the secret, not the audience's order of discovery** (appendix F, seaQuest): use **"What we will discover is that…"** and **"What X doesn't know is that…"** to make clear who is manipulating whom and who does not know what, and state that **at series level only relationships may change and the power structure must be restored.**

## 4. Beat Sheet / Outline: Terminology, Length, Format and Four Hard Annotations

**3.2 Beat sheet / outline (several names for one object).** Terminology (Douglas chapter 4): a **treatment** is in theory the whole story in prose with no dialogue, but "nobody writes treatments like that," and in television being asked for a treatment means being asked for a few pages of summary; a **beat sheet** describes the major events act by act and does not list specific scenes; an **outline** lists the actual scenes to be written. The header may read `Revised Outline` / `Rough Beat Sheet by` / `Treatment` / `Story Area by` — **use whatever the show calls it and do not argue about names.**
- **Length and deadline** (Goldberg, as written): **"usually six or seven scenes per act, four acts per episode"**; scene headings, one paragraph of description per scene; **6–20 pages**; **the WGA specifies 7 working days.** Douglas's format target: **one page per act, about 4 pages for an hour outline**; scene numbers restart at #1 each act; every "step" must be **a scene with a protagonist, a goal and an opposition**; break beats by content rather than by time and place (a new conflict in the same location is a new beat).
- **Four uses**: to test whether there is really a script inside the idea ("a good idea often falls apart as it takes shape") / so that you need not worry about plot moves while writing / to reassure the producer / **as a production tool — the line producer scouts, builds and budgets from it.** The implication of the fourth: **every scene must state a location and a filmable behaviour.**
- **Douglas's four hard annotations**: ① enter every scene as close to the conflict as possible, do not let characters wander and burn screen time; ② **animal wrangling is expensive**, so spend it only where it is dramatically necessary; ③ you cannot see an exterior from inside the squad room, so if you want driving, change the slug line, and otherwise cut it; ④ **"the protagonist's fear of heights is invisible on screen" — do not put into an outline what cannot go into the script, and if you must, say how it is played.** Plus: **what day of the story is this? Settle it at the outline stage, not after 50 pages** (typing will expose "this scene is written as daytime but it is night" and "this character should not be at work in the evening").
- **The temp-dialogue dilemma**: putting dialogue in shows the conflict, but bad temp dialogue makes a producer doubt your dialogue; **worst of all is when it turns up verbatim in the script** (proving you did not rethink the scene when writing it). The reverse: one EP was furious that temp dialogue tossed out in the room did not make the script — "these words tossed out in the room were precious as gold." → The instruction (Sheldon Bull, Smith): **"If producers pitch a joke, they will expect to find that joke in your outline. Write it down verbatim and put it in."**
- **The outline is a contractual paid step**: it generates a "story by" credit; a contract with cut-offs may end here; it may be **"locked"**, and on a low-budget show prep may even start from the outline.

## 5. The First Draft: the 14-Day Method, the Half-Hour of Preparation, and Draft-Naming Discipline

**3.3 The first draft.** **Do not make big changes to an assignment without warning** (Douglas chapter 4): the author once changed the Act Four ending on her own initiative, and the producer called in an emotional state — the original ending had been his idea, and "**This was about respect.**" The rule: when writing reveals a problem the outline did not expose, **solve it, staying as close to the outline's spirit as you can; if you would stray far from the approved beats or need a major restructure, make the phone call first** — but do not call at every bump: "If they wanted to wrestle with the everyday problems of writing a script, they would have written it themselves instead of hiring you." (Goldberg)
- **The 14-day method**: the first draft is due two weeks after the outline is approved, so **28 beats ÷ 2 scenes a day = 14 days.** Each day: read and revise yesterday's work in the morning → treat today's scene as "the single most important passage you have ever written" → write two or three pages **and then stop** → after lunch, get away from it → in the afternoon reread and revise, then write the second scene, and stop. **Disagreement preserved**: the other kind of writer gets up at 4 a.m. and writes furiously without looking back, treating it as raw material to be cut afterwards — **painter vs. sculptor, neither superior.**
- **The half-hour of preparation before starting** (Goldberg): is the car slug `INT. CAR` / `INT. MARK'S CAR` / `INT. THE MUSTANG`? Is the dialogue header `DR. SLOAN` / `MARK` / `MARK SLOAN`? "These may seem like small details to you, but they aren't." → **Before you start, list the standing sets and locations with their spellings, and check every character name and header convention.**
- **The draft's name is its number** (Miller chapter 9): **rough draft → writer's draft (several versions) → (some people add a reader's draft) → first draft → endless rewrites.** Three hard rules: the only job at first is to put words on the page; **you need not write in order**; **do not revise as you write.** When you stall, go back to character wants — "Every scene will have at least two characters with different wants."

## 6. Second Draft, Polish, Coloured Pages and Keeping Drafts

**3.4 Second draft, polish, coloured pages.**
- **Revising ≠ rewriting** (Douglas chapter 4): fixing spelling, tightening a line, cutting a paragraph is part of writing; **a rewrite rethinks structure and even character** — "put the script down first, everything on the table," or you will "turn into a pretzel trying to fit a structure around scenes that don't belong." A **polish** is a small pass (usually a dialogue polish) and does not include restructuring, new characters or new arcs; a **"cultural wash"** is also called a dialogue polish (bringing in a second writer to make characters from a given background sound natural, **usually uncredited**).
- **Coloured pages** (Goldberg): each revision is printed on dated coloured stock in a fixed order, **pink → blue → green…**, with every change marked by an asterisk. The meaning is that **somebody is always still giving notes** — producers → studio → network → and, once approved, production → actors. The Sopranos pilot went through **6 revisions** from the Production Draft of 7/16/97 to the Final Shooting Script of 8/25/97.
- **Keep every draft** (someone will always say "I preferred that line in the last version"); Miller's practice is a single collecting document called **cut-outs**, into which anything cut is pasted.
- **Writing long vs. writing short (the disagreement is in section 9 of SKILL.md)**: Armstrong writes **70+ pages** an episode (the norm being 60, with some shows down to 50), "I like to write long and I encourage my fellow writers to write long. **I rewrite long.**"; Kelley is the opposite: "if I can say a thing in four words, I will never use five" (Blum).

## 7. Rules for Choosing a Show to Spec, and a Tone-Matching Transcript

**4.1 Rules for choosing a show to spec.** The definition (Goldberg chapter 5): "No one asked for it. No one's paying for it. No one wants to read it." — but "**it's the only way in.**" A TV spec is rarely over 65 pages: **"The fat ones are always the last to be picked."**
- **Five rules for choosing** (chapter 6): pick a show that will **last** (a spec of a cancelled show is "about as welcome as month-old lox"); pick a show that **will not transform** (do not spec season one — "the staff have not found how to write it yet"); **avoid heavily serialized shows** (where there is a long arc, write a stand-alone, "one that could fit into any season"); pick a show **people in the industry are watching**; and **be prepared to break all of the above** (in the early 1990s a comedy pair stood out from a pile of Cosby and Night Court specs with an *I Love Lucy* spec and got onto the number-one comedy).
- **Two "almosts"**: **some shows do not let you shine** (Walker, Texas Ranger's characters are black and white and its dialogue exists only for exposition) — **"It's not your job to write the show you think it should be; it's your job to write the best possible version of the show that is."**; and **some shows nobody wants to read** (readers favour shows they have watched).
- **Tone match matters more than "a show everyone agrees is good"** (the Martial Law hiring transcript): agents sent Homicide and The Practice (too dark), Ally McBeal and Sex and the City (also wrong); in the end they had to say plainly, "send us Xenas. Send us Hercules. Send us Buffys." — and the writers hired came from Star Trek: Voyager, Hercules and La Femme Nikita.
- **Douglas's opposite rule (disagreement preserved)**: **"DON'T SPEC THE SERIES YOU PLAN TO PITCH."** (3rd ed. chapter 6, rule 2) — producers know their own minefields (they have heard enough dog stories, enough bodies in pools, two actors had a row yesterday so you cannot write them in love), and **an outsider cannot possibly know.** Evidence: in 2010, producers from procedurals to science fiction to family drama were all reading **Breaking Bad** specs, "they were reading for ability with characters"; Souders hired for Smallville off **Mad Men specs** ("action writing doesn't matter much on our show… **what we care about is character writing**"). But the woman in the "fairy tale" in the same chapter broke in precisely by **sending specs to one show over and over**, and Douglas notes herself, "Okay, that's opposite the lesson in the fairy tale." → **Specing a different show is the normal route; repeatedly specing the same show holds only with the stamina to trade three scripts for one note.**

## 8. Researching a Show: Shot-by-Shot Main Titles, the Stasis Question, and an Angle Only You Can Bring

**4.2 Researching a show.**
- **Method one: take the main title apart shot by shot** (Goldberg chapter 9). A main title costs $40,000–100,000 and picks only 10–12 shots from thousands of feet of film — **each one is the EP's understanding of the show**; three kinds: the **format sequence** (narration or text stating what the show is, as in Law & Order), the **mood sequence** (tone, as in The X-Files) and the **character sequence** (Hunter). The test: **"Does your story fulfill what the main title promises? If it doesn't, you're pitching the wrong stories."** Five worked cases are in Goldberg chapter 9.
- **Method two: ask what comes up while the show is in stasis** (Douglas rule 3). Do not use story lines the show has already laid out — those arcs will be over before you finish, and **the big changes that turn a show are executive decisions, not freelancer work.** The right question: **"while the characters are in stasis (behaving normally), what impulses or issues come up out of them?"** Then dig into **why they do it.** Alongside: **catch the voices** — "ask how Vampire Bill's word choice and syntax differ from Vampire Eric's on True Blood; how Peggy differs from Pete on Mad Men. **The differences are on the page.**"; and **"Be unpredictable within the world of the show"** (example: writing about breast cancer, **give it to a man**).
- **Method three: bring an angle only you can bring.** Specing Dexter when you are a marine biologist → he is often on the water, so what can the life in that water add to the conspiracy? While Friday Night Lights was on air, **the best specs all came from people who had lived in Texas and knew the lore of rattlesnakes and dust storms** — "You could smell the authenticity in the pages."

## 9. Pitching an Episode: Number and Length, Delivery, Live Etiquette From Six Producers, Three Endings

**4.3 Pitching an episode.** **"A pitch is an audition."** (Goldberg) Nobody in the room wants to be there, and each of them has three other things to do — **"So you've got about two minutes to win them over."**
- **How many and how long (three books merged)**: Goldberg — **three solid ideas plus half a dozen half-formed notions**; Smith rebuts — **bring 10–15 springboards** (a producer will cut one or two because they were done elsewhere, and another one or two because "we did that" or "we are about to"); Blum gives the length — "a springboard is **three to five sentences**… six at most." → **Take the number from Smith and the length from Blum: tell it in 3–5 sentences, move to the next when it is cut, and go back to the several-paragraph version only when someone bites. A pitch meeting usually runs only 30–45 minutes.**
- **What to tell and how**: broad strokes only — **"You might start with the teaser, then launch into the major character arcs and the act breaks."**; **the Full Monty rule** ("these laid-off steelworkers strip for money, but they all have bodies like mine"), rather than starting with "there's a town in England where the economy is bad and the mills have closed…"; **"What happens next?" is the sound of success.** Two kinds of bad pitch: **the rewinder** ("the patient is called Fred… did I mention he has a dog? Let me back up…") and **the beat-by-beat** ("Joe walks into the hospital and says 'Hello, Dr. Sloan,' and Sloan has a coffee stain on his lapel…", reaching the actual story 45 minutes in). Matt Williams's version of the same rule: **"pitch the sizzle, not the steak."**
- **Live etiquette** (live advice from six producers, item by item): **don't be married to what you pitch** (a producer will change your subject, your killer or your kind of disaster on the spot — catch it; if a suggestion genuinely breaks everything, **explain why**, or "you have just talked yourself out of $27,000"); **never lose your temper** (a supervising producer once stood up mid-pitch with "You keep pitching — **I'm going to the bathroom to throw up**"; the author laughed, said "Guess you don't like that one," went on to the next, and **got the assignment**); **follow their lead**, especially the loudest person in the room; **"Never appear desperate or needy"**; **never open by saying an idea is great or flawed** ("many a writer has sold the weakest, lamest idea tossed out in a pitch"); **stop pitching other ideas the moment someone bites on one**; **never open with "Have you done the one about…?"** (ask that and she stops listening to anything else; conversely, colliding with a story they have already done **is not a mark against you**, it proves "they know what our characters would do"); **pre-pitch** (call a junior writer before the meeting and say "here is roughly what I am thinking"). Two more from Landau 2e: make eye contact with **everyone** in the room (ignore one junior executive and he may sink your pitch after you leave), and **stop talking when you are done** ("**Finish the pitch and don't buy it back**… **Do not give in to this temptation**").
- **What to pitch (at the subject level)** (chapter 10): **"If it's a detective show, don't think about the case. If it's an occult show, don't think about the monster. If it's a medical show, don't think about the illness." Think about what? Conflict.** Three opening questions: **How do my characters get involved in this story? What is at stake for them? How is this different from the story we told last week?** The story must be character-based and conflict-driven, and give the protagonist a **character turn** before it ends. The model: the Matlock pitch that sold consisted of two and a half acts of character turn (he is appointed temporary judge and must sentence to death a man he believes innocent; at the end of Act Two he goes to death row and says "I'd like to help you," and the man answers "Haven't you helped me enough? You are sending me to the electric chair," and he smiles: "But you are innocent and now I am going to defend you on appeal.") — **while pitching, the authors had no idea what the case was or who the real killer was**: "We had the major story moves and the important conflicts for the lead—that's all that was important to the pitch."
- **Three endings** (Smith): ① they want time to think → leave the leave-behind, and call your agent on the way out to chase it ("suggest it yourself. **Strongly.**"); ② everything is rejected → the only rescue is **asking to pitch again in a day or two** and asking which directions they want explored ("two strikes will leave a bad taste"); ③ **"Go write an outline" = a sale, so stop pitching immediately.**
- **Once it sells, you are a stenographer** (Sheldon Bull, Smith): **"When you are a freelance writer in a story meeting with producers and staff writers, you are not an equal. You are a stenographer."** Alongside: do your homework, go in with story ideas and jokes; before you leave, confirm the delivery date; **ask the production assistant for a few of the show's past outlines as formatting templates.**
- **Why they will not let you crack the story alone** (the list of production realities you do not know): the episode budget; how many days must be stage and how many location; which actors' contracts cap their episodes per season; which actor works only three days a week; which episodes must be shot in six days rather than the usual seven. And the staff do not break story alone either — **the whole group does it together, sharing the understanding, so that anyone can answer a freelancer's question while the EP is in the edit, and the answer will match the EP's** ("because their jobs are to second-guess and mimic him").

## 10. Practical Material on Notes: the Telephone Code, Metabolizing, Warm/Cold Reads, and Giving Notes

**The telephone code table**: "Nice work" / "Dynamite" = good; **"Really good first draft" = they may love it or hate it, and either way there are a lot of notes**; **"Thanks for getting it done so fast" = take cover, they think it is garbage.**

**5.3 Metabolize: digest before executing** (Landau 2e chapter 14, Berger on Le Bureau). Canal+'s notes went to the producer, Berger, not to showrunner Éric Rochant; he was on the phone about 90 minutes and wrote **six pages of notes**, "it was so brutal that I hung up and went for a walk in the Tuileries to sort things out." Éric's process: ① **"Let's take the time to go through every word and every meaning of what Canal+ are saying."** (done over one weekend) ② enter what he calls **"metabolization"** — "He had to **metabolize** everything, to integrate the notes and remarks on the script." ③ then **raise his own questions**, back and forth several times. ④ The second version of the pilot "became stronger, tighter, and clearer." → **Go through every word and every layer of meaning first (without rebutting), then digest (without transcribing), then go back with your own questions and iterate.**

**5.4 Find your own readers first: warm read → cold read** (Miller chapter 10). A **warm read** comes from someone who **knows your project**, ideally **other writers** ("The notes writers give you will be different from notes other people, even people in the business, will give you"); the discipline is **not to defend the work — "Just listen and take notes."** A **cold read** is **someone who knows nothing** ("The reader knows nothing") — **keep two or three of your smartest, harshest friends aside specifically for cold reads and tell them nothing**; **the question you must ask** is **"Ask your cold readers where they think your story is going."** (If they can answer, your series passes; if they cannot, or get it wrong, go back and find where it went off; then ask **why** they think so and **which elements most helped them understand your world.**)
- **Three rules for filtering notes**: ① **look for repetition** (if several readers give similar notes, there really is a problem); ② **look for what you already agree with** (a reader naming a weakness you already knew about must be addressed); ③ **look for the note that makes you deeply uncomfortable** — "The note itself may not be precise, but **your mind is rebelling against the note for some reason.**"
- Two companion methods: **read it aloud** ("If you stumble over dialogue or description, change it."; print a paper copy every time and mark it on paper, to resist fixing as you read); and **fix a line in isolation** (pull every scene of the B line into a separate document, fix it, and put it back). **Do not fix a logic problem in the scene where it appears**: go back one or two scenes — "That's like saying if we put a Band-Aid here, it'll fix this broken leg." The root cause is that "**Whenever something serves your plot, it will feel forced.**"

**5.5 If you are the one giving notes.** **Anonymous reads** (Souders, Smallville, 2011): when scripts come in, **tear off the title page and number them** → send to several readers → **at least two readers per script** → only those with a "+" reach the showrunner's desk (the motivation being that the pressure from agents phoning to demand "put this person's script on top" was too great). **A note's origin may reach further than you know**: "the note you receive may come from someone who **knows what happens four episodes from now**, and has nothing to do with whether they like what you wrote"; "if you think your script does not need another pass, you are being naive." **Embrace the rewrite** (Eric Trueheart): "New writers want to get it perfect the first time… and you never, ever do. **The rewrite is your biggest friend.**" Miller: professional television writers "**rip the script apart, write entirely new sections, rearrange sequences—we do heavy lifting**… **If you don't like to rewrite, this isn't the job for you.**" The seven rules for the writer taking notes are in SKILL.md §5.1.

## 11. The Rank Ladder, Currency Notes, the Step Deal, and Three Characterizations of Freelancing

> **Currency flag**: the figures come from **2003 (Goldberg) / the 2007 Chinese edition and the 2011 English 3rd edition (Douglas) / 2009–2011 (Smith) / 1999–2001 (Blum)** respectively. **Every amount and week count is out of date** (WGA minimums are renegotiated every three years); **the structure (seven title rungs, the step deal's two cutoffs, the 50% credit rule, staff doing most of the writing, freelance slots being auditions) still holds.** The full title table follows.

| Rung | English | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | freelance writer | Writes a single episode; **does not attend story meetings, does not see dailies, has no office at the show** |
| 2 | staff writer ("baby writer") | **Someone who has never been on a staff**; usually given 1 episode a season (with a senior writer "supervising," though that person is ready to rewrite it); **up or out at the end of the season**; the script fee **is set against the salary** |
| 3 | story editor / executive story editor | Goes through every stage of an episode; usually writes 2 original episodes a season plus polishes and rewrites |
| 4 | producer / co-producer | Three kinds of people mixed together (line-producer types / writers who came up / hybrids); when shooting starts tomorrow and the script has a problem, **this may be the person who rewrites it overnight while the credit stays with the original writer** |
| 5 | supervising producer | **At the breaking-in stage, this is the title you are most likely to pitch to** — usually able to give you an assignment and guide the script (the deal needs EP approval) |
| 6 | creative consultant | "A mysterious title"; given to writers with high prestige, **with no obligation to come in**, and they may write nothing and attend nothing — or they may be the real showrunner |
| 7 | executive producer / showrunner | See section 1 |

**The step deal and its two cutoffs** (Douglas): **the first is after the story** — if the story is useless, or if in their view you cannot deliver a quality script, the deal is **terminated**, and someone else writes the script from your story or writes a new story; **the second is after the first draft** — triggered by your **failure to catch the characters' voices, to express what the show is about, or simply by bad writing** (flat or purely expository dialogue, unfocused scenes, absent dramatic tension). **The 50% rule in credit arbitration**: if the story is substantially yours → you keep the story credit; if **50% of what you wrote survives in the final draft → shared credit**; if a new writer changes more than 50% → **you lose it.** **The staged total is deliberately higher than a flat fee, as a reward** (you only reach the next stage if the option is picked up). Goldberg's phrasing is **"cut you off at story"** — "We aren't going to invest $27,000 in you unless we are convinced you can do the job." **A sitcom has only one cut point** (Smith): after delivering the story outline, and "few sitcom producers actually cut writers off"; read the contract to see whether you are hired for "story and teleplay" or **"story with option for teleplay"** (the latter pays a little more, because it is the option to cut you loose).

**Is freelancing a career or an audition (all three coexisting is the full industrial picture)**: Douglas chapter 5 — freelancing was once a viable lifestyle; chapter 6 — **"freelance work is now really an audition for a staff job (audition scripts)"**, and **many of them are rewrites rather than originals** (starting again from the outline / the story works but the script must be rewritten / a dialogue polish only), so "**Say yes. Think of it as dues.**"; Souders (2004) — **"we have two freelance episodes this year and we give them to the assistants."**

## 12. The Real Reasons People Get Replaced or Never Get In, the Seven Deadly Sins in Full, Room Politics

**The real reasons people get replaced or never get in.** Hiring and firing is decided (Goldberg, introduction) almost always on **one script, sometimes on one page of a script**, at most two scripts and a half-hour meeting. **Casting a writing room**: "Casting a writing room is a delicate process and is **just as important as casting your actors**." Beyond talent, Goldberg wants people who are **tough and confident, who will fight for an idea in the room and challenge the showrunner; who can produce an eleventh idea after ten have been shot down.** Two counter-examples: a story editor on a hit show wrote superb scripts, shot almost as written, and was not renewed for being **"too enthusiastic"** (that EP wanted a quiet room in which he could think aloud while the staff took notes); a young woman writer had very good scripts but spoke softly with her head down in the interview — "**She just wasn't tough enough.**" → **The "correct personality" for a room varies by showrunner; work out which kind of room it is during the interview.**

**Douglas's seven deadly sins ("The Staff from Hell," chapter 5; she says she committed all of them)**: ① **Do not sit far from the writing staff** — the old hands all take the rooms right by the showrunner's office; he sees them the moment he steps out, comes to them for a rush rewrite, and they hear the gossip first. ② **Do not mix personal life with work** — the warning sign of crossing the line is "discomfort in the room, or the head writer saying 'let's move on.'" ③ **Do not keep other plans running** — "driving off the lot for lunch means being late back in the afternoon." ④ **Do not hide at home to write** — she wrote a beautiful first draft at home two weeks early and came back to find that the previous episode had killed the character she was using for her turn, that the network had rejected a key location, that two actors were now dating, and that an intern had taken her office; "**Stay connected, even if you get virtually nothing written all day and have to work all night at home.**" ⑤ **Do not treat your own pages as precious** — "If you're a good enough writer to be on the staff you're skilled enough to rewrite… **If you don't, someone else will.**" ⑥ **Do not disparage the staff's culture** — the smarter move is not to join on their terms but to **find another shared interest with at least one person and build an ally.** ⑦ **Do not stay on a show that does not suit you** — "misery is not an essential rung on the ladder"; what you need is not only credits but **referees** (the next producer will certainly call the last one, and may even ask other writers on the staff "what was it like working with them"); the judgement: if the show's quality justifies it and you still write well in a bad atmosphere → stay and bank the credit; **if your writing is deteriorating → talk, then leave** (ideally negotiating a reference that costs nobody anything).

**Smith's additions on room politics** (chapter 18): read the draft to be rewritten and take notes before going into the roundtable, do not improvise on the spot; **humiliate someone in front of their boss and they will come for you**; observe before you open up; **do not talk about people behind their backs** (listening to it is a form of assent); ally upward if you ally at all; and **the most important one, identical to Douglas's fourth: do not work at home.**

## 13. Everything Costs Money: the Cat-Becomes-Dog Ledger, Page Economics, and a Case That Worked

**7.1 Everything costs money, and locations are the first thing changed.** The ledger for `EXT. STREET – DAY  A CAT ambles off the sidewalk and is run over by a car.`: a trained show-biz cat plus a wrangler; a non-regular character driving means you cannot use a company picture car, so you rent one and hire a stunt driver; a street permit, a location fee, and off-duty police to direct traffic. After the line producer negotiated each item, the EP's conclusion was one sentence: **"Make it a dog."** (A cat is $5,000 and a dog $800, and there was no usable trained cat in all of L.A. County; Isis became the dog-headed Anubis, with gender changed throughout the script.)
- **Page economics**: an episode averages **7–12 pages a day**; a company move (wrap the camera, break it down, load, drive, unload, set up again) easily loses a quarter of a day → merge scenes, switch to a third location, or move everything onto standing sets. **The test question: "are the locations central to what the scenes are about, or are they just decoration?"**
- **A case that worked** (West Wing's "The Drop-In"): Sam finishes shouting in Toby's office, slams the door and leaves, and the next scene continues the argument in a high-end bar — illogical, except that the bar is the bar of the Regal Biltmore, **"five seconds by elevator"** from C.J.'s location in the same episode. Sorkin added a few bar-specific lines and moved the scene there, and **it works because the scene is about the two men's emotions and about what politics demands of ideals, not about the location.**

## 14. The Bottle Episode Forced by Budget: the Economic Model of West Wing's "17 People" and Its Conversion

**7.3 The bottle episode forced by budget: treat the constraint as a signal that it is time to move the main line.** West Wing's "17 People" (S2E18). Sorkin sets out the economic model in his preface to the episode: **Warner covers the gap between what NBC pays and the actual cost through deficit financing**, in the hope that at around **100 episodes** the syndication value per episode will exceed its cost — "**until then every episode loses money**"; and the real source of the overage is that "**it takes us a little longer to shoot and that means overtime and that's the real money.**" So Warner ordered that the next episode have **no guest cast, no locations, no new sets, no extras and no film.** Sorkin's conversion (the one sentence most worth copying): **"In other words, I got to write a play. It was time for Bartlet to start coming clean about his MS."**

## 15. Actors: Vetoes, Availability, Bodies — and Writing for Actors

**7.4 Actors: vetoes, availability, bodies, and writing for actors.**
- **An actor can veto a whole story 24 hours before shooting.** Martial Law's "Sammo Blammo": the original setup had Grace undercover as an arms dealer and Sammo playing her butler; **Sammo Hung refused to play a butler** (he had not objected at either the outline or the one-page-proposal stage — "An actor generally focuses only on the show he's shooting"). Locations were locked and guest stars signed, so the script could not be abandoned → changed to Arsenio Hall undercover → he refused too → **24 hours before shooting** it became Gretchen Egolf undercover with Sammo as her butler. "**Same story, same structure, entirely different scenes.**" The lesson: **the same basic story can be remade three completely different ways.**
- **Actor availability is a script parameter**: the Diagnosis Murder writers' guidelines state it outright — scripts run **68–70 pages**; **Mark Sloan must not appear past pages 32–35** (Van Dyke's schedule); **7 shooting days**; at least **2–3 days on standing sets** (hospital, police station, beach house).
- **Cut the dialogue when the actor plays it well enough** (Fellowes, Downton footnotes): **"A good actor's skill will always obviate some of the dialogue."**
- **An actor's veto can be a good thing** (Fleabag): Sian Clifford stopped the author on set from changing a line — "**It's good. Stop it.**"; Andrew Scott **insisted** the Priest say "I love you too" at the end — "thank God he did" (**the show's final line of love was added at an actor's request**).
- **Writing a part for a specific actor** (Fleabag): Olivia Colman saw the stage show and said she would do it any time — "**I ASKED IMMEDIATELY. I then ran to my producers, cracked open the pilot script, rewrote the end and created the part of Godmother specifically for her.**" The original pilot ending had no Godmother, and that line later became the object-driven main line of season 1. The same thing (Succession, Frank Rich's season 2 introduction): Gerri was originally a man called Jerry; and Sarah Snook's pregnancy in season four generated a mid-season story line — **on condition that "the series' architecture could support it without buckling the overall design."**
- **Rewriting on set is normal, not an accident** (Fleabag): Brett Gelman's kitchen monologue in S2E6 was **rewritten in eleven minutes in the car on the way to set**, and he had thirty seconds to learn it. **But this works only because the structure was already locked and only the lines changed** — the content of Martin's monologue changed, its function (making Claire beg him to leave) did not.

## 16. Alts, the Freebie Take, the Economics of "Writing Long," and Recycling Cut Material

**7.5 Alts, the freebie take and the economics of writing long** (Armstrong's "Note on the Text" lists seven reasons the script differs from the finished episode, each of which is a working method):
1. **The edit — and the core method is writing long.** **70+ pages** an episode (against a norm of 60, with some shows down at 50). Two reasons: ① the economies of scale of television shooting mean that a few extra **dialogue pages** (not car crashes) "return exponential rewards," letting you refocus the story in the edit; ② writing long **lets you avoid choosing** — you need not, forty drafts in and "snow-blind," cut those "small or weird or nothingy" moments, which sometimes become "**the heart of a scene or just a jewel of a moment.**"
2. **Alts**: a day or two before shooting, the showrunner picks a number of lines (**0 to 10–15**) that might be "funnier or better or truer" and **sends them to 3–5 writers asking for alts**; the 10–20 options are filtered to **5–10 kept in the pocket**, and fed to the actors as the situation warrants after the scripted version is shot.
3. **Late changes** (changes too close to shooting to be entered into the script, usually because of practical staging, **written in by ballpoint**) and **fog of war** (small changes to word order and word choice; he does not stand at the monitor with a script, watching only the performance; **an actor's slight paraphrase counts as an "implied note"**, meaning the line has no natural speech rhythm — the fair exchange being that if he is still rewriting on the day of the shoot, he must tolerate how much anyone can learn at short notice).
4. **Improvisation — the freebie take**: after the scripted version, do one "freebie." **Its value lies not in the improvised line you happen to catch but in the "spirit of freedom and collaboration" the method gives every take.** Also **ADR** (used for plot clarification, adjusting details, adding jokes) and **the remembrance of things past** (there are always changes after the read-through, "sometimes just reorderings and sharpenings, sometimes a wholesale restructuring"; actors remember the old draft and stitch deleted fragments back in while shooting — "**a reassuring safety net**").

**A cut scene moves to where the resting heart rate is lower**: the Succession footnotes repeatedly record **material being recycled** — the S1E1 scene of Shiv giving Logan an album of the old house was cut and later stitched into S2E8 'Dundee'; Tom's "You're fired. Follow the dream" scene was shot for both S1E3 and S2E2 and aired in neither. Fellowes's practice is more systematic: **for every cut, write down what was lost** (an explanation, a new colour for a character, a statement of the theme) and judge whether to recover it in a later episode (Carson's memory of Mary's childhood was recovered by reshooting).

## 17. When the Episode Cannot Be Saved

**7.6 When the episode cannot be saved.** Cobra's "A Few Dead Men": shooting in Vancouver on a budget under $650,000 an episode; the whole of Act Four was a large military-exercise action sequence, and January rained continuously, the forest became a bog, and every alternative location was taken by other productions — so **the finale ended up in a high-school gymnasium**, written in a hotel room in the middle of the night, and "**it was terrible. And it was necessary.**" (Shutting down costs tens of thousands a day.) "There are times in television when you have to say, '**This episode isn't going to work**' and move on to the next one." — even the best shows have a stinker or two a year; **trying to save the unsalvageable will wreck the next three episodes.** Other acts of God: a beach episode hit by a week of storms → it became a warehouse episode; a lead lost their voice two days into shooting → everything was rewritten around the second lead; a Diagnosis Murder episode ran into Princess Diana's death on a Saturday, and **new pages went out first thing Sunday turning a European princess into an heiress.**

## 18. Comparison Table of Five Processes, and the Reality/Unscripted Counter-Case

| Process | Representative | Method | Preconditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The standard American room** | ER (Wells), Dexter (West), Succession (Armstrong/Prebble), Smallville (Souders) | 8–12 people, 5–6 hours a day, for months; break collectively → write individually → group notes → revise → notes again; the showrunner has the deciding vote | Multi-line ensembles; a need for sustained output |
| **Single author plus a substitute room** | Fleabag (Waller-Bridge) | No room; **five roles** substitute for it: a dramaturg / script editor (who can see what you have not written yet) + a story producer (to talk to, and to build the Post-it wall with) + a director (who objects only when something is untrue) + the actors (line control and veto) + the editor (**"Editing is essentially the final draft of the script."**), plus one subject-matter consultant | Half-hour, six-episode seasons, a strong individual voice, heavily autobiographical material; **and you must actively write those five roles into your working plan rather than hoping for inspiration** |
| **No room, no outline** | Euphoria (Levinson) | Writes every episode himself and directs most; **writes no outline** (his reason: an outline kills unpredictability) | Strongly autobiographical material plus a creator who also directs plus a permissive platform. **This is an exception, not a generalizable method** |
| **A full season outline plus a strict hierarchy** | Gomorrah, Le Bureau des Légendes | The whole season is outlined before shooting; four tiers: **showrunner and artistic EP → senior writers → juniors (doing research and writing scenes for the seniors) → interns (filling the room, absorbing the tone, preparing for later seasons)** | An industry with no room tradition; a contractual delivery rhythm (Canal+: ten episodes a season, one reviewed every two weeks — "Nobody was used to that pace in France."), at **50–60% more than the local going rate, and about €1 million to write a season** (Landau 2e chapter 14) |
| **Comedy: group break plus individual first draft plus punch-up** | HIMYM, 2 Broke Girls, The Mindy Project | The room breaks story → the writer goes off and writes → back to the room for a group rewrite / punch-up → table read → run-through → shoot | Half-hour comedy. **Joke mechanics, the punch-up person, and the differences between multi- and single-camera writing are in `sw-sitcom-comedy` and are not repeated here** |

**The reality/unscripted counter-case** (Douglas 3rd ed., the Scott A. Stone interview): an unscripted show's deliverables still include a rundown broken act by act "with six act breaks, and it must have a beginning, a middle, an end and a cliffhanger," and one show's pre-production is written into "a bible about four inches thick" and approved by the platform; Stone says of himself, "**60% of my time is writing or rewriting treatments.**" But his own characterization is exactly why you should learn the room: **"This is a producer's medium… whereas scripted series are a writer-driven medium."**

## 19. John Wells's ER Room Transcribed, Smallville's Anonymous Reads, Dexter's February Camp

> The full versions of three rows in the table in section 1. Sources: Douglas 3rd ed. chapter 5, "A Slice of Life" (ER), and chapter 7 (Souders, West; the interviews are from **2011**); the 2nd-edition Chinese translation, chapter 5, supplies the terminology.

**19.1 The physical configuration of the room (transcribed as written; usable directly as a "writers' room" setting)**
- **A long, wide dark wood-grain conference table fills the entire meeting room**; the seating is fixed: **4 senior writers + 4 staff writers + 1 full-time researcher + Wells himself.**
- **The chair count differs between editions, and both are preserved**: the English 3rd edition records **10 chairs** (exactly those 10 people), the Chinese 2nd edition records **12.** → Take the **headcount of 10** as authoritative.
- **At the back of the room** are further chairs and sofas (the Chinese edition says "more than 10 chairs and several sofas") for the **full-time staff physicians** and for production people when they are needed.
- **All the chairs face a monitor** — dailies are watched there, so the room doubles as a screening room.
- **A large whiteboard on the wall**: covered with the **plot points and story breakdowns of the 12 episodes to be completed this season** (one board for the whole season, not one per episode).
- **Another board or side cabinet**: possible scene ideas listed in **coloured marker** under four headings — **Big / Serious / Humorous / Other.** → The single most copyable thing here: **do not first ask an idea "which episode does this go in," ask "which category is it"**; the existence of the Humorous column institutionally guarantees a comic release valve in every episode.

**19.2 The season and episode timetables**

| Stage | Duration | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|
| The whole staff designing the season (from **the first week of June**) | **6 weeks** | "The ideas and specific story lines of every episode of the season; the team has to **pound them out**" — settling **every major turning point, where the act breaks land, and how many episodes an arc covers** |
| One episode end to end | About **8 weeks** | See the chain below |

- **The single-episode chain (as written)**: send a writer off to write a **story treatment** → they come back and **hear the group's notes** → revise once → **another round of notes** → only then are they sent off to write the **script** → the script comes in and gets **a round of notes** → the **second draft** → if it works, prep for shooting. **Notes meetings happen three times, and two of them are at the treatment stage.**
- **Notes meetings**: Monday, Wednesday and Friday afternoons; the English edition records **four to five hours** each, the Chinese edition "**over 4 hours**," working **page by page, scene by scene, to the end of the script.**

**19.3 One real notes meeting (a *Written By* reporter visiting the earliest season of ER)**
- The opening image: the writers file out of their offices straight to the conference room, **each carrying a photocopy of the script**; **Wells at the head of the table**; coffee arrives but they start working immediately.
- The episode under discussion: a teenager with **cystic fibrosis** whose mother fears he will die, with the turn being that **the son does not want to be saved**, placing Dr. Ross between the mother, the son and the Hippocratic oath. The room's questions fall into three kinds — **relationships** (what about the boy's girlfriend), **the protagonist's choice** (does Ross save him), and **repetition** ("has the audience already seen the aspect of Ross this episode displays").
- **Wells's two interventions = a showrunner's standard moves in a notes meeting**: ① the story is "almost there," but **one key action is missing, one that would make the emotional rhythm of the whole passage finer and subtler** — which leads into a discussion of "one character is underdeveloped"; ② he **proposes a new topic himself: a few scenes using comedy to release pressure.**
→ **A notes meeting is not a line-by-line fault-finding exercise; it names "the one missing action" and then nominates the next topic. The person giving notes owns the agenda, not the solution.**
- (The three reasons for a recorder rather than a laptop are in SKILL.md 5.2 and are not repeated.)

**19.4 Kelly Souders on Smallville (2011, chapter 7)**
- **The room model (as spoken)**: "a group of writers **break story together and produce a rough outline**, and then someone takes it away to write. On some shows the writers break story separately. **We stay in the group and do it together. Multiple minds are better than one mind. A very traditional writers room.**"
- The **anonymous reads** process is in §10 (5.5); here only the motive and the boundary: the motive was that **the pressure of agents phoning to demand "put this person's script on top" was too great** — "**that system didn't work for us**"; the boundary is "**we really do read without knowing whose it is.**" → The corollary for submitters: **a recommendation cannot move this kind of room, only what is left after the title page is torn off.**

**19.5 Wendy West on Dexter (co-executive producer, 2011, same chapter)**
- **The February "camp"**: the production period runs **February to October**, so February is the off-season; the camp's main task is **settling the season's arc and "who this character actually is"**; **all of February is eight writers talking, only talking** (not writing).
- The "break the emotional line first" quotation is in section 1; two additions: ① **everything is filtered through Dexter's POV** (plus the voice-over), which "helps position you inside the story"; and ② **the direction is locked while the route stays open** — "usually we know the beginning and the end. **We know what's in episode twelve and then everything has to work to that.**"

**20.2 Smith's ladder: ten titles and 8–12 person staffing (2009–2011)**
- **Bottom to top (as written)**: **Term Writer → Story Editor → Executive Story Editor → Co-producer → Producer → Supervising Producer → Story Consultant → Consulting Producer → Co-executive Producer → Executive Producer.** Against Douglas's seven-rung table: Smith splits rung 4 into co-producer/producer and rung 6 into story consultant/consulting producer, **and has no "staff writer" at all, calling the bottom rung Term Writer.**
- **Scale**: a sitcom uses **5–20** writers and writer-producers, **averaging 8–12**; not every show uses every title, and **a favourite can skip a rung** (Story Editor straight to Co-producer).
- **Four things that do not change with title (as written)**: "all staff writers write scripts, join in group writing sessions, and attend **read-throughs, run-throughs, and final shoots**."
- **Negotiate five things**: the title, the weekly rate, the **guaranteed number of weeks of employment**, the guaranteed number of scripts, and a timetable for future promotion. The counter-intuitive rule: **the more weeks guaranteed, the lower the weekly rate.**

**20.3 Blum's institutional provisions (1998 MBA; the amounts are void, the provisions remain knowledge)**
① **Non-terminable contracts trade job security for a lower rate**: over 13 weeks the weekly rate drops 10%, over 26 weeks 20%. ② **The syndication/rerun ladder**: for series produced after 8 August 1988 — **the first 52 weeks at 2/3 of the minimum, the next 52 at 5/6, and the normal minimum thereafter.** ③ **The head-writer system for daily strips is explicitly recognized**: "**the head writer's fee may be reduced by up to five times the minimum script fee**"; **rewriting or polishing a non-prime-time series script pays 30% of the script fee.** ④ **Payment rhythm**: pay within **48 hours of delivery where possible, and at most 7 days**, and "**payment shall not be conditioned on the company's acceptance or approval of the material delivered.**" ⑤ **"Format" and "series bible" are separately priced deliverables**, with **additional payment for each story line beyond six** (→ `sw-series-engine-bible`).

## 21. The Multi-Camera Sitcom Production Week and the Freelance Timetable

> Source: Smith chapter 14 (the production week, **2009**) and chapters 11–13 (the freelance timetable, **2009**). The day counts are for sitcoms; one-hour is in section 22.

**21.1 The five days before shooting (multi-camera, transcribed as written)**

| Day | What happens | The writer's day |
|---|---|---|
| **DAY ONE** | The **table reading**: producers, writers, cast, department heads, network and studio executives attend; **everyone but the executives sits around one big table or a ring of tables**, and the cast reads through the draft to be shot that week → notes → the writers go away and rewrite; the director and departments simultaneously prepare the physical elements | **"If the script looks bad, the writers face a very long night."** |
| **DAY TWO** | Usually **another read and more notes**; once the script is acceptable, the director starts blocking cameras and staging, and there may be **a full run-through** | **Some shows fix this as rewrite night regardless of the script's state** |
| **DAYS THREE AND FOUR** | The cast learns lines in earnest and **the script locks**; the director stages and sets cameras | Notes and rewrites continue |
| **DAY FIVE (shoot day)** | Possibly **two full passes** — a formal **dress rehearsal** first, then notes, final changes, perhaps a meal, then the **final shoot** | In the edit, **the best of the two passes is assembled into the finished episode** |

- **Three ways to break stories as a group at the top of a season (pick one, do not mix)**: ① pitch stories one by one → each writer beats out their own episode → come back for a group revision → each writes their script; ② **the whole group beats out every episode** → individuals write the scripts; ③ **the whole group beats out and writes every scene together.** A serialized show settles **the season's story arc** first and then distributes its segments across the episodes.

**21.2 Freelance stage timetable (2009 figures, as written)**

| Stage | Duration |
|---|---|
| Preparing the **spoken pitch** | **2–5 days** (also recorded: try for 2–3 days of preparation, but take whatever time the producer names, however inconvenient) |
| Writing the **outline** | **2 days – 1 week** (chapter 11 also records "pitch selected → outline delivered in **2–7 days**") |
| **Revising the outline** | **2–5 days** |
| **First draft** | **1–2 weeks** (about 30 pages) |
| **Second draft** | **2 days – 1 week** (notes come **within a day or two** of the first draft) |

- **The same steps are shorter for staff; a spec is up to you.** One freelance assignment end to end = **3–5 weeks** of intensive writing.
- **The room takes a share of that time first** (same set of producers, disagreements side by side): **Matt Williams** — "we almost always spend **half a day to three days** in the room beating it out together," then give the writer **two days** for the outline (at the end of a season, "**Bring it back tomorrow**"); **Lapiduss** — a first freelance story will be "pitched out to within an inch of its life," must fit a slot **between episodes 5 and 8**, and is then nailed down beat by beat over **1–3 days**; **Gurvitz** — one day breaking it in the room, and the outline that comes back "**better reflect exactly what was said in the room**."
- **One disagreement about delivery discipline (preserved)**: **Irma Kalish** — never say "Do you want it good or do you want it tomorrow?", "**Make it good and hand it in when you promised.**"; **Douglas** (section 22) — even if you finish a day or two early, **do not deliver early**, let it cool for a day. The two do not conflict: **finish early, deliver on time.**

## 22. Douglas's Six-Week Professional Schedule for One Episode, and the 14-Day Method Day by Day

> Source: Douglas, "Rule two: take a series deadline seriously," and chapter 4 on the first draft. These are one-hour figures.

**22.1 The standard cycle for one episode = 6 weeks (transcribed as written)**

| Step | Duration | As written |
|---|---|---|
| Writing the **story outline** | **1 week** | "you may take 1 week to write the story outline" |
| **Revising the outline** | **a few days** | "a few days to revise it" |
| Delivering the **first draft** | **2 weeks** | "deliver the first draft of the episode two weeks later" |
| **Hearing notes** | **1–2 days** | "another day or two to hear the notes" |
| Writing the **second draft** | **1 week** | "then write the second draft within a week" |
| **Total** | **about 6 weeks** | "from the pitch to the second draft is only about 6 weeks" |
| (**Polish / revisions for production**) | another **1–2 weeks** | "polishing and revising for production adds another week or two" |

- **There is exactly one consequence of missing it (as written)**: "if you cannot finish on time, the showrunner has to hand your work to another writer."
- **This table does not contradict Wells's "about eight weeks an episode" in section 19**: Wells's eight weeks is **the whole process from assignment to shootable** (including three rounds of notes and waiting for approval); Douglas's six weeks is **the writer's own net writing period** (up to the second draft).

**22.2 The 14-day method, day by day (as written, seven steps)**
- **The starting point**: the English 3rd edition says **the first draft is due two weeks after the outline is approved**; the Chinese 2nd edition renders this sentence as "for a multi-episode show, it usually takes two weeks after the outline is approved to determine how many episodes will be written" — **follow the English**, as the Chinese distorts it here.
- **The arithmetic (as written)**: "Say you have a **28-scene** outline (four acts, nearly 7 scenes each). I like to follow my outline strictly, so I can write **2 scenes a day.** There! **14 × 2 = 28.**"
- **The seven steps of one day**: ① **read carefully through what you wrote yesterday first thing in the morning**, to "get yourself into the right state"; ② "I treat it as if these next few pages were **the single most important passage of writing I have ever produced**… **full of subtext and grace**, while laying the story out as tightly as I can"; ③ before writing, pick one of three: **imagine the whole scene / jot the idea down while walking / close your eyes and wait for the characters' voices**; ④ **write 2 or 3 pages and then stop**; ⑤ the reason (the key to the whole method): "**forcing yourself to write reduces the quality**" — rest, eat lunch, run an errand, exercise, make phone calls, **physically leave it**; ⑥ **later in the day (afternoon or evening) reread and polish the scene you wrote**; ⑦ repeat ①–⑤ for the **second scene**, and stop when it is done.
- **The result (the whole payoff)**: "by the time I reach the end of the script, **my first draft has already been edited**, because I have been crafting it carefully every day."
- **The opposite temperament and the "painter vs. sculptor" conclusion are in section 5**; only the handling of blank-page fear is added: "**if a blank page frightens you, put something on it — anything.**"

## 23. Beat Sheets and Leave-Behinds in the Flesh: What Six Documents Look Like

> Source: Goldberg & Rabkin, appendices D–I (**2003**). This section takes only the shape and length of the documents and **does not restate the plots**; the corresponding specifications are in section 3 (leave-behind) and section 4 (beat sheet / outline).

**23.1 The headers of the six documents: four names for one kind of file**

| Document | Header (as printed) |
|---|---|
| The Martial Law leave-behind | `MARTIAL LAW` / `"Sammo Blammo"` / **`Story Area by`** / `Lee Goldberg and William Rabkin` |
| The Martial Law beat sheet | `MARTIAL LAW` / `"Sammo Blammo"` / **`Revised Outline`** / `Written by` |
| The seaQuest leave-behind | `SEAQUEST 2032` / `"Depths of Deceit"` / **`Treatment`** |
| The seaQuest beat sheet | `SEAQUEST 2032` / `"Depths of Deceit"` / **`Rough Beat Sheet by`** |
| A Diagnosis Murder one-hour | `DIAGNOSIS MURDER` / `"A Passion for Murder"` / **`First Draft Beat Sheet by`** |
| A Diagnosis Murder two-hour | `DIAGNOSIS MURDER` / `"The Last Laugh"` / **`Rough Beat Sheet – First Draft`** |

**23.2 The five-paragraph functional template of a one-page leave-behind (story area)**
The Martial Law one runs **about a page and a half in five paragraphs**, each doing one job: ① **open on the teaser action and immediately pose the visual problem unique to this show** (the opener: `The story opens with our heroes narrowly thwarting…`); ② **the villain's entrance plus his situation** (one line of backstory); ③ **the villain's trouble is the story engine** (`Only problem is, …`); ④ **why the villain must use the protagonist** — writing the protagonist's expertise as the villain's tool (the sophisticated version of a procedural's "force the case onto the hero"); ⑤ **the device plus the countdown plus a closing line** (the closing line may be a pun on the show's title). → One page holds **three constraints** (it cannot stop, it cannot be dismantled, it has a deadline), and those three constraints are themselves a three-act-plus plot machine.

**23.3 The difference between the two leave-behinds (the thing most worth remembering here)**

| | Martial Law version | seaQuest version |
|---|---|---|
| Length | About a page and a half, five paragraphs | **About two pages of prose, no scene numbers** |
| Running character conflicts | **Not written** — "we didn't deal with the character conflicts that would be running through the story, **just the conflict that drives the episode**" | Written |
| Secrets | Only the hook as the audience experiences it | **Every secret written out** — using `What we will discover is that…` and `What X doesn't know is that…` (specification in section 3) |
| Intended reader | Enough for a producer to sell the story to a network | For a decision-maker to see the hierarchy and the manipulation |
| The authors' noted risk | **Not enough for a freelancer trying to win an assignment**: "The network takes it on faith that the character conflicts will be spelled out in the beat sheet, but **a producer won't grant a freelancer the same benefit of the doubt.**" | — |

**23.4 The two beat sheets' formats compared (one kind of document, two showrunner styles)**

| | Martial Law (appendix E) | seaQuest (appendix G) |
|---|---|---|
| Scene marking | **Full slug plus a scene number inside the act** (`1. EXT. OIL REFINERY – NIGHT`) | **Location in caps only, no scene numbers** (`OCEAN –`, `NEW ORLEANS –`, and even `SOMEPLACE –`, `SOMEWHERE –`) |
| How each scene is written | One to three paragraphs per scene, with key lines and camera direction (FADE OUT, POV, SHIFT PERSPECTIVES) | One paragraph per location, mostly behaviour and information |
| Length | About **15 pages** | About **6 pages** |
| Act marking | TEASE plus four acts, **with END OF ACT after each** | TEASE plus four acts, **act headings only** |

→ **At the "rough beat sheet" stage you may even write the location as SOMEPLACE**: lock the order of power moves and information reveals first, and leave refinement to the next draft. The authors' own characterization (the introduction to appendix G): **"Showrunners have their own styles, which can be reflected in their beat sheets, too."**

**23.5 Three measured sets of scene distribution (calibrating section 4's "6–7 scenes an act, four acts")**

| Document | Scenes per act | Total | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Martial Law's "Sammo Blammo" (action buddy show) | TEASE 2 / ACT ONE 5 / ACT TWO 8 / ACT THREE 5 / ACT FOUR **10** | About **30 scenes** | **An action show's later acts fragment markedly** (10 scenes in Act Four, mostly short cross-cut scenes) |
| Diagnosis Murder's "A Passion for Murder" (one-hour whodunit, **shot**) | TEASE 1 / ACT ONE 6 / ACT TWO 7 / ACT THREE 6 / ACT FOUR 6 | About **26 scenes** | The closest fit to "6–7 scenes an act"; **no tag, with the last scene doubling as one.** (The notes transcribe one or two more entries than the marked scene count; the file's own count is authoritative.) |
| Diagnosis Murder's "The Last Laugh" (two-hour) | **Continuous numbering 1–44 across acts**: ACT ONE 1–5 / TWO 6–15 / THREE 16–23 / FOUR 24–27 / FIVE 28–32 / SIX 33–36 / SEVEN 37–40 / EIGHT 41–44 | **44 scenes / eight acts** | **Eight acts, no TEASE marking**; locations in caps, one paragraph per scene |

- **The format of the one-hour document**: slugs (`INT. COMMUNITY GENERAL – ER – DAY`) but **no scene numbers**, one or two paragraphs per scene, **everything in the present tense**, stating behaviour and key lines, four acts plus a TEASE.

**23.6 How much a first-draft beat sheet may leave blank (the most reassuring practical finding here)**
Both Diagnosis Murder documents expose the real state of a first draft — **blanks are allowed**: you may write **"Brilliant farce to be figured out."** (with only the known outcomes after it: the set-up happens, the money box appears, everything goes wrong, the specific farce left to the script stage); you may write **"(or something like that)"**; you may write **"(or whatever the game show is called)"**; you may write a location as **SOMEPLACE.** → **A beat sheet's job is to lock the order of information and the position of the act outs, not to finish the script**; but **act outs, the planting and paying off of clues, and the characters' turns must be nailed down.**

## 24. Julian Fellowes's Rules Distilled From 419 Footnotes (with the decision type of each)

> Source: the **419** Fellowes footnotes in the `Downton Abbey: Series Two` script collection. The table gives each rule and **which kind of decision it is.** Five categories: **[craft]** (a pure writing choice), **[editing]** (where the finished episode differs from the script, decided in the edit by consensus), **[research]** (historical, legal or medical verification), **[actor-driven rewrite]** (performance quality, availability or physical characteristics rewriting the script) and **[production cost]** (the money for locations, sets, props, animals). Items 18b and 19 already appear in sections 15 and 16 and are only categorized here.

| # | Rule (Fellowes's own words, distilled) | Category |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **"The Downton dilemma = both sides are right"** (the most frequently repeated self-description): "a typical Downton argument, where hopefully you're on both sides." | [craft] |
| 2 | **No character is entirely unsympathetic**: "There are no entirely unsympathetic characters in Downton. But there is always a sub-text, or at least I try to suggest one." | [craft] |
| 3 | **Open each episode with two or three lines restating the lines in play**: "never assume that they will remember every detail of an earlier episode, because they won't." | [craft] |
| 4 | **One ensemble scene advances six or seven lines, each getting a single line**: "the audience has to store it all and then put the plots together at the end." | [craft] |
| 5 | **Let the audience know more than the characters**: "you engineer uncomfortable meetings, and then you let the audience fill in the detail." | [craft] |
| 6 | **Once the audience knows, you need not show them**: withhold dialogue at the moment a truth is heard; leave sex and violence to the audience's imagination — "owes more to old Hollywood than modern television" | [craft] |
| 7 | **Upstairs and downstairs emotional stories get equal weight** ("giving them both the same attention"), with **mirrored pairings** so the same thing is said once in each register | [craft] |
| 8 | **Deliberately stage scenes between two people who would never otherwise meet** ("there is no greater distance at Downton than that between the Dowager Countess and Daisy") — permitting a little **institutional irregularity** for it, with the reason stated in the footnote | [craft] + [research] |
| 9 | **Three staging devices**: the moment the whole company rises after dinner can recombine pairings; the segmented day of a shoot likewise "can bring characters together or push them apart"; **passage acting** in the corridors runs several lines in parallel | [craft] |
| 10 | **The retention premise of a workplace drama**: "One of the basic questions of any drama is: **why do these men and women stay?**" — the answer is that it is their livelihood, so every so often remind the audience that this is a workplace with a budget, wages and ranks | [craft] |
| 11 | **"Truth reminders"**: plant, at intervals, a line of truth that punctures the warmth ("Who is Daisy?"), so the ensemble does not become a love-in | [craft] |
| 12 | **Three rules for killing characters**: you cannot have no regular ever die; the one who dies must be familiar and loved — "**It's like a whodunit. The killer has to be someone who was there all the time.**"; and **deliberately misdirect the audience toward the wrong person** | [craft] |
| 13 | **A season finale must give a reason to come back**; the last act is pure pay-off plus **"streamers"** — "we won't resolve this tonight, but if you keep watching, at some point we will." | [craft] |
| 14 | **Judging when lovers unite**: "you keep them apart and keep them apart, but… there comes a time when the audience thinks, oh, for Christ's sake, are they going to get together or not?" | [craft] |
| 15 | **"drama requires a logic not always supplied by truth." But history and medicine must be verified** (the collusion revoking a Decree Nisi, spinal shock vs. transection, the brachial plexus, Spanish flu killing the young), and he prefers references that **"look implausible and turn out to be exactly accurate"** | [research] |
| 16 | **Do not give period characters modern attitudes**: "It always slightly annoys me when characters in period drama, in order to make them sympathetic, are given modern attitudes and modern choices." | [research] |
| 17 | **Put it in without explaining it**: "the style of Downton… refer to people and events… without making a point of explaining them." | [craft] |
| 18 | **Production reality rewrites the script, and he records it as normal**: rolling up Highclere's carpets requires **the owner's approval**; **there is a ceiling on dining-room scenes** (Carson and the footmen cannot stand that long); a **convertible stage bedroom** is kept available (no ceiling, movable walls, hence overhead shots); **actor availability → scenes cut or written out**; **an actor's physical marks → a vaccination scar removed in post** | [production cost] + [actor-driven rewrite] |
| 18b | **Cut the dialogue when the actor plays it well enough**: "A good actor's skill will always obviate some of the dialogue." (see section 15) | [actor-driven rewrite] |
| 19 | **Editing is a three-way consensus, and the author often loses and admits he was wrong**: "the consensus went against me… **I think I was wrong and they were right.**"; what goes is mostly **expository dialogue** and small actions "adding a new colour to a character," and he **records what was lost every time** (see section 16) | [editing] |
| 20 | **American broadcast has no concept of a "special"**: the Christmas special has to "play either as the end of a series, or as a stand-alone" | [production cost] (distribution/scheduling constraint) |
| 21 | **Write a character's opposition as an institution, not as malice**: documentary evidence is required, an illegitimate child has no legal claim on the father, a scandal is bought off, a Decree Nisi is revoked — **the resistance is supplied by rules, and the antagonist is merely someone who knows the rules** | [research] + [craft] |
| 22 | **The joke and the exercise of power in the same line**: the Dowager's lines are frequently both a **scene button and an exercise of power** ("I hope it is not vulgar in me to suggest that you find some way to overcome your scruples." / "I have a cold.") | [craft] |

**Category counts and the inference**: of the 22, **14 are [craft]**, 4 are [research] (2 of them shared with craft), 2 are [production cost], 2 are [actor-driven rewrite] and 1 is [editing]. → **The value of the footnotes lies not in "how constraining production reality is" but in the repeated self-description of "both sides are right"**; and yet **for every forced change he wrote down the loss** (item 19), which is a directly copyable working habit: **a change log with two columns — what changed / what was lost.**

## 25. First-Hand Revision Evidence in the Succession Footnotes: the Migration Chain of Cut Scenes

> Source: Armstrong's footnotes in the Faber edition of the Succession scripts (which print **the scripts as they stood when shooting began**, "as they stood when we started to film"). In the `Note on the Text` he gives three reasons why the script differs from the finished episode — **editing choices / new lines the writer added on set / actor improvisation** — and says **footnotes are added only where the reason for a change is not self-evident.** The two S1 examples (the Shiv album scene → stitched into S2E8 'Dundee'; Tom's "You're fired. Follow the dream" shot for S1E3 and S2E2 and never aired) are in section 16; this section gives the S2→S3 chain.

| # | Original position | Where it went | The footnote's wording / reason | The working method it proves |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **S2E10** Roman–Gerri's restaurant scene ("It will be incredibly brief" / "Maybe I'll throw you under the bus?") | **S3E1**, the Roman–Gerri scene at the airport hotel | Cut because the episode was overloaded, but "we kept its **substance** for a Roman–Gerri scene at the top of the next season, **when the episode's resting heart rate is a little lower**"; the S3 footnote records that this scene was **"first written for episode ten of season two"** and in S3E1's flow "**find[s] a spot to nestle more comfortably**" | **Cross-season reuse of material**: a good scene is not thrown away, it waits for a structural gap with a lower resting heart rate |
| 2 | **S2E10** the four siblings playing cards ("We knew." / "Well I did not know." / "We kind of knew.") | **S3E2** | On the S2 side: it "**did not fit the very particular rhythm of this episode's ending**," but "we kept that dynamic and that argument… **the emotional temperature probably lets it land better**"; on the S3 side: this **"wolfpack-speculation material"** had been tried in S2E10 and **felt redundant**, and here it is "**more germane and sprung-in**" | **The value of the same scene depends on whether enough pressure precedes it** — the scene was not bad, the position was |
| 3 | **S3E3** Tom saying "Divorce" outright to the receptionist | **Cut**, leaving only the lawyer's name **Rex Hendon**, paid off in **S3E9** | The explicit line was cut because it "**cast too heavy a shadow over subsequent events**"; an attentive viewer can discover in the finale that Rex Hendon is a divorce lawyer | **Dim the brightness of a plant**: too bright and it gives the finale away early; the same plant can survive as a single proper noun |
| 4 | **S3E8** the entire **nanny-firing** strand (Rava's reminder, Kendall asking Iverson, firing Bianca, Bianca leaving) | **Deleted entirely**, not migrated | **"We had to cut the whole nanny-firing-game strand from the episode. It was a tough one to see go but the episode was just too long."** | **Length is a hard constraint, so cut the most complete but most separable strand** — complete so it comes out cleanly, separable so it does not drag other lines down |
| 5 | **S3E9** the long Marcia–Shiv exchange | **Cut**, not migrated | **"I would love to have included this Marcia–Shiv exchange, but the velocity required by the gathering storm of the end of the episode meant we had to lose it."** | **A finale's acceleration outranks the quality of an individual scene** — good scenes get cut from a finale precisely because they decelerate |

**A sixth piece of evidence in the same set of footnotes (not an editing decision but an improvement on set, recorded here so the table above is not misread)**: the footnote beside the last line of S3E9 — **"The end of this episode is a good example of how scripted scenes get improved by the opportunities afforded on set. At script, I didn't know what the final image would be."** On set, the director Mark Mylod and two actors found a "much stronger final image" together. → **The script's job is to make the emotion and the alignments clear, not to lock the shot; the final image can be left to the floor.**

→ Combined with Fellowes's practice in section 16, this is the complete working method: **Armstrong decides where a cut scene goes; Fellowes records what a cut scene lost. Do both.**

# Sitcom and Half-Hour Comedy

The core proposition: **funny is not something you stuff into the lines afterwards, it grows out of the story premise.** A comedy writer layers tension on three levels — premise → sequence/scene → dialogue/action; comedy planted at an earlier level will "automatically produce more, bigger and more seamless laughs" at the later ones (Smith, *Writing Television Sitcoms*). So the first move is not to think of jokes but to ask whether **this premise is itself a joke-producing machine.**

Writing a half-hour is not scaling down an hour's parameters; it is **a wholesale switch**: page count, layout, scene count, joke density, structural unit, and the operating logic of the character relationships all change together. This skill covers that switch. General method is not rewritten here — dialogue is in `sw-dialogue`, the scene value turn in `sw-scene-craft`, the hour's act structure and act-out taxonomy in `sw-series-structure`, engine and bible in `sw-series-engine-bible`, the full picture of the room in `sw-writers-room`, and the Fleabag episode tables in `sw-series-case-studies`.

Format specifications, the joke-technique catalogue and structure templates are in [reference.md](reference.md) in the same directory.

## 1. How Half-Hour Comedy Differs From the Hour: the Switch Table

| Dimension | Half-hour comedy | One-hour drama | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pages | film/single-cam **27–30**; tape/multi-cam **45–50**; animation **40–50** | 48–63 | Smith, Appendix A; Landau 1e |
| Acts | cold open + 2 acts (multi-cam) or 2–4 acts (single-cam) + tag | teaser + 4/5/6 acts | Landau 1e Tip 21 |
| Act length | teaser 30–60 seconds; each act 10–12 minutes; tag ≤1 minute | each act 10–15 minutes | Blum |
| Act One | **very short (1–5 pages out of 30)**, the characters and world already established | about a quarter | Smith |
| Scene count | multi-cam **15–20 scenes** an episode, 3–5 an act, long scenes; single-cam many short scenes (HIMYM often 40–50, sometimes 80) | depends on format | Landau 1e Tip 21 |
| Sets | **3 ideal (1 main + 2 secondary), 5 the ceiling** | many more | Blum |
| Structural unit | beats: main line 5–9, subplot 3–5, each thread 3–6 | scenes and act outs | Smith |
| Joke density | **2–4 real jokes a page**; on multi-cam almost every line is expected to land one | still needs a comic viewpoint | Smith |
| Stakes | small but vital to the character (**a "tremendous trifle"**); "sitcoms are smaller" | life and death / career / institution | Landau 1e; Smith |
| Silence | on multi-cam, silence is dead air; on single-cam it can be cringe "but in a good way" | silence is a resource | Landau 1e Tip 21 |
| Character growth | within an episode "selfish → realization → lesson learned… reset by the next"; long-term by **expansion** rather than change | arcs across seasons | Smith; Lloyd |

1. **The engine is relationship density, not events.** Blum records producers being fastidious about "how intimate the characters are with each other," because that is what lets a comedy develop steadily. Episode one of Fleabag has no "event that pushes the protagonist into a new situation," it merely lays out a web of people already hurting each other.
2. **Multi-camera and single-camera are two different writing actions, not two layouts.** Multi-camera is like a stage play: few scenes, long scenes, and you must constantly design entrances and exits on the same set (Michael Patrick King thinks **multi-camera is harder**, since single-camera "could just CUT TO: the next scene"); single-camera is "a little feature a week," running on fragmentary scenes and cross-cutting, and leaning more on visual jokes. Some executives see "HUGE creative differences" and will only read single-camera specs when hiring for single-camera — **have a sample of each.**

## 2. Premise-Driven Comedy: the Three Levels

Three sentences (Smith): **① weave the funny elements into the story premise; ② exploit and compound them at the sequence/scene level; ③ grow the dialogue and action out of the humour the story already carries. "It's all about building rather than repairing."**

### 2.1 Level One: the Four Comic Sources in a Premise

**Predicaments / character mix / style of comedy / casting.**

**A predicament is a joke-producing machine.** A predicament inside a scene lasts a few pages; a continuing predicament woven into the premise keeps producing jokes until it is resolved, and **an unresolved predicament makes the audience watch in anticipation, which makes even unrelated jokes land better.** A permanent series predicament (Gilligan's Island's "no phone, no lights, no motorcar") is the first seam to mine for episode stories. The preconditions: the audience must believe the characters will not really be hurt, and must have been told "this is a comedy."

The **nine classic predicaments** and **six character mixes**, with names, original examples and limits, are in [reference.md](reference.md) §2.

### 2.2 "Every Show Is Its Own Genre"
Subcategories (domestic, odd couple) are not genres: Two and a Half Men and The Simpsons are both family shows and are completely unlike in their attributes, because **each show's premise specifies a set of attributes the audience expects.** Before writing for an existing show, answer the style questionnaire: does the humour come mainly from the predicament, the relationships or the environment? Belly laughs or smiles-and-nods? Primarily visual or primarily verbal? Does it end in an explosive comedy run, or stay even throughout?
**Two beginner errors**: **Too Funny to Pass Up** (however funny, throw out an idea that does not belong to this show); and **the genre shuffle** ("blending genres throughout is not the same as shifting"). The ideal episode **"expands (but does not change) a show's premise."**

### 2.3 Level Two: Compounding at the Sequence and Scene Level

A sequence is a run of continuous scenes carrying one sustained action; a scene is one place and one stretch of time, and a change of either starts a new one. Three moves:
① **Compound story predicaments** — compound them at the story level, and the tension "does not double, it rises exponentially"; you may stack the same kind (Big Lie → Bigger Lies) or Mix and Match; predicaments at this level are "more finite, occur in real time," appearing, developing and resolving inside one continuous passage. ② **Stir up the character mix** — form a temporary "unnatural alliance," so the audience waits at once for the story predicament to resolve **and** for the alliance to collapse, restoring the original proportions at the end.
③ **Three reminders**: **escalation** (build to the comedy climax at the end; many shows fix a raucous comedy block at the end of every episode); **look to the series premise** (the best stories come from the protagonist's foibles); and **be consistent** (check the joke strength of each passage before writing the dialogue — by the time you are in love with your own line, it is too late).
The character basis (Sheldon Bull): sitcom characters "have no self-edit button," act more impulsively, and react enormously to problems an ordinary person would shrug off.

## 3. Joke Mechanics (Level Three)

The model: a **setup** (straight line) introduces the subject and hints at some complication or incongruity → creating tension; the **punchline** resolves it with a turn from an unexpected direction → releasing the tension. "Setups are the true unsung heroes." Smith's qualification must be kept with it: the greatest value of this vocabulary is in **fixing and revising, not in creating** (editing and repairing, not creating).

### 3.1 Five Rules for Setups

1. **Ordinary and credible** — pull the audience in with a reasonable setup, then hit them with an unexpected punchline; the moment the setup looks odd, the audience knows an ambush is coming.
2. **Two parts**: at minimum ① the subject plus ② **a beat or two of development** (caused by a complication or an incongruity). Without the second part there is not enough tension, and the punchline only reads as further information. Smith's controlled experiment (cutting Jack's long dissection of Liz's life in the 30 Rock pilot down to one line) concludes that it is "still funny but not as funny, and it reads as more openly hostile, and less true to character" — **the development section determines both the joke's strength and the character's truth.**
3. **It can be broken up and planted far ahead**: the parts may be scattered through a scene or planted several scenes earlier.
4. **Never telegraph**: "You brought a whole beer keg." → "Yeah. Where can I fill it up?"; add one more line — "Is that thing full?" — and the joke dies.
5. **Life itself is half a setup**: shared everyday suffering and shared cultural experience can serve as the first part.

### 3.2 Six Rules for Punchlines

The definition: "a sudden twist that makes both sense and nonsense" — it resolves the setup, but from outside the direction the audience was led to expect.

1. **Last things last**: put the punch word at the end of the sentence. "I think that he thinks it's a paper for people who work. Daily." beats "it's a daily paper for people who work" by a distance. (The same principle as the periodic sentence in `sw-dialogue`.)
2. **Don't go past the joke**: the punchline usually also ends a speech, and talking on after it will crush it. The exception: rambling is this character's rhythm.
3. **The topper**: one setup yielding two or three escalating punchlines ("Sex." → "It's illegal... Next best thing." → "Torture."). Three limits: **top it twice at most** (any more and the audience notices the machinery); some jokes should be left alone (to carry a dramatic moment, or to control rhythm); and **dramedy generally does not use toppers.**
4. **Running gag / callback**: a complete joke returns 2–3 times; a new punchline referring back to the original is a callback. Three ways: the same character repeating it in a new situation / a different character repeating it / a different character delivering a variant of the same setup. Each return carries a second layer of meaning, and **the last one is often an irony or a reversal for the big laugh.** A terminology note: a running gag is also called a runner, but "runner" also often means "a very small subplot," which need not be funny at all.
5. **Stuck? Go back and change the setup** (swap in a different incongruity, or widen the contrast) — rather than grinding at the punchline.
6. **Go for literal**: take it literally ("Call me a taxi." / "Okay, you're a taxi."). Literal jokes usually come from **either very stupid or very clever** characters.

### 3.4 Character-Specific Ways of Being Funny
Ten comedic nuances: understated response / exaggerated response / irony / nervous babbling / pulls no punches / space-shot POV / wordplay / double entendre (plus two emotion-driven variants). Each is **a character's fixed way of being funny**, not a bag of tricks for the author: **settle which one or two a character uses before you write the dialogue, and give them nothing else.**

### 3.5 Five General Rules

- **Hanging a lantern on it**: have a character shout "That's crazy!" to get an outlandish premise past the audience. Anything implausible must be foreshadowed, and **once you plant a seed you must harvest it.**
- **Bigger really is better**: not telling the neighbour but broadcasting the boss's baldness nationally; not offending one colleague but lecturing the whole office on diversity. As long as it does not violate the character's nature or the show's premise, **think bigger.**
- **Small truths, big laughs**: "Surf's up, Jim. Watch me ride this wave of inspiration! (GRINS, INTO PHONE) Yes, I'll hold."
- **Reiss's three conditions**: every joke must simultaneously **be funny, serve the story, and not make a character unlikeable**; and however hard the position, there is a joke there — "you may have to look for two hours, but you will find it."

### 3.6 The Density Standard

**2–4 real jokes a page.** You implement this not by estimating but by **counting**: circle the real jokes in red pen and fix it where it falls short; count them one by one in the opening scenes — a reader will not be lenient because it is a pilot, and will not wait until page six for "the comedy to really start." At the other end: no producer has ever said a script was too funny, but it must not be **too jokey** — **the quantity and the kind of joke must stay steady from beginning to end.** Audience response comes in three grades, **smile / chuckle / laugh** (physical comedy gets laughs, witty dialogue gets smiles). **Toppers and running gags are the most economical way to raise the joke count.**

## 4. Half-Hour Structure

### 4.1 The Beat Sheet (one page)

**Main line 5–9 beats; each subplot 3–5; a thread structure (the Seinfeld-style four equal lines) 3–6 each.** A beat is a moment, discovery or event that changes the protagonist's goal or raises the tension, usually in two halves, "action → reaction," compressed into one sentence: "Character One blabs secret—claims (new lie) to cover up." Lay out multiple lines separately first, then weave them; **begin every beat with the central character's name** and then rewrite it as their reaction (Ellen Sandler). If the story on this one page does not build, does not follow logically, is not funny and holds no surprise, go back to the drawing board. A terminology flag: on a drama staff, "beat sheet" means something else — a multi-page, scene-numbered, telegraphic outline.

### 4.2 Three Acts and Page Numbers for a Single Main Line

The typical path: the protagonist meets a new problem or opportunity → pursues it by **outlandish** (not reasonable, or there is no story) means → the obstacles escalate and the goal becomes more urgent → at the end it blows back, they are exposed and humiliated, and "learn the error of their ways. Sort of."
**Act One is very short** (the inciting incident inside a beat or two); **Act Two is longest** (half to two-thirds of the whole, "think development, not repetition," usually ending on a big reveal plus a major choice); **Act Three is about a quarter** (accelerate, build to the climax, the confrontation, the truth, the resolution).
**Page counts (film format, 30 pages, single main line): 1–5 / 15–20 / 5–10.** A thread show gives each of four lines about a quarter (7–8 pages), and where the acts fall cannot be predicted.

> ⚠️ **These page numbers and the switch table's "each act 10–12 minutes" are two mutually exclusive schemes, differing fourfold on Act One (1–5 minutes vs. 10–12). The selection rule: settle the act count first.**
> - **Two acts** (cold open + 2 acts + tag, common on multi-camera): 10–12 minutes an act, and Act One is not short.
> - **Three acts with a single main line** (film format, 30 pages): 1–5 / 15–20 / 5–10 pages, with a very short Act One, because the characters and world are already established.
> - **The pilot exception**: episode 1 has to build the world and the characters, so Act One will necessarily run longer than 1–5 pages; **do not fail it on this rule.**
> Diagnostic ⑦ applies only to non-pilot episodes with three acts and a single main line.

### 4.4 Dramatic Structure vs. Broadcast Format

"Act" has two meanings: the dramatic act, and the segment between commercials. **The ad slots do not necessarily coincide with the dramatic turns** — the commercial that follows Act One in the broadcast sense may land in the middle of Act Two in the dramatic sense. What you type as `ACT ONE, ACT TWO` in an outline or script is the broadcast format. The practice: beat sheet first, then in the outline move the key plot points to just before that show's habitual ad slots; it does not matter if they cannot all be aligned, but align them where it amplifies a beat — **a newcomer who writes strong act breaks into a spec gains a great deal of credit.**

### 4.5 Landau's Seven-Step Formula
The seven steps in full (the definition of the tremendous trifle, which act-end the midpoint reversal goes on, the "chase the dog up the tree" image, and the function of the tag) are in [reference.md](reference.md) §3.

### 4.6 The Key Dramatic Moment and the Block Comedy Scene

Matt Williams: every episode must build toward one **key dramatic moment** (a decision, a discovery, a reconciliation), with everything moving toward it and then away from it — and **nine times out of ten it sits inside the block comedy scene.** The corollary: settle that moment first, then decide which scene it lands in; and that scene must simultaneously be the episode's largest comic passage.

## 5. Character: Who Deserves to Be Put in This Machine

- **They need not be likeable, but they must have drive, purpose, a soft spot and a distinctive skill** — at minimum "some small spark of passion." Michael Scott is a self-important and often unkind idiot, but he is the company's top salesman and always wants to do the right thing (and then does not). **"In comedy, it's usually the troublemakers that we like best."**
- **Proximity counts**: the premise **must contain a setting or dramatic device that regularly squeezes the characters together** so they keep colliding episode after episode. You need not build another office or living-room show (single-camera can roam), but this device cannot be missing.
- **Conflict must occur between regulars**: two series regulars arguing about which way the toilet roll should hang will hold an audience.
- **Complementary traits** (Miller): **the situation is not funny, the characters are** — an incubator, an apartment, a hospital, a restaurant are not funny in themselves. The traditional route is the Aristotelian "imitation of men worse than the average" (It's Always Sunny, Curb); the contemporary softening trend is **complementary character traits** (flawed but well-meaning), which are more relatable. Three copyable moves from the Modern Family pilot's character list: **everyone is described in positive terms; any pair of them can generate a joke; and every one of their viewpoints has its own validity.**
- **Ten behavioural character types**: supportive parent / idiot savant / idiot idiot / clown / operator / mentor / confidant / irritant / romantic interest / critic. The qualification matters: these are **temporary behavioural modes, not fixed labels** — Frasier is the supportive parent to his father in one episode and the childlike idiot in the next.
- **Naming supporting characters** (Fleabag): name them by **one characteristic** and never change it (Arsehole Guy, Bus Rodent, Bank Manager, Godmother), and leave the protagonist unnamed. The naming method must itself be the protagonist's ability or her flaw — she can reduce anyone alive to "a bus rodent" or "an arsehole guy" — so a later episode must make her pay for it (the Priest = the first person she cannot label).

## 7. Direct Address and Device Comedy (Fleabag)

Direct address (`(to camera)`) **is not a style but the character's psychological defence**: she woke up one day and found there was an audience watching her, and did the only thing she could — "she put on a show" ("Who is Fleabag?"). That characterization is what lets it become plot in season two. Three basic uses: narration-style lay-in ("You know that feeling when…") / one line of interior annotation mid-conversation (she says A, turns to us and says B, turns back and says C) / a silent glance ("She looks at the camera." as a reaction shot).

1. **Test the device for viability before writing the script.** She could not crack the adaptation problem for a year, until Vicky Jones filmed her on a phone for five minutes talking to the camera in her kitchen, put the phone down, and said "It works." — **treat a device as a testable hypothesis, not a writing decision.**
2. **A triple plus a fourth beat inverted by someone else.** Give the adjective first, then the evidence, and **the evidence is always a notch below the adjective**: "He's a feminist." / "I have a sister."; or "don't say it — don't say it — said it" (three turns to camera within five beats). **Give the inversion to the other character, never to the protagonist.**
3. **Tell the camera only jokes, and people the truth.** Give her exactly one long monologue a season **not addressed to camera** (the confessional), and that is the emotional peak of the whole show.
4. **The device is itself the season arc.** In season one it is an accomplice; the finale inverts it for the first time (she hides from the camera, it chases her into a corner and forces the flashback out); season two lets **one character see it**, escalating step by step: discovery ("Where did you just go?") → interrogation ("it's like you disappear") → ordering her to stop → her pushing it away → her asking it not to follow (the final line is a stage direction written to the camera, "Goodbye."). **Once the device completes that journey, the show is over.**
5. **Visual sentences**: write the trauma as one 2–4 second image (Boo standing across the road, traffic sweeping past) and cut it in at a moment when the protagonist "looks fine" (laughing, having sex, walking down the street), so the audience instantly knows she is not — do not make a character restate it. The director's comment on delivering that image was "that was all we needed."

**The standard beat pattern for an emotional scene is pain — laugh — pain**: the truth → one visual joke or one cruel line → back to the truth. Two accompanying rules: **let the protagonist lose the argument** (after her impassioned case, a prop proves on the spot that she was on the wrong side, and then a supporting character escalates the theme in two lines); and **a finale declaration may be spoken three times and answered in two words**, followed by a seven-beat anti-climax (declaration → a gentle refusal → their gift in return → an everyday piece of bad luck → a purely comic prop → an object connected to the dead → the device says goodbye).

## 8. Dramedy and "Comedy Carrying Tragedy"

**The test for dramedy (Douglas)**: in the 1960s the American networks institutionalized "half-hour = sitcom, one hour = drama," and to this day offices are physically split into comedy and drama sides and you can only pitch to one VP. A dramedy's attributes are continuing characters and story lines with backstory depth and dramatic arcs, **rather than setups and jokes at fixed intervals**; the laughter is wry and ironic. Two rules: **nothing over three minutes in any form can survive joke-to-joke writing**; and **outside children's adventure, nobody can write a straight drama with no comic viewpoint at all, and episodes rushing toward tragedy especially need comedy** (Shakespeare puts a clown before the saddest scene). Douglas's lesson: Frank's Place was forced into being a pure comedy because audiences found it "not funny enough" — **audience expectation is set by slot and format, so a dramedy must actively tell the audience what to expect.**

**Six practices from Succession**:

- **Use a joke to catch the darkest confession.** After Kendall says he killed the waiter, Roman knocks the charge down a grade at a time with jokes (murderer → irresponsibler → "at worst you're a manslaughter-er"), and Shiv follows with "Oh yeah I've killed a coupla kids. Sure. Just little ones."; the script's own note reads **"It is so dark that it is kind of funny."** The mechanism: **acceptance arrives not as comfort but as a cruel family joke, because that is the only language of love this family speaks.**
- **A comic subplot is a thermostat, not a diluent.** Greg's Luxembourg-throne line is inserted repeatedly between the darkest passages — and in the end it is that line that forces Tom's decision. **The test: a comic subplot must also carry a plot function, or it is padding.**
- **Give the series finale one regressive game with no plot advance** (the kitchen "meal fit for a king"): the whole family shares one intention for the first and last time, and **the jokes must be cooperative**; let the one line that tries to be tender be interrupted — that unfinished pause is the trailer for the ending.
- **The ordering rule**: the strongest proposal in the room is always an appeal to reality over the mythic, **losing only occasionally to whatever is funniest.**

## 9. The Sitcom Pilot and the Series Format

**Write a typical-episode pilot by default.** A premise pilot dramatizes "the event that created the show's premise," and the hook is itself material — but **it cannot demonstrate what a normal episode looks like** ("that Martian won't crash-land in someone's yard every week") and it reruns badly. So **most network executives prefer a typical-episode pilot** — a half-hour "just like episode 17, 52 or 89 of this show," giving almost no exposition when new characters appear. Fleabag's first episode is the model case.

The most executable of the nine practices: **be funny on page one, and literally count the jokes in the opening scenes**; **character appeal first** (the lead must be fresh, relatable and appealing); **proximity counts**; **keep it simple and defer exposition as long as possible**; and **know your rights** — "You can't sell what you don't own.": option any source material before you write, and register copyright before you send it out.

**The series format document (10–15 pages, eleven sections)** has three judgements: it must prove **legs** (a premise rich enough to generate a hundred or more episodes); **the premise section introduces each character with a single phrase**, letting action and dialogue define her; and **the format is itself a writing sample** — a document that reads like an annual report will not sell a comedy.
**Pitch execution rules**: pitching an episode, bring **10–15 springboards** (3–5 sentences each, short version first); **pitching a new series, bring only the one big idea plus one or two backups**, and stop mentioning the backups the moment the big idea is accepted. And **"Try to pitch funny—you're supposed to be a comedy writer."** The general pitch document and the three bible tiers are in `sw-series-engine-bible`.

## 10. The Comedy Room

**The basic division of labour**: break story collectively → write the first draft individually → punch it up collectively. **Roundtable writing (tabling)**: 5–15 people revise the draft line by line inserting jokes, with a senior producer adjudicating; some shows hire a **punch-up person.** Five or more passes over a script is unremarkable, and **many writers reckon they are lucky if half their original dialogue survives to the shooting draft.** Process and ranks are in `sw-writers-room`.

**Alts and the candy bag** (Landau 2e ch. 13): the first draft is dissected collectively and new lines and jokes are pitched; the new lines are called **alts / alternates.** Schur's figures: **a script plus alts swells to 50–60 pages and is then cut back to the required 30.** Discarded jokes are stored for future episodes, which Greg Daniels calls **"the candy bag"** (Michael Patrick King's version is "the whipped cream"). **But do not hoard the good material** — Schur: "a loyal audience is a rare commodity"; Daniels puts it as **"Pack the sausage; put it all in."** The middle way: **burn the big structural ideas, store the jokes.**
**Alts on set** (Succession's version, generally applicable): a day or two before shooting, pick 0–15 lines that might be "funnier or better or truer," send them to 3–5 writers for alts, filter the 10–20 options to 5–10 kept in the pocket, and feed them to the actors as warranted after the scripted version is shot.

| Room model | Method | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Parks & Rec (Schur) | Break collectively, each writing their own episode; writing a whole episode collectively hits "diminishing returns" | Ensembles with similar voices |
| Mindy Kaling | Index cards plus a whiteboard; pitch three or four "story area documents" to the platform at the top of a season for approval; assign one or two people per episode to the first draft (seeking a "wonderful patchwork of voices"); once the draft comes in, the script becomes the whole room's property | Wanting to preserve multiple voices |
| Insecure (Issa Rae) | Write every episode collectively, **and read it aloud in parts in the room by writers and producers** (before the table read with the actors) | An author-driven show with a unified voice |
| Schitt's Creek (Dan Levy) | A small room of four to six, together almost throughout, strict A/B/C; **write the beats into dialogue before splitting up the drafts** | Small budget, precise tonal requirements |

**"Writing beats into dialogue" deserves its own line.** When beats stop at "someone gets caught, has to escape the embarrassment, and is finally neutralized by someone else's embarrassment," nobody asks **"what is this embarrassment? How does it serve this character right now? Could this piece of physical comedy carry an emotional function at the same time?"** — with the result that the line feels strange across the whole episode. The fix: once the beats are settled, **go back to the top and write out all the teaser's dialogue to verify it works.**

**Comedy can be written collectively, drama cannot** (Bays): the room rewrites comedy heavily, but "the dramatic moments I try not to mess with or punch up in the room — drama needs to feel personal and cannot be written by committee."

## 11. Disagreements (What to Use When)

| Issue | Side A | Side B | How to choose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can comedy be taught | Landau: structure, subtext and dialogue can be taught, **but funny itself is a gift** — "no one can teach you how to be funny," and only the techniques are teachable | The four showrunners in chapter 13 of the same book give nothing but teachable processes (prequel questionnaires, breaking from emotion, alts/candy bag, beats written into dialogue, in-room read-throughs) | **Process is teachable, joke taste can only be calibrated**; use process to raise the floor |
| Collective writing vs. individual first drafts | Schur: writing whole episodes collectively hits diminishing returns; Kaling: individual drafts for a "patchwork of voices" | Issa Rae: write every episode collectively plus an in-room read; Dan Levy: a small room together throughout | Decide by **the show's voice**: a single authorial voice → break collectively and write individually; a multi-voice ensemble → fully collective is possible, but somebody must adjudicate |
| What a table read is for | Smith / Landau / Lloyd: test the jokes and the rhythm, and revise from it | Dan Levy: test only the structure, **not the jokes** | If the jokes are in the lines → test jokes; if they are in reactions, physicality or the edit → test structure only |
| Must every act end on a cliffhanger | Smith: end each act on a cliff-hanger, and strong act breaks earn a spec credit | Dan Levy: almost never ends on a cliffhanger and never plays will-they-won't-they — "It's emotional stakes" | Ad slots → an act out is mandatory; no ads plus emotional stakes → an "earned moment" can replace it. ⚠️ **When writing a Chinese-language series**: the comparison table in `sw-chinese-series-practice` requires "a big hook at the end of every episode," which points the opposite way from this row. The adjudication is in that skill's corresponding row: an ad-free, emotionally driven half-hour comedy follows this row, but must book a specific debt for payment in the episode outline. |
| Should a newcomer speak in the room | Lapiduss: a newcomer is "almost better to kind of not speak up too much" | Sandy Frank: "in the room your job is to pitch jokes"; Matt Williams wants to see whether you are a punch mind / joke mind / story mind / character mind | **Read the senior producers' signals**: if they pitch nonstop, do not self-censor; if they dislike half-formed suggestions, be selective |

**Currency flag**: Smith is the **2009** revision — the fees (a pilot script minimum = 150% of the episode minimum, $33,349.50 for May 2009–May 2010), the new-media list, and the "broadcast vs. basic cable" landscape are all out of date; the craft (jokes, structure, format, the room) is largely not. Blum is **2001** — animation writers not being fully covered by the WGA Basic Agreement, the $75 annual Animation Writers Caucus fee, the software template counts and the Becker weekly schedule are historical record. Landau 1e is **2014** — "single-camera comedy was not favoured at the time" and Web Therapy's sponsored-web-series model are artefacts of their moment.

## 12. Diagnostic Checklist

**Premise level** ① Is this premise itself a joke-producing machine? If it is only funny once you force wordplay into the lines, go back and change the premise. ② Is the predicament continuing, or good for only a few pages? When does it get resolved? ③ Do any two people in the character mix contribute the same thing? ④ Does this episode "expand but not change" the show's premise? Is there a Too Funny to Pass Up or a genre shuffle? ⑤ Does the premise contain a device that squeezes the characters together repeatedly?

**Structure level** ⑥ Does the main line have 5–9 beats and each subplot 3–5? Does every beat change a goal or raise the tension? ⑦ Is Act One too long (it should be 1–5 pages out of 30)? **Applies only to non-pilot episodes with three acts and a single main line**; two-act shows and pilots do not run this item — see the selection rule in section 4. ⑧ What is this episode's key dramatic moment? Does it sit inside the block comedy scene? ⑨ Is the predicament escalating toward the ending? ⑩ Are the act breaks written strong? Does each act end on a joke or on a new plot point? ⑪ Is each line led by a different character? Is the protagonist driving the story, or being towed by a visitor? ⑫ Do the scenes start late and finish early? Is there a button? ⑬ Are there more than 2 new sets, or more than 5 sets in total?

**Joke level** — **item ⑬ is the standing-set economics of multi-camera stage shooting** (standing sets are built and occupy the stage long-term), and **single-camera location shows do not count that way**: count **shooting locations and company moves** instead — the lobby, the exhibition hall, the office, the storeroom and the back yard in one building are one location, not five sets. The single-camera equivalent test is "does this episode require a new location just for one scene?"
⑭ **Go through with a red pen: are there 2–4 real jokes on this page? On the first three pages?** ⑮ Does every setup have a second development section? Does any telegraph its punchline? ⑯ Is the punch word at the end of the line? Do you stop once it lands? ⑰ Has anything been topped more than twice? Did you use a topper in a dramedy? ⑱ Does the running gag return 2–3 times, with the last one inverted? Have all the planted seeds been harvested? ⑲ Is there a punchline you simply cannot write? → change the setup, or go literal. ⑳ Does every joke satisfy all three of Reiss's conditions? ㉑ Is each character using their own one or two comedic nuances? With the names covered, can you tell who is speaking? ㉒ Are the quantity and kind of jokes consistent throughout? ㉓ Is there a scene that should have used a take, a piece of stage business or a visual joke and was written as dialogue instead?

**Format level** ㉔ Is this show film or tape? Is the page count right (27–30 / 45–50 / 40–50)? Do the capitalization of stage directions, dialogue line spacing, scene numbering and underlined entrances and exits all follow that one system? ㉕ Has the spec picked up scene numbers, a cast list, sound slugs, camera directions, "First Draft" or a date? Are there too many dialogue cues?

**Tone level** ㉖ If writing dramedy: is the laughter wry and ironic, or has it become joke-to-joke? ㉗ Is there comedy in the episode that runs toward tragedy? Does someone catch the darkest confession with a joke? ㉘ Besides regulating temperature, what plot function does the comic subplot carry? (Cut it if it only regulates temperature.) ㉙ How many things does this plot line accomplish at once? **Cut anything doing fewer than two.**

## 13. Workflow: From Zero to a Second Draft

**0 Choose a show and research it** (for a spec): pick a show you genuinely love that has run at least a season and will run again; **a spec written for SHOW X exists to be sent to other shows.** **Reading scripts matters more than watching** — the page and the screen are not the same thing, and your work is on the page. Build Smith's analysis table: the starting page of each scene / a one-line identification of the scene / columns marked A, B and C tracking each line's beats / **underline the last scene before each commercial** to mark the act breaks.

**1 Generate ideas** starting from the protagonist's emotion and goal and asking "what if" ("Dwight and Jim loathe each other — what if a business trip forced them to share a room?"). **What counts is the emotion, not the situation.** When filtering, check **lag time** (will a topical reference still work in four months), do not write celebrity cameos, and avoid holiday episodes.

**2 The springboard (3–5 sentences)**: the first sentence or two give the hook, and the rest imply the funny complications; **you need not give the ending** ("if they want the ending, they can pay you to write it"); and **it must be written in funny language.** The test: if a few lines cannot capture the essence, the concept is too muddy — and a muddy concept will not clarify itself.

**3 The beat sheet (one page)**: generate it at the beat counts in 4.1; lay out multiple lines separately and then weave. Self-check: does it build? Is the logic sound? Is it funny? Is there surprise?

**4 Add the comedy at the premise level**: with the beat sheet in front of you, ask which kind (or which compound) of predicament this is; at which beat the character mix gets stirred; whether the style of comedy matches this show. **If you cannot answer, do not go on.**

**5 The outline (5–7 pages single-spaced, or 8–12 double-spaced by the show's convention)**: **"An outline is a selling tool."** Ask of every detail, "does the reader absolutely need this to follow the story?" If not, cut it — **the beginner's tell is being too eager to educate the reader.** Arrange it in broadcast format, moving important plot points to just before the breaks. Hook the reader with at least one line within two or three pages; keep scene descriptions brief and **containing the jokes we will see**; **play it, don't say it**; "this will be very funny here" does not count; dialogue fragments must be extremely short and extremely good (do not spend all your jokes).
Take Matt Williams's warning as a health check: **nine times out of ten, an outline collapses two-thirds of the way through** — you can set up and you can complicate, and at two-thirds you do not know what this episode is about. So **when the outline is done, turn to the two-thirds mark and ask "what is this episode about?"**

**6 The first draft (30 pages, about 1–2 weeks)**: do not carve page one on day one — "blair through the script," writing two or three scenes or even the whole of Act One in a day. **Before any big change, go back and redo the beat sheet and the outline.** The first thing to do when you finish is **count the jokes in red pen.**

**7 Revision (the priority order cannot be inverted)**: **story → sequences → scenes → moments → dialogue, watching the comedy at every level**; reverse the order and you get "a bad patch job." **Say nothing while listening to feedback** — if you have to defend a creative decision, it probably needs another look. Handling notes: **a vague note usually points at the wrong place** ("the basement scene isn't funny" may really mean the predicament was not established three scenes earlier); what you bring back must reflect what was said in the room; and **the greatest sin of a second draft is laziness.**
The delivery timetable for each freelance stage (spoken pitch / outline / revised outline / first draft / second draft) is in [reference.md](reference.md) §5.

## 14. Exercises

1. **Count the jokes**: take a real script of one episode, circle the real jokes page by page, work out the density, and label which kind each is (setup-punchline / topper / running gag / take / stage business / visual / sound). Then do the same to your own draft.
2. **Fix the setup**: take your three weakest punchlines and, **without changing the punchlines, change only the setups**, and see whether six new punchlines grow out of each.
7. **Device test**: for any unconventional narrative device, film five minutes of yourself performing it on a phone first, and confirm "it works" before starting.
10. **Punch-up simulation**: write 10–20 alts for someone else's scene, then filter them yourself to 5–10; the rejects go into your candy bag.

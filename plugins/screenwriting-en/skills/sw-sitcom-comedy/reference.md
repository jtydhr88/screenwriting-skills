# Half-Hour Comedy: Format Specifications, the Joke-Technique Catalogue, and Structure Templates

> Use alongside SKILL.md.

## 1. The Three Formats, Rule by Rule (Smith, *Writing Television Sitcoms*, Appendix A)

| | film / single-cam | tape / multi-cam | animation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pages | **27–30** (one page ≈ one minute) | **45–50** | **40–50** |
| Stage directions | Mixed case | **ALL CAPS**; on entrances and exits, **underline the name and the verb** (`JAKE ENTERS`) | Mixed case, single-spaced, ending at character position 70, a little camera direction allowed |
| Dialogue blocks | Single-spaced | **Double-spaced** | Double-spaced |
| Dialogue cues | Lower case in parentheses | **ALL CAPS in parentheses, set as another line inside the speech** | As tape |
| Scene numbering | None | **Lettered or numbered in sequence except the cold open and tag** (`SCENE A`), and most producers want each scene to start a new page | None |
| A new character's first appearance | Name in all caps | Name underlined | All caps |
| Transitions at scene end | Usually omitted | **Usually ends with `CUT TO:` / `DISSOLVE TO:`**; transitions in all caps **and underlined** | Headings and transitions in bold |
| Margins (character positions, 12pt Courier New, shifted 6 positions right for binding) | Action **16→75** / dialogue **28→63** / cue **34→57** / character name **from 40** / transition **from 60** | Action **16→70** / dialogue (including cue) **26→60** / character name 40 / transition 60 | As film, with stage directions ending at 70 |

1. **The markers are fixed**: `FADE OUT.` → two blank lines → centred, underlined, all caps `END OF ACT ONE` → **the next act starts a new page** with `ACT TWO` → two blank lines → `FADE IN:` → one blank line → the scene begins; the tag is treated as a new act (a new page reading `TAG`); the end reads `END OF EPISODE`. If the first scene is a cold open, the scene after the first commercial is Act One, and you write `END OF COLD OPEN` first.
2. **What a spec deliberately omits**: scene numbers, a page header naming the draft, a cast list under the scene heading, separate sound or effects slugs, camera directions, the words "First Draft," and a completion date — adding them slows the read and suggests this is a production draft already punched up by a staff.
3. **Avoid dialogue cues where you can**: professionals barely use them, trusting the director and the actors.
4. **Set economics**: aim for **3 sets** an episode (1 main + 2 secondary), with 5 the ceiling (Blum). ⚠️ **This is the standing-set economics of multi-camera stage shooting and does not apply to single-camera location shows** — on single-camera, count shooting locations and company moves instead (several spaces inside one building count as one location). A multi-camera show typically has 3–5 **standing sets** plus one or two **swing sets**; a spec adds one or two new sets at most. A Hawaii or Italy episode is a staff stunt for sweeps, and writing one into a spec proves you are "out of the loop or just not very bright."
5. **Two animation rules**: act counts are not standardized (The Simpsons uses 2, King of the Hill and South Park 3), so check your target show; the layout is closer to the single-camera feature format, with only the double-spaced dialogue left as a sitcom trace. The world rule (David X. Cohen): **an absurd situation plus realistic human reactions is the engine; the absurdity must not spread into the characters' reactions.** Groening's warning: do not assume that because it is animation you can do anything.

## 2. The Nine Classic Predicaments and Six Character Mixes

**Nine classic predicaments**: ① The Big Lie; ② The Big Secret; ③ The Misunderstanding; ④ A Rock and a Hard Place; ⑤ The Surefire Scheme; ⑥ Something That Rocks the Boat (an outsider threatens the status quo → a showdown → the outsider leaves); ⑦ Time Bomb; ⑧ Trapped in a Space ("It's a pressure cooker."); ⑨ Sex; and a tenth, The Dead Body. Listing them **is not an invitation to repeat them, it is a demonstration of how to weave comedy into a premise** — "as long as you use them freshly and truthfully to character, you can use them again."

**Six classic character mixes**: role reversal / fish out of water / odd coupling / sex (two people urgently need to be together and are always prevented — Cheers weakened once Sam and Diane actually got together, so the producers separated them and replicated the original tension with Rebecca) / **pretense** (pretending to be someone you are not → inevitable exposure and humiliation; Smith calls this "a big one") / plus one category he leaves as an opportunity. One principle: **characters must complement rather than duplicate, each contributing something new.** Jim is not funny alone; the jokes come from his being a foil — sometimes goading, sometimes commenting, sometimes just "reacting as we would."

## 3. Landau's Seven-Step Formula (in full)

① Establish the ordinary world, then break it with a problem (the A-line catalyst, usually the cold open or tease), with stakes that are a **"tremendous trifle"** — something small that is vital to the character; ② after the titles, Act One begins the search for a solution, the B/C-line problems arrive for supporting characters, and the protagonist settles on a game plan; ③ one unifying theme strings the A/B/C lines together; ④ **a midpoint reversal plus escalated stakes — at the end of Act One in a two-act sitcom, at the end of Act Two in a three-act structure**: "trying to fix a small problem (inadvertently) creates a bigger one"; the image is **the protagonist chased up a tree by a vicious dog in Act One, with bystanders throwing rocks at him in the tree in the later acts**; ⑤ from the midpoint, move to all-is-lost (late in the second half); ⑥ at the end of the second (or third) act, overcome the problem through **honest communication and/or amends** ("In movies, the climax = the truth," and the same holds in a sitcom); ⑦ after the commercial, sometimes a very short tag showing the protagonist safe and content back in the ordinary world.

## 4. The Fleabag Six-Episode Season Skeleton and Two Closing Rules

Fleabag runs two seasons of 6 episodes × about 25 minutes, with every episode strictly `cold open (1–3 minutes) → TITLES → 3–7 movements → button (≤1 page, usually wordless)`. The copyable season skeleton: ① lay out every character plus the season's suspense (a typical episode), giving each character one defining line at their first appearance; ② convert episode one's emotion into a followable want; ③ play the first card face up at a family or social occasion; ④ **the bottle episode** — a closed space plus a prohibition, which the protagonist violates one item at a time, each violation a joke and the last an emotional detonation; ⑤ **the false victory at its peak** ("the best day"); ⑥ recall everyone to **a closed social occasion** (an exhibition, a wedding), where every line closes.

Two closing rules: **the finale's button resolves the theme, not the plot** (season 1's is "People make mistakes." plus the pencil eraser); and **one object runs through the season, changing hands once an episode, with its final move changing the protagonist's understanding of the past** (the statue: stolen → sold → a birthday present → confessed → returned → a trophy → a wedding present → "She was based on your mother." → she takes it away again).

## 5. The Freelance Stage Timetable (2009 figures)

Timetable (freelance, 2009): preparing the spoken pitch 2–5 days | outline 2 days – 1 week | revised outline 2–5 days | first draft 1–2 weeks | second draft 2 days – 1 week.

## 6. The Nine Predicaments, Item by Item (Smith 2009, chapter 4)

Each gives four things: **the mechanism / the book's own example (using only shows it names) / the conditions under which it fails.** Three general rules come first, because they govern all nine:

- **General rule A (the preconditions)**: the audience must believe the characters will not really be hurt, and must have been told "this is a comedy." Break either and the predicament immediately becomes drama.
- **General rule B (the shelf life)**: a predicament's productivity is premised on its being **unresolved** — "a continuing predicament woven into the premise keeps producing jokes until it is resolved," and while unresolved even unrelated jokes land better. **So the first failure point of every predicament is the moment it is resolved.**
- **General rule C (paraphrasing the original)**: "the old predicaments recur for two reasons: they reflect human behaviour, and they are funny. As long as you use them freshly and truthfully to character, you can use them again." — these nine are listed not for repetition but to demonstrate how comedy gets woven into a premise.
- Items marked "**no specific limit given in the notes**" are governed only by A/B/C; no limits are invented on Smith's behalf.

**① The Big Lie**
- Mechanism: a moment of weakness produces a lie → the lie grows → collapse.
- Example: South Park's Cartman fakes Tourette's so he can insult people freely, is invited onto *Dateline*, and then starts uncontrollably saying what he actually thinks; at the end Kyle tries to expose him, creates chaos and saves him instead.
- Three variants the book lists: the liar carried away by his own luck (The Simpsons' Bart believes his own press after faking heroism); a moral dilemma; and endings where the character "escapes with dignity and the prize."
- Failure conditions: no specific limit given in the notes. The correct way to add to it at the sequence level is to stack Big Lie → Bigger Lies, not to repeat the same lie at the same level.

**② The Big Secret**
- Mechanism: knowing a secret you cannot tell → letting it slip everywhere → telling it to the one person at the worst possible moment.
- Example: The Office's "The Secret" — Michael promises to keep Jim's crush on Pam quiet and lets it slip twice.
- Failure conditions: no specific limit given in the notes. But the same example demonstrates the **compounding direction**: Jim asks Michael to keep the secret → Michael believes they are now close friends → Jim is forced to hang around with Michael. The secret itself cannot fill an episode; what fills the episode is **the new obligation that keeping it creates.**

**③ The Misunderstanding**
- Mechanism: assembling what you saw into the wrong causal chain and acting on the wrong conclusion.
- Example: 30 Rock's Liz sees her new neighbour Raheem's walls covered in maps, suspects he is a terrorist and reports him to Homeland Security; in fact he is making an *Amazing Race* audition tape; after being interrogated he announces he will "create something spectacular."
- Failure conditions: no specific limit given in the notes.

**④ A Rock and a Hard Place**
- Mechanism: being forced to choose between two good options or two bad ones.
- Example: My Name Is Earl's Earl having to get on stage in tassels to apologize to a stripper he injured; or sleeping with an ex-convict's elderly mother to avoid being killed.
- **A time-bound qualification (a 2009 judgement)**: the protagonist used to end up choosing the honourable side; Smith says it is now equally common to be selfish throughout and lose everything, "**or not lose — the days when selfishness had to be punished are long gone.**" So the old rule "he has to choose right" no longer holds; do not end on it.
- Failure conditions: it does not work when the two options are not equally weighted (once the choice is obvious the predicament disappears) — this follows from Smith's definition and is not stated separately in the book.

**⑤ The Surefire Scheme**
- Mechanism: "Trust me, what could go wrong?" → the plan follows its own logic into disaster.
- Example: in South Park the three of them dye Cartman ginger so he will know what discrimination feels like, and he incites the ginger children to massacre everyone who is not a redhead.
- Failure conditions: no specific limit given in the notes. The adjacent rule: a sitcom character's strategy must be "absurdly stupid," and "sitcom characters almost always ignore obvious solutions"; the moment you let them adopt a sensible plan, this predicament goes out.

**⑥ Something That Rocks the Boat**
- Mechanism: an outsider threatens the status quo → a showdown → the outsider leaves and the status quo returns.
- Examples: 30 Rock's Devon Banks; Everybody Hates Chris, where Greg stays for a week and the whole family dotes on him and forgets Chris.
- Failure conditions (the book gives two in chapter 10, Visiting Characters): **the visitor must not outshine the regulars** ("Not Too Fresh and Exciting" — star agents will call), and **the story the outsider brings must rapidly become the regulars' business**, with their line count controlled. Fail either and the outsider is not a predicament, they are scene-stealing.

**⑦ Time Bomb**
- Mechanism: an impossible task inside a deadline.
- Example: throwing a party while the parents are away, the vase breaks, and the parents come home early. (The book uses a situational example here rather than naming a show — the notes give no title, and none is invented.)
- Failure conditions: no specific limit given in the notes.

**⑧ Trapped in a Space**
- Mechanism: lock incompatible people into one space where each has a reason they must get out. "It's a pressure cooker."
- Example: an elevator plus a pregnant woman or a claustrophobe, with someone due at a wedding.
- **An explicit failure condition (the book's own)**: the related variant "locked outside, naked" **usually sustains only one scene and cannot carry an episode.** By the same logic, a spatial predicament ends the moment "someone gets out" — it belongs to the sequence-level family that is "more finite, occur in real time."
- A close relative: Fleabag's retreat episode (S1E4) is how a half-hour turns this into a whole episode — a closed space plus a prohibition (silence), which the protagonist violates one item at a time, each violation a joke and the last an emotional detonation (see §11).

**⑨ Sex**
- Mechanism: pursuit and evasion, both wanting it and being prevented, the office romance.
- Examples: the book gives situation types in the predicament section; the named shows appear under Sex in the character-mix section (Dwight–Angela, Pam–Jim, The Office).
- **Failure condition (shared with §7④)**: let them actually consummate happily and the machine stops — Cheers weakened after Sam and Diane got together, and the producers had to separate them and replicate the original tension with Rebecca.

**⑩ The Dead Body (Smith's tenth, outside the nine)**
- Mechanism: hiding a body, being suspected of murder, impersonating the dead, being forced to keep the dead person's dog.
- Example: MTM's *The Mary Tyler Moore Show*, "Chuckles Bites the Dust" — a clown dressed as a peanut is trampled by a parade elephant, and the whole episode is a group of people trying not to laugh.
- Failure conditions: no specific limit given in the notes; this one is the most sensitive to general rule A (the audience must be certain nobody is really hurt).

## 7. The Six Character Mixes, Item by Item (Smith, chapter 4)

Two sentences govern all six: **characters must complement rather than duplicate, each contributing something new** (an opposing viewpoint, a talent for trouble, a strange way of living); and **some characters are not funny alone** — Dwight generates jokes anywhere, Jim does not, and his jokes come from being a foil, "sometimes he goads, other times he comments, and still other times he just reacts as we would." The corollary is the general failure condition: **the moment two characters contribute the same thing, the mix is void** (which is what item ③ of the diagnostic checklist asks).

**① Role Reversal**
- Mechanism: being forced into a role that runs against your nature.
- Examples: a single father with three toddlers; taking over someone else's job; impersonating an enemy's spouse. **The big series-level example**: Married with Children and The Simpsons inverted the Cosby-style family into an "anti-family," which made FOX.
- The variant **casting against type**: Christopher Walken as a timid geek on SNL — Smith calls it "role reversal through casting."
- Failure conditions: the reversal must **contradict** that character's nature; once they adjust to the new role, or if the role suited them anyway, the tension goes to zero. (This is the inverse of the definition and is not stated separately in the book.)

**② Fish Out of Water**
- Mechanism: put a character into a world that is not theirs.
- Examples: The Beverly Hillbillies; Entourage (four men from Queens in Hollywood); Weeds (a soccer mom dealing drugs). Smith notes separately that Entourage's series premise predicament *is* fish out of water.
- Failure conditions: no specific limit given in the notes. The adjacent warning: **the real gold is cultural difference, not accents** — let a foreign character be foolish, but do not write them as fools, crooks or sleazebags; South Park's Starvin' Marvin and The Simpsons' Apu are the inverted use, attacking xenophobia.

**③ Odd Coupling**
- Mechanism: bind two incompatible ways of living together.
- Examples: The Odd Couple (fastidious vs. slovenly, cultural snob vs. blue collar); Two and a Half Men's Charlie/Alan; 30 Rock's Liz/Jack. **At the episode level**, bring in a visiting relative, lover or partner of the opposite temperament.
- Failure conditions: when a visitor is used for an episode-level odd coupling, the two Visiting Characters rules apply (they must not outshine the regulars, and the story must rapidly become the regulars' business).

**④ Sex**
- Mechanism: two leads who "urgently need to consummate... but are somehow prevented."
- Examples: The Office's Dwight–Angela and Pam–Jim. Another variant: **a healthy sex life being constantly interrupted** (The King of Queens).
- **An explicit failure condition (the book's own)**: Cheers weakened after Sam and Diane really got together, so the producers separated them and **replicated the original tension with Rebecca.** That is: consummation = failure, and the fix is to rebuild the same tension with a different person.

**⑤ Pretense** (Smith's own words: "This is a big one")
- Mechanism: pretending to be someone you are not → **inevitable exposure and humiliation.** Freud's formulation is having "seized dignity and authority by a deception."
- Examples: Michael Scott (The Office), Johnny Drama (Entourage), Larry David (Curb Your Enthusiasm); Bart cheating his way into the gifted class (The Simpsons); Michael bragging about his gift for improv and ending up the outcast of the improv class.
- **The book's own failure condition**: the worst version is the reunion episode (pretending to be rich / thin / engaged) — but 30 Rock inverts it by having Liz confront the bully from back then and discover that the bully was herself. **That is: the formulaic version of pretense is used up, and the only rescue is to make the protagonist the one exposed.**

**⑥ The sixth: gay identity as a source of tension**
- The book's shortest entry: after Will & Grace there have been "few new shows," and Smith flags this as **"an opportunity for you."**
- **Currency flag (2009)**: this is the 2009 revision's judgement about a market gap, not about craft; using it today requires a fresh judgement against the current landscape, and the book gives it neither mechanism nor example — **so it is not a usable option**: this pack treats the character mixes as **five usable plus one doubtful**, and you should not reach for it just to fill out a set of six.

**Dynamic use of a mix** (at the sequence level, alongside compounding predicaments in §6): form a temporary "unnatural alliance" — Jerry and Newman asking each other for help in Seinfeld — so the audience waits both for the story predicament to resolve **and** for the alliance to collapse, with the original proportions restored at the end ("order (the character mix) was restored").

## 8. The Joke-Technique Example Bank (Smith, chapter 6 and chapter 12, Writing Dialogue)

### 8.1 Ten Comedic Nuances: Character-Specific Ways of Being Funny

Definition and use: a nuance is **this character's fixed way of being funny**, not the author's bag of tricks — settle which one or two a character uses before writing the dialogue, and give them nothing else. The notes transcribe **8 named items** from chapter 12; the book says "ten," and **the remaining two are unnamed in the notes and are not invented here.**

| Nuance | The book's example (named shows and lines) |
|---|---|
| **Understated response** | Darnell (My Name Is Earl), Hank (King of the Hill) — the sky could fall and they stay low-key; Smith's judgement: "incongruous reactions almost guarantee a laugh" |
| **Exaggerated response** | Joy (My Name Is Earl), Tracy Jordan (30 Rock) |
| **Irony** | Ari (Entourage): "Let's hug it out, bitch!"; Johnny Drama: "I've been working steady the past twelve years, minus the last three." |
| **Nervous babbling** | Kenneth (30 Rock, in short bursts); Kelly (The Office) — it has become the character's defining trait |
| **Pulls no punches** | Hank (King of the Hill): "I wouldn't have answered, but you coulda left a message, which I would've quickly erased."; Ari's mailroom line to Adam |
| **Space-shot POV** | Creed (The Office) — "Their words make sense, but only if you live in a different dimension" |
| **Wordplay** | Seinfeld: "How do they do it? How? Do they know people I don't know?..." — repetition, juxtaposition, ambiguity |
| **Double entendre** | Charlie (Two and a Half Men), Quagmire (Family Guy) — hearing someone's innocent line as filth |

**Do not confuse this with the adjacent list**: the same chapter contains a list of "how emotion changes language" (arguing / depressed / happy / fearful / anxious, plus seduce / placate / manipulate / impress / terrorize), which is **the same character deformed by different emotions**, not their fixed way of being funny.

### 8.2 The Example Bank on the Setup Side

- **The two-part setup (the most important item in this chapter)**: the 30 Rock pilot — Jack asks Liz whether she knows the GE Tri-vection oven → "I don't cook very much" (part one: she is trying to block him) → Jack refuses to be blocked and delivers a long, precise dissection of her life ("New York third-wave feminist... Single and pretending to be happy about it. Over-scheduled, under-sexed...", part two, the development, itself containing several small jokes) → Pete: "That's dead on." → Liz: "Are you gonna guess my weight now?" → Jack: "You don't want me to do that." **Smith's controlled experiment**: cut Jack's speech to a single "New York third-wave feminist" before the weight joke — "Still funny but not as funny. Plus, it seems much more openly hostile, which isn't true to the characters." → The development section determines both **joke strength** and **character truth.**
- **The setup must be ordinary and credible**: My Name Is Earl — Randy reads the invitation slowly, "You—are—cordially—invited—to Joy and Darnell's wedding"; Earl looks outside, "I wonder if Willie dropped my invitation in the parking lot"; Catalina: "I don't think so. This says 'Randy Hickey plus anybody but Earl.'" The audience guesses he was not invited; they do not guess "anybody but Earl."
- **Broken up and planted far ahead (three layers)**: Two and a Half Men — Charlie teaches Jake not to apologize sincerely, "just shut up and look sorry. You've got those big cute eyes. Use 'em!" (layer one: a parent giving unparental advice) → several pages later Jake tries the doe-eyed look on his teacher and fails (layer two) → Charlie himself tries the same look on the teacher (layer three, topping). Without the first plant, the other two are "far less funny."
- **Never telegraph (with a counter-example)**: The Simpsons — Homer: "Wow, Barney! You brought a whole beer keg." → Barney: "Yeah. Where can I fill it up?" **The counter-example**: Homer adds "Is that thing full?" → Barney: "No. Where can I fill it up?" — the joke is destroyed.
- **Life itself is half a setup**: the Seinfeld rental counter — Lydia denies the reservation and goes "to find a supervisor," so Jerry performs for Elaine the fake conversation between clerk and supervisor ("Hey Marge, these people think I'm talking to you so just pretend you're talking to me...") and Elaine plays the supervisor, after which Lydia returns with "I'm sorry, my supervisor says there's nothing we can do." The whole passage runs on a shared grievance about reservations being meaningless. Shared cultural experience (famous shows, celebrities, religious rituals, presidents, all of Star Trek) works the same way.
- **Tension can supply both the setup and the punchline (the dramedy joke source)**: the Entourage double date — Johnny brags about landing a woman he has wanted for years and guarantees Turtle will "get laid" (setup) → Turtle's date is loud and unattractive, and the jokes come from expressions and outrageous behaviour rather than lines (development) → Turtle wants to leave, Johnny begs him to stay, and gets one of the rare verbal punchlines, "We're still going to split this, right?" → in the hot tub the two women announce they are swapping, the dream woman leaves with Turtle and Johnny is left with the other. "Setup, comedic tension, and punchline are all supplied by character actions, not dialogue." Smith's own note: the "double date disaster" premise is not new, and it works on titillation plus the characters' shamelessness, so it "seemed just new enough."

### 8.3 The Example Bank on the Punchline Side

- **The punch word last**: The Office — Dwight reads out "the Scranton Christian Science Reader and the Scranton Daily Worker"; Jim's talking head: "I think that he thinks it's a paper for people who work. Daily." Far funnier than "it's a daily paper for people who work." The ancestral example: "Take my wife... please!" dies as "Please take my wife!"
- **Don't go past the joke**: the punchline usually ends a speech too, and talking on will "trample the thing." Several jokes in one speech can be separated by a beat, a pause or an ellipsis, but it is better to trust the actor and the director to find the air. **The exception**: the character is genuinely long-winded, is rambling for plot reasons, or the extra words are "a necessary rhythm to her speech and are true to her character" — then keep them. At the other extreme is the Ellen DeGeneres "more is more" approach — ramble, and then ramble about your rambling.
- **The topper**: The Office — Michael, "find out what motivates people more than anything else." → Dwight (off), "Sex." (the camera widens and he is standing right behind Michael) → Michael, "It's illegal, can't do that. Next best thing." → Dwight, "Torture." Why it works: the jump from sex to torture is "so huge it's completely unexpected," and it is true (torture is indeed motivating) and connects sex with torture; the first joke builds momentum and the audience is hit again before recovering. **Three limits**: top twice at most ("Any more and the audience will notice"); some jokes should be left alone (carrying a dramatic moment, controlling rhythm, or already exhausted); and **dramedy generally does not use toppers** (too conspicuous). A terminology note: say "topper" in front of other comedy writers and they will laugh, but there is no substitute word.
- **A complete four-part running gag / callback**: Two and a Half Men's "Miss Pasternak" — ① Charlie sends Jake away and smiles, "I'm sorry, it is Miss Pasternak, right?" (we know he is deploying his one talent); ② several scenes later she kisses him and he sighs "Oh, Miss Pasternak," she asks why he will not use her first name, "I don't know. This is so much hotter." (he is in control); ③ he tries to drop her, "Listen, Delores, we need to talk." → "Oh, call me Miss Pasternak." → "Uh... not right now." (the situation inverts); ④ she announces God wants her to be "God's instrument" and reform him, "Now, sit up straight and eat your pie." → "All right." → "'All right' what?" → "All right, Miss Pasternak." (CHARLIE GLUMLY EATS HIS PIE — the great manipulator reduced to a schoolboy).
  - Three ways to do it: the same character repeating the original punchline in a new situation / a different character repeating it / a different character delivering a variant of the same setup; each return usually carries a second layer of meaning, and **the last is often an irony or a reversal for the big laugh.**
  - **Series-level standing runners**: Jerry and Newman saying each other's names with loathing in Seinfeld; killing Kenny every episode in early South Park ("viewers were just waiting to see how Kenny would be killed").
  - Terminological confusion: a running gag is also called a **runner**, but "runner" often means "a very small subplot," which need not be funny.
  - The economics: under a target of 2–4 jokes a page, **toppers and running gags are the cheapest way to raise the joke count.**
- **Stuck? Go back and change the setup**: Smith spent several drafts grinding at a weak punchline until a passing producer said offhandedly, "Change the setup?" — and after changing the joke's premise, "half a dozen great punchlines popped into my head." The rule: when a punchline will not work, check whether the setup needs **a different incongruity or a bigger contrast.** Alongside it, Michael Reiss (The Simpsons) with his three conditions plus a promise: "It has to be funny, it's got to serve plot, and it can't make the character look bad... you might work for two hours, but you'll get it."
- **Go for literal**: Helitzer's textbook example, "Call me a taxi." / "Okay, you're a taxi." The example from My Name Is Earl: Randy hides under the table eating bad meatloaf and comes out with "This stinks, Earl." → Earl (indicating the meatloaf): "Hold your nose. It tastes better than it smells." → Randy: "Not the meatloaf. Having to hide." — literal and ironic. The observation: literal jokes usually come from **either very stupid or very clever** characters.

### 8.4 Action, Visuals and Sound

- **Mannerism**: Ray Romano's deer-in-the-headlights on Everybody Loves Raymond can be milked for ten seconds of laughter; Charlie Sheen shrugs his way out of things on Two and a Half Men; Homer's "D'oh!" Some are brought by the actor, some are designed by the writer when building the premise — **when creating a new character, remember to give them one.**
- **Stage business**: an actor fiddling with something while speaking; it can contrast with the dialogue to raise the tension; **it can be a punchline in itself** (finally putting the fragile ornament back on the shelf — and the shelf collapses); it can also be unrelated to the plot, existing only to raise the scene's comic temperature.
- **The take (a look, a reaction)**: a character's startled expression is frequently the entire punchline. A script can write "(reacts)" or "(beat)," but "**better yet, let the actor and the director decide.**"
- **Slapstick**: structured like a verbal joke (setup → payoff). A model setup: a friendly slap → a harder one back → a shove → the two of them wrestling on the carpet (a friendly gesture turning into hostility, true and unexpected). The Seinfeld example: Kramer drops an oil-filled balloon from a high floor to test an invention, Jerry's new girlfriend happens to park at the landing point, Jerry shouts a warning she misunderstands (dialogue developing the setup), and the balloon falls — **not showing the consequence is also a big laugh.** The qualification: "very broad, even unrefined," and only when the show permits.
- **Funny visuals**: a humiliating uniform ("House of Wieners"); a couple eating in a sexually suggestive way; **hiding something** (an ex-girlfriend, a broken vase), where the gap between what is said and what is seen produces small laughs continuously and accumulates into the big laugh at exposure; "But who could possibly be so stupid?" → Stupid enters (worn out, and always usable, because it is a convenient way to bring someone into the scene).
- **Funny sounds**: a scream next door, a crash outside, a fall down the stairs, a toilet flushing at the worst moment, a whisper repeated at volume.
- **Alliteration**: King of the Hill's doctor, "considering you seem like a man who butters his bacon" — try it as "butters his steak." **The qualification**: it rarely produces a laugh on its own, but it preserves comic momentum through a serious passage.
- **Small truths, big laughs**: The Office's Dwight dialling, "Surf's up, Jim. Watch me ride this wave of inspiration! (GRINS, INTO PHONE) Yes, I'll hold."
- **The inverse limitation for a spec**: **do not pile visual jokes into a spec** — someone reading for ten minutes catches verbal jokes more easily, and "a surprising number of industry pros aren't even able to appreciate visual jokes (in a script)"; some executives say they read only the dialogue and not the stage directions. A visual joke is an asset that gets shot, not a selling point on the page.
  - ⚠️ **This contradicts two positions in this same skill**: the switch table says single-camera "leans more on visual jokes," and diagnostic ㉓ asks "is there a scene that should have used a visual joke and was written as dialogue?" **The adjudication (supplied by this pack; none of the three books gives one): write the visual jokes, but never let one be a scene's only source of laughs — leave at least one verbal joke in every scene as page insurance, and keep purely visual jokes under about ten per cent of the whole script.** The cold open is the exception, and may be entirely wordless.

## 9. The Nine Rules of a Sitcom Pilot and the Eleven Sections of a Series Format (Smith, chapter 20)

### 9.1 The Nine Rules of a Pilot Script (in the book's order)

First remember what makes a pilot hard: inside the length of one ordinary episode you must introduce brand-new characters and settings, establish the time frame, comic style, theme and episode structure, tell an excellent funny story, and prove there are legs — **while not letting anyone see you doing any of it, and without overdoing it.**

1. **Read other people's pilots**: find successful shows' pilots and pilot scripts and take them apart as you would a spec target — **paying particular attention to how much exposition is delivered and how**, and identifying the elements that make the show believable as something with legs.
2. **Study the market**: know what is in development around town and which kinds of show appeal to which network; cable buys by demographic (age, gender, ethnicity, education). The book's test sentence: **"You might have the best new space-western concept ever conceived, but it's not likely that Lifetime is going to buy that pitch."** (The buyer map is 2009 and the platform names need updating; the method does not.)
3. **Decide between a premise pilot and a typical-episode pilot** (the book takes a side): a premise pilot dramatizes "the event that created the premise" (a Martian / a caveman / a mermaid crashes into someone's back yard and causes chaos, and ends with that person letting it move in) — fun to write, because the premise's hook is itself material; **the drawback is that it cannot demonstrate what a normal episode looks like** ("that Martian won't crash-land in someone's yard every week"), and **it reruns badly.** "**Therefore, most network executives prefer to see a typical-episode pilot**" — a half-hour "just like episode 17, 52 or 89 of this show," giving almost no exposition when new characters and settings appear, where "less is more as the pilot artfully reveals the minimum amount that we need to know."
   - A worked sample: **Fleabag S1E1** is a pure typical-episode pilot — no "event" pushes the protagonist into a new situation, it simply lays out the characters and their web one by one, with each character's first appearance accompanied by one defining line to camera (see §11).
4. **Character appeal first**: structural weaknesses and jokes that do not land are survivable, but **the lead must be fresh, relatable and appealing** — they need not be likeable, kind, honest or cheerful, but they must have drive, purpose, a soft spot and a distinctive skill, "or at least some small spark of passion." Three examples: Michael Scott (The Office) is a self-important and often unkind idiot, but he is Dunder Mifflin's top salesman and always wants to do the right thing (and then does not); Earl (My Name Is Earl) is a lazy thief with a list of people he has wronged, but he doggedly makes amends out of fear of karma; Cartman (South Park) is vicious and calculating and happiest when hurting people, but pursuing his mad plans he is "incredibly clever and persistent." The conclusion: **"in comedy, it's usually the troublemakers that we like best."**
5. **Be funny from page one**: "the reader will not cut you slack because this is the pilot, and she will not wait until page six 'when the comedy really kicks in!'" — the method is to **literally count the jokes in the opening scenes** and confirm you are not imagining them (the density standard is 2–4 a page).
6. **Proximity counts**: there is a reason sitcoms are usually set at home or at work — "you need your characters to keep bumping into each other so they can keep generating conflict and comedy in episode after episode"; you need not make another office or living-room show (single-camera can roam), but **the premise must contain a setting or dramatic device that squeezes the characters together regularly.**
7. **Keep it simple**: aim for a "dynamic but straightforward plot" with a few surprising turns, fresh humour and a satisfying ending, perhaps with **a small cliff-hanger to make them want episode 2**; introduce the protagonist and the main conflict as fast as possible, and **limit and defer exposition** ("We can learn about Cousin Billy and those nuts at the office another time").
   - A copyable act-break demonstration (Landau 1e Tip 19, item 10): **The Big Bang Theory pilot** — at the end of the cold open, Sheldon and Leonard meet their new neighbour Penny (whose arrival changes their relationship permanently); at the end of Act One, Wolowitz meets Penny too and is smitten, creating competition and leaving "who gets Penny"; the end of Act Two is the dramatic resolution — Leonard helps Penny retrieve her television from her ex's apartment so she can get closure, she is formally single, Leonard is still smitten, and the long-running will-they-or-won't-they is established. Landau's general rule: in a two-act sitcom the break between the acts is critical — **something has upset the balance of the show's world, and Act Two is the effort to restore harmony** (since every sitcom has a happy ending); a three-act sitcom has two act breaks and therefore two mini-cliffhangers.
8. **Know your rights**: **"You can't sell what you don't own."** — to adapt a book, film, comic, article or true story, **option or buy the rights before you write or pitch**; the first question any producer or executive asks about an adaptation is "Do you own the rights?", and if you cannot answer, "you'll look like an amateur and that contact will probably lose your number." With no money, a **free option** is sometimes negotiable for material that is not commercially hot.
9. **Get people to read it** (once the concept has taken shape or the first draft is done, paying particular attention to the logic holes and the passages they did not follow); and **register with the Copyright Office or the WGA before sending it out** — "Unlike an ordinary spec, this is an entirely original work." (Contrast: **a spec of an existing show should not carry a registration number**, since you do not own the premise, characters or sets, and marking it looks "insecure or inexperienced.")

### 9.2 The Eleven Sections of a Series Format Document

The definition: experienced writers sometimes pitch with a format instead of a pilot script — "a brief selling document that describes the series premise and hints at how future episodes would develop"; or take both. **Length is usually 10–15 pages with plenty of white space.**

| # | Section | The book's specification and test |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title page** | Series title, creator's name, contact details for the creator's rep (or the creator); layout per Appendix A |
| 2 | **Sell copy on the first inside page** (optional) | A one-line logline / the protagonist's personal creed / a statement or question expressing the theme / a quotation, to make someone want to keep listening. **Many formats omit this section, and done badly it reads as cutesy** |
| 3 | **Description of the series premise, 1–3 pages** | Characters, relationships, setting, time frame, story type, **the source of continuing conflict.** "It must convince the reader that the show has **legs**, a premise rich enough to generate a hundred or more episodes down the road." One approach is to **carry all that information on the pilot's story** (disguising it in comedy and drama). **Introduce each character here with a single phrase**, letting her actions and lines define her |
| 4 | **The pilot story, 1–2 pages** | If the previous section did not use the pilot. Illustrate the premise with a real story — "it's the best way to prove that the series will actually be funny" — and prove you can write this show |
| 5 | **8–10 loglines / springboards for future episodes** | Prove the legs and hook the reader with funny concepts; a serialized show substitutes **several main story arcs for season one**, or interleaves the two (threading the arcs through the springboard list) |
| 6 | **The regular character list** | A paragraph each, more for leads and less for supporting players, **naming the key relationships that generate conflict and comedy (the character mix)**; detailed character description goes here and must not be pushed back into section 3 (it will sink that section) |
| 7 | **Market analysis** (optional) | Recent ratings performance of comparable shows plus current competition for the same audience. "It's best to keep this section very short and objective," and include it only where the numbers strongly support you; **the book also records the opposing view — some argue for omitting it entirely**, since dryness dilutes the excitement of the creative sections |
| 8 | **Budget summary** (optional, 1–2 pages) | Supplied by an experienced producer, or a cost estimate for the pilot and first episodes; any money-saving production or co-financing approach goes here |
| 9 | **Unique aspects** | A star already attached, a basis in a published work you have optioned, a substantial producing or financing partner. **Warning**: "Do not assume that this (used-to-be-a-star) actor or that (working-his-way-up) producer will help you to make a sale" — if they are not currently in demand, **attaching them will sink your project instead** |
| 10 | **Credits of the creator and anyone attached** | No credits and no names → **drop the whole section** |
| 11 | **Closing sell copy** (optional) | "a corny closer can kill a sale" |

Three overall qualifications: ① this is only one blueprint, and the order and combination may be adjusted per project; ② **the format is itself a sample of your writing** — "keep those adjectives and adverbs at bay," and prove throughout that the show is timely, unique and **funny**, because "**you can't sell a sitcom with a document that reads like an annual report**"; ③ attachments (the article that inspired the show, ratings data, partners' letters of intent) do not count toward the 10–15 pages.

## 10. The Multi-Camera Production Week and the Cast & Set List (three books' data side by side, each dated)

### 10.1 Smith's "Five Days Before the Shoot" Model (**2009**)

| Day | Content |
|---|---|
| **DAY ONE** | The **table reading** — producers, writers, cast, department heads, network and studio executives attend (everyone but the executives around one big table or a ring of tables), the cast reads the draft to be shot that week → notes → the writers rewrite, while the director and departments begin preparing the physical elements. "If the script looks bad, the writers face a very long night." |
| **DAY TWO** | Usually another read and more notes; once the script is acceptable, the director starts blocking cameras and staging, possibly with a full **run-through**; **some shows fix this as rewrite night regardless of the script's state** |
| **DAYS THREE AND FOUR** | The cast learns lines in earnest and the script locks; the director stages and blocks cameras; notes and rewrites continue |
| **DAY FIVE (shoot day)** | Possibly two full passes — a formal **dress rehearsal**, then notes, final changes, perhaps a meal, then the **final shoot**; then into the edit, **assembling the best of the two passes** |

Machines and variables on the same page: multi-camera uses **3–5** cameras shooting the whole episode continuously (stopping only for retakes or set changes); single-camera uses one camera over several days and angles; whether there is a live audience, whether the dress rehearsal is recorded as backup material, and whether canned laughter is added all vary by show. The rhythm is **one episode a week** (with an occasional dark week to catch up), over a production season of **9–10 months.**

### 10.2 Blum's Sample Shooting Week (**the script is dated 9–15 February 2000; the book is 2001**)

Taking Becker's "one angry man" as the example, the draft notes that "the times below are approximate, and exact times will be announced in advance each time":

| Day | Place | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **Wednesday (day 1)** | Cooper conference room → Stage 31 | 11:30–12:00 **production meeting**; 12:00–13:00 **table read**; 13:00 **network notes**; rehearsal from 13:00 |
| **Thursday** | Stage 31 | 9:30–13:00 rehearsal; 13:00–13:30 lunch; 13:30 rehearsal; **producers' run-through** TBC |
| **Friday** | Stage 31 | 9:30–13:00 rehearsal; 13:00–13:30 lunch; 13:30 rehearsal; 14:00 **network run-through** |
| **Monday** | Stage 31 | 9:00–13:00 **camera blocking**; 13:00–14:00 lunch; 14:00 continue; run-through TBC |
| **Tuesday (shoot day)** | Stage 31 | 12:00–15:00 rehearsal; 15:00–16:00 producers' run-through; 16:00–19:00 meal / hair / make-up / wardrobe; **19:00 shoot before an audience** |

How to read it: **network notes arrive on the day of the table read → three consecutive days of rehearsal, each ending in two tiers of run-through notes (producer level, network level) → camera blocking on Monday → recording before an audience on Tuesday night.** That is the external clock explaining "why a sitcom script is rewritten daily and why there must be few sets."

### 10.3 A Third Version: Two Real Weekly Schedules Recorded by Landau 1e (**2014**)

- **How I Met Your Mother (multi-camera, Bays/Thomas)**: break story on the room's whiteboard (sometimes bringing in a rough two- or three-page beat sheet) → the writer goes off and writes a rough outline (shape only) → brings it back to the room to be filled in → goes off for **a week** to write the first draft → delivers, and one of the two showrunners gives notes → a few days for a second draft → back to the room for a collective rewrite → **Monday table read plus rewrite; Tuesday run-through on stage plus another rewrite; shoot from 9 a.m. Wednesday through Friday.** And **the episode's writer always stays on stage** to fix jokes and try alts live. "We are always trying to beat the jokes, find the extra twist or surprise or a way to call something back that we never thought of until Tuesday night before shooting on Wednesday morning."
- **Modern Family (single-camera, Lloyd)**: break story collectively for **three days** → cards on the board → only when they are sure the whole episode works do they say "you can write it" → one person or team goes off to write the outline → **deliberately let a week or two pass** before coming back to read it, with half a day of notes → the writer delivers a script two or three weeks later → the group rewrites for a day or two ("that should be just for refining jokes. You shouldn't be changing story at that point") → table read (about 100 people) → another day or two of rewriting for the final draft. The single-camera time comparison: **"we take eight or ten hours to shoot four minutes worth of a show, so we can stop and refine it"**; multi-camera's opposite advantage is that the jokes have been "sound-tested" through a week of rehearsal, "but you can't stop."

**Keep the three side by side rather than reconciling them**: the shoot day is **one evening on day 5** (Smith, 2009) / **one evening on Tuesday** (Blum's record of Becker in that week of 2000) / **Wednesday through Friday, three days** (HIMYM, 2014, because it imposes a single-camera editing style on a multi-camera form, often running 40–50 and sometimes 80 scenes an episode). Confirm which category your target show belongs to before picking a template.

### 10.4 Page and Act-Length Parameters (three books side by side, **and they conflict**)

| Source (year) | Pages and distribution for a multi-camera / tape half-hour |
|---|---|
| Smith, Appendix A (**2009**) | Tape format **45–50 pages** (because of double-spaced dialogue); film format about **30**; animation **40–50** |
| Landau 1e (**2014**) | Teaser **1–2** | Act One **17–20** | Act Two **17–20** | Tag **1–3** | **total 40–48 pages**; a single-camera sitcom is written in feature format and averages **at least 30** |
| Blum, chapter 11 (**2001**) | Multi-camera tape scripts **40–50 pages**; examples: Frasier, 2 acts, scenes A–H, **about 50 pages** (with no "The End," ending on "End of Act Two"); Friends, teaser + 2 acts + tag, generally **26 scenes** (sometimes lettered A–Z), about **50 pages**; Ally McBeal, 4 acts, scenes A–J, **50–55 pages** (an hour-long written in sitcom style with double-spaced dialogue) |

**Act lengths (Blum, chapter 11, 2001)**: teaser **30–60 seconds**; Act One **10–12 minutes**; Act Two **10–12 minutes**; tag (button/epilogue) **no more than 1 minute**; the whole show **2–3 acts of roughly equal length.** — Note that this is compatible on paper with Landau's "Act One/Two 17–20 pages each" (a multi-camera page ≠ a minute), but incompatible with film format's "one page ≈ one minute": **when you change format, change the page-to-minute conversion rate with it.**

**Set counts (the two books agree, and this is the hardest production constraint in this body of notes)**: Blum records Dan Wilcox's experience — "**three sets is ideal for a show: one main set plus two secondary ones.** Though he has also successfully used five in production"; Smith records that multi-camera shows typically keep **3–5 standing sets plus one or two swing sets** (used occasionally, rolled out from the wings or pulled from storage), that **a spec adds one or two new sets at most**, and that Hawaii or Italy episodes are a staff stunt for sweeps, so writing one into a spec proves you are "out of the loop or just not very bright."

### 10.5 The Cast & Set List: Fields and a Sample (Blum, chapter 11, **2001**)

Its purpose: once the script is shootable, the producers finalize the cast & set list **to determine which characters each scene needs and how it will be dressed.** To save money, **fold props into standing sets wherever possible**, or the producers must book a swing set or a new shooting location. **An explicit exception (the book's own): "but if you are writing a television movie, this kind of list is useless."**

The sample is Frasier's "hot pursuit":

| Field | Sample content |
|---|---|
| Header | **Series title / episode title / episode number** (#40570-163) |
| **CAST** | Leads listed with **character name plus actor name** (the leads are long since cast); **guest and other roles listed too, with actors unassigned** |
| **SETS** | Grouped as interior / exterior / exterior-interior: interior — Frasier's living room / kitchen, a restaurant room; exterior/interior — a van |
| **Act and scene breakdown** (for shooting) | **Act One / Act Two → scene letters (A, B, C, D, E, H) → scene heading (INT/EXT + place + time) → the characters in that scene**; a set change inside a scene is written "change set to: … continuous"; the margin carries **page numbers** (16) (17) (19) (24) (32) (36) (43) and **day/night codes** such as "night/1"; act ends are marked "End of Act One / End of Act Two" |

An episode's "production list" therefore has six fields: **act → scene → set → characters → page position → day/night.**

**Cross-source corroboration**: Blum's fifth multi-camera format rule is "**the characters appearing should be noted before each scene begins**"; Landau 1e's multi-camera format specification records the same thing — "**(CHARACTER LIST) in parentheses directly beneath the slug line, naming the characters this scene requires.**" Two books thirteen years apart giving the same rule can be treated as a stable feature of multi-camera practice. (Note: Smith's Appendix A puts **the cast list on the "deliberately omitted from a spec" list** — it is a component of a production draft. The three do not conflict: leave it out of a spec, and deliver it once you are on staff.)

**Five qualifications to read alongside this table**:

1. **The movement counts do not match the ranges the notes themselves give, and both are preserved**: the season 1 format note says "cold open (1–3 minutes) → TITLES → **3–7 movements** → button," and the season 2 note says "cold open → TITLES → **3–5 movements** → button"; but counting the episodes gives **S1: 4/6/4/5/4/6, S2: 6/5/5/5/7/8.** That is, **the later part of season 2 clearly exceeds its own stated range** (seven movements in E5, eight in E6), with the finale the longest. Use the actual counts, not the stated ones, when treating this as a template.
2. **S2E2 and S2E3 have no separate button in the notes** — their final movements close them directly. No button is invented for them.
3. **Season 2's structural changes**: set a year after season 1; the engine restarts with Dad and Godmother's engagement and the wedding in E6; the flashback body switches from Boo to **the mother's funeral** (E4), with Boo demoted to a 1–2 second quotation (the script marks it `FLASHBACK FROM SERIES ONE`); **every episode contains one long monologue for a supporting character**, fixed in position just before or after the climax (E3 Belinda's "menopause / People are all we've got"; E4 the Priest's chasuble and "castrated himself"; E5 Anthony's "Hair. Is. Everything."; E6 Martin's "I am not a bad guy, I just have a bad personality." and the Priest's homily, "Love is awful!… It feels like hope."); and all the objects settle in E6 — the statue (returned to Godmother → "**She was based on your mother.**" → she takes it away again), the fox (planted in E3 → the last comic shot in E6), the bassoon (Jake's piece is called "Where's Claire?"), Claire's hair extension (butchered in E5 → left on the seat in E6 as she runs for the airport), and the Bank Manager's hamster Stephanie.
4. **Head and tail are isomorphic**: S2E1 (the engagement dinner at the restaurant) and S2E6 (the wedding, entirely at and beside Dad's house apart from the closing bus stop) — **a season's first and last episodes are both a family gathering inside one closed occasion**; season 1's equivalent is E5's lunch on the mother's anniversary and E6's sexhibition.
5. **Anti-climax as a closing technique (the seven beats of S2E6)**: love is spoken aloud (three "I love you"s) → gently refused ("It'll pass.") → returned ("I love you too. Ok.") → the bus reads "Cancelled" → the fox (pure comedy) → the statue (the mother) → the audience is sent away (the device). **Not one of the seven beats is "she gets him," and every beat brings her closer to being able to stand alone.** The device completes its journey from defence to exposure to farewell, which is also the structural reason a third season need not exist (Harry Bradbeer: "If she has something else to learn, then we have more story to tell").

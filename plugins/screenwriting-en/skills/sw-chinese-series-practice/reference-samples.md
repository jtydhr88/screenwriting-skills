## 9. Samples of the Four Chinese Script Formats (Yao Kougen)

This section supplies the physical examples behind the comparison table in §3.2. All four formats come from §2.3 of chapter 2 of Yao Kougen's *An Introduction to Television Drama Writing* (p0063–0081), where Yao borrows the taxonomy from Zhu Baishui's *How to Write a Television Drama* (Taiwan Television Culture Company, 1984) and states flatly that "for the format of the 'body' inside a scene, **there is no unified standard**" (Yao p0064). **Each sample below is transcribed in the layout of the original book and can be copied directly; which one you use depends on who is reading this draft and what they are reading it for.**

All four formats share the same scene-heading rule (Yao p0064, as written): before the body of each scene you mark **the place; interior or exterior; the time; day or night**; "some also mark **the scene number** and **the characters appearing in this scene**."

### 9.1 Format 1: the Picture Track and the Sound Track Separated (Yao p0064–0068)

The method (Yao p0064, as written): **strictly separate the image part and the sound part, writing them side by side left and right** — some divide them top and bottom instead, or set them together but with a clear distinction between picture and sound (the picture track, for instance, wholly marked with a conspicuous sign) — "so that when the director, the actors and the crew hold the script in their hands, **it is clear at a glance and cannot be confused**."

**Sample A (Yao p0064–0065, transcribed as written)**:

```
PICTURE                                SOUND
scene: 5   time: day   set: living room
· The second hand of the wall clock turns. The camera tilts down; the living
  room is silent, empty.
· A woman comes out hurriedly, doing up      · An urgent knocking.
  her buttons as she goes.                    WOMAN: Who is it?
· The woman opens the door in alarm; a       MAN: (off) Me, open up, quick!
  man rushes in, tense.                      · Sirens and footsteps in the
                                               distance.
· The man seizes the woman and they flee in a panic through the side door.
                                             MAN: We're done for, the police
                                               have found out about us — go!
```

Yao's analysis (p0065): what is visible and concrete (the wall clock, the tense flight) belongs to the picture track, and the knocking, the sirens and the characters' dialogue belong to the sound track — "**the two parts are cleanly separated and do not interfere with each other.**"

**Sample B: the opening of episode 1 of *April Rhapsody*, written by Wang Huiling** (China Translation and Publishing Corporation, 2000; Yao p0065–0068). The scene heading is three separate lines:

```
SET: by the little stream behind the village of Xiashi
CHARACTERS: village women, villagers
TIME: a summer morning in 1980

· caption: summer, 1980
· A village woman washes clothes on a bluestone slab by the stream.
VILLAGE WOMAN: ……
```

Layout points (Yao p0065–0068): the picture track is led by "·"; dialogue is led by "NAME:"; a caption stands on its own line ("· caption: summer, 1980").

**This passage is at the same time the model teaser for a biographical drama (Yao p0065–0068)**: five scenes complete a fourfold leap in time — a village woman washing clothes on a bluestone slab by the stream (summer 1980) → the slab is turned over, and "**the slab turns out to be a gravestone, and on it is written 'The Tomb of the Poet Xu Zhimo'**" → winter 1898, the Xu family's first-birthday grabbing ceremony (the child grabs a bamboo dragonfly, and the old lady reads it as "rising straight to the clouds, soaring into the sky") → summer 1904, the seven-year-old Zhimo flying a kite tumbles down a grassy slope and "has the first doubt of his life" → autumn 1931, among the same wild grass and haze, a search party finds the wreckage of an aeroplane → "caption: Xu Zhimo (1897–1931)." Every leap is stitched with **the same visual motif (the bluestone slab / the tangled grass / flight)**.

**When to use it**: dramas in which the sound design itself carries the narrative (music, effects, voice-over, layered ambience); drafts that must tell the sound department and post-production "who hears what and when"; the opening of a biographical or period drama with a complex picture-sound relationship.

### 9.2 Format 2: the Stage-Play Format (Yao p0069–0071)

The method (Yao p0069, as written): "it consists basically of the characters' dialogue, narration and a small number of cue words, **with very little careful description of the characters' appearance, psychology or the atmosphere of the environment**. This script format is particularly suited to the **indoor drama**, which is built on dialogue."

**Sample: the opening of episode 1 of *Oshin*, written by Hashida Sugako** (Chunfeng Literature and Art Press, 1985; Yao p0069–0071). Layout features: the set name on a line of its own ("the Shima Peninsula" / "the Tanokura house (morning)" / "the bedroom" / "Oshin's room"); **stage directions wrapped whole in square brackets**; dialogue as "NAME: (emotional cue) line."

```
THE SHIMA PENINSULA

[VOICE-OVER] Showa 58 (that is, 1983), in a season when spring was still young.
At the Tanokura house in a small town on the Shima Peninsula, the grandmother,
Oshin, has suddenly left home. Oshin is already eighty-three years old. Her
second son, Tanokura Hitoshi, runs sixteen large supermarkets in the surrounding
towns, and today is the auspicious opening day of the seventeenth. That the
Tanokura family's business flourishes as it does today is entirely the result of
Oshin's painstaking work. And yet it is precisely this Oshin who, on the very
day that the seventeenth store opens and symbolizes the Tanokura family's grand
prospects, has gone off alone — something no one in the family can believe. And
of course not one of them can guess the reason.

THE TANOKURA HOUSE (MORNING)

TANOKURA HITOSHI: (looking at his watch, agitated) If we don't set off soon
        we'll be late.
TATSUNORI: Shall we tell them to put the ceremony back an hour?
TANOKURA HITOSHI: It isn't just family — we've invited a great many guests, and
        even the mayor is coming. Could the Tanokura family do anything so
        shameful?
TATSUNORI: But if Mother isn't there…
TANOKURA HITOSHI: That is the old lady's temper. When the time comes she will
        appear out of nowhere. …In the first place, what reason could she have
        that she must leave home?
TATSUNORI: Mother has been strongly against this opening from the very start…

OSHIN'S ROOM

OSADA: (dejected) So Mother did plan to leave after all! …Five of Mother's
      favourite garments are missing, and two obis… and the handbag I bought
      her in Paris is gone too.
MICHIKO: It is hard being the daughter-in-law.
```

Two techniques (Yao p0069–0071): ① **open on a mystery (why did she leave) and answer it across the whole drama in flashback**, which is the standard opening gambit for a pure-dialogue drama; ② **use one concrete action, taking inventory of the wardrobe, to upgrade "she has wandered off" into "she left by plan"**, while Michiko's one grumble establishes the mother-in-law relationship — that is, **stating every premise through an argument**.

**When to use it**: indoor drama, sitcom, dialogue-led family drama; a rehearsal draft that will be handed straight to actors to read; a project that needs no description of the environment because the sets are fixed.

### 9.3 Format 3: the Film-Script Format (Yao p0071–0072)

The method (Yao p0071, as written): "attend to the image on screen, arranging the material basically **with a shot or a group of shots as the unit of the sentence**. The sense of the shot is strong, the jumps between images are large, visual form is foregrounded, descriptive words are few, and **the image and the sound are set out in the most economical language possible**."

**Sample: the opening of episode 1 of *Sixteen-Year-Old Blossoms*** (Yao p0071–0072). Layout features: the set name in square brackets ([the camp] / "the gate of No. 101 Middle School"); the body in short lines; dialogue written inline, and inversion permitted ("'He Damen, fall out.' Instructor Pan called He Damen's name in front of everybody").

```
[THE CAMP]

The ranks are dressed.

INSTRUCTOR PAN: "Line up on each other. The hand must go to this position.
      Forward — march."

HAN XIAOLE: "Sir — like that his backside sticks out."

The students snigger.

HAN XIAOLE: "In heat like this we'll all be roast duck."

A wave of laughter (suppressed) goes up.

"He Damen, fall out." Instructor Pan called He Damen's name in front of
everybody.

HAN XIAOLE: "Sir, I didn't say anything, that was my stomach rumbling."

INSTRUCTOR PAN: "Our new recruits are only two years older than you, and they
      train six hours a day. You do one hour."

Outside the gate of No. 101 Middle School. Parents are gathered. Somebody passes
a slice of cake in; somebody passes in a bottle of medicated oil.
```

Structural points (Yao p0072): Han Xiaole's three interruptions in a row = **three escalations, three rounds of "the students snigger / a wave of laughter (suppressed) goes up**," and the instructor finally counters with a statistic; then the cut to the parents outside the gate (the cake and the medicated oil for a grandson with a bad stomach) forms a "**counterpoint of inside and outside the school**."

**When to use it**: visual, action-driven, fast-paced drama; youth drama, action drama, short-run series; a draft that must let the director see the shot division at a glance.

### 9.4 Format 4: the Television-Novel Format (Yao p0072–0081)

The method (Yao p0072–0073, as written): "relatively free. On one side it attends to describing the televisual image, and on the other it takes up a narration close to that of a novel, setting out the scene's environment and atmosphere in some detail and frequently inserting explanatory text about the characters' psychology or the development of the plot. In the writing it pursues literary readability… **This format is at present rather widely adopted: it not only supplies a script for shooting but at the same time lays the groundwork for publishing the television novel.**" — This sentence is the explanation for why the commonest Chinese script format is what it is.

**Sample: *The New Shore*** (an educated-youth subject, by Liu Yanhua and Gao Yuangang; a long transcription at Yao p0073–0080). Yao then gives the book's only analysis of technique (p0080), which can be taken directly as a set of writing instructions for this format:

- The overall verdict: "**the three passages — dredging the river silt, the ox cart going into town, and the sickbed visit — run through in one breath, fully mobilizing every element of image and sound.**"
- **The three escalating passages of "dredging the river silt"** (the standard use of a repeated motif): the first time, "each of the two is on guard and they ignore each other"; the second, "the wary look turns into a calm and gentle expression"; the third, "the cart sinks into the mud and the two of them strain to drive it out, after which Liu Yanhua comes to the edge of the pond and washes both pairs of muddy shoes in the light of the sunset."
- **The sound design**: natural ambience, the shouts driving the animals, the "creak-creak" of the ox cart's wheels, the brass bell on the ox's neck.
- **Space = the relationship made visible**: in the ox-cart scene, "from one in front of the cart and one behind, they come to sit together at the front of the cart and have their first conversation, and then they are one in front and one behind again — **the change of distance implies the change of feeling.**"
- **The prop chain**: the yellow rubber shoes appear three times (**taking them off → wedging the cart → washing them**); and the corn buns are set against the white-flour sugar pancakes (at the Gao house, "save me some white flour… bake a few sugar pancakes" → the pancake offered on the cart is refused → at the sickbed he sees "the cracked, dried corn bun in the coarse china bowl").

**When to use it**: the default option; a project publishing a novel of the same name alongside the drama; a submission draft or sample script that must put psychology and atmosphere in front of producers and financiers. **Note**: this format puts psychological description on the page, but a camera cannot photograph psychology — which is why Yao immediately follows it with the shot breakdown below, showing that the literary draft's psychology must be converted by the director into shots.

### 9.5 *The New Shore*: the Literary Draft → 10 Shots, Side by Side (Yao p0080–0081; the book's only literary-draft-to-shot-list comparison)

After the long transcription of *The New Shore*, Yao sets out as written the director's breakdown of the sickbed scene into **10 shots**. The left column is what that passage narrates in the television-novel format; the right is the director's conversion into shots.

| # | What the literary draft (television-novel format) says | The director's shot (Yao p0080–0081, as written) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gao Yuangang comes in and sees Liu Yanhua on the kang | **medium-close — full (pull, follow)** Gao Yuangang hurries in and looks; he starts in alarm — Liu Yanhua is lying on the kang. |
| 2 | His worry | **medium-close —** Gao Yuangang looks on, worried. |
| 3 | The illness (breathing, lips, unconsciousness) | **medium-close —** Liu Yanhua's breathing is rapid, her lips cracked; she is unconscious. |
| 4 | Fetching a towel, ladling water, wetting it, laying it on her forehead, sitting on the edge of the kang watching | **close — (pull, follow)** Gao Yuangang hurriedly takes a towel from the line, ladles a dipper of water from the vat and wets the towel, lays it gently on Liu Yanhua's forehead, and sits on the edge of the kang watching anxiously. |
| 5 | She comes round | **medium-close —** Liu Yanhua comes round and opens her eyes a little. |
| 6 | His anxiety | **medium-close —** the anxiety in Gao Yuangang's face. |
| 7 | Her "feeling beyond words" and her "closing the door of her heart" (the literary draft writes psychology) | **medium-close — close-up (push in)** Liu Yanhua's eyes moisten, hot tears welling. Afraid that Gao Yuangang will see this feeling beyond words, she raises her hand with an effort and pulls the towel over her eyes, closing the door of her heart. But the tear rolls out from under the towel to her cheek, leaving a track behind it. |
| 8 | He masters his tears and looks around | **close —** Gao Yuangang masters the tears in his eyes and looks around him. |
| 9 | The split yellow rubber shoes and the cracked corn bun (the literary draft writes "something that lets one feel a certain emotion") | **close-up — (pan)** Below the edge of the kang, the familiar pair of yellow rubber shoes has split; on the little table, the corn bun in the coarse china bowl is cracked and dry… |
| 10 | His heavy heart | **close —** Gao Yuangang's heart is heavy. (exits frame) |

Yao's reading (p0081, the key points as written): the medium-close shots express "what he sees on coming in, and the relationship between the two"; shot 7 "**pushes from a close shot to a close-up, and expresses exactly right the tear that rolls out from under the towel and the track it leaves on the cheek**"; and Gao Yuangang's feeling is handled with "**a POV shot plus a close-up**" (the split rubber shoes, the cracked corn bun) — "this seemingly careless glance lets one feel a certain emotion hidden deep in the protagonist's heart."

→ **The conversion rules for a writer** (inferred from the table above): ① every sentence in the literary draft saying "he worries / she is anxious / his heart is heavy" takes its own shot in the breakdown, so **every change of emotion must begin a new sentence** rather than being folded into a long paragraph; ② whatever is **unfilmable psychology** in the literary draft ("a feeling beyond words," "closing the door of her heart") must be hung on a filmable action (raising her hand to pull the towel over her eyes) and a filmable piece of physical evidence (the tear rolling out from under the towel); ③ when you write "he feels a certain emotion," you must give, in the same paragraph, the **two props** that make him feel it (the split shoes, the dried bun), or the director has nothing to shoot.

## 10. Sample Planning Documents (Yao Kougen)

This section supplies the physical examples behind the "six documents" table in §2. Source: chapter 15, "Planning a Television Drama," of Yao Kougen's *An Introduction to Television Drama Writing* (p0328–0348). Yao's list of planning documents (p0329, as written): "in the ordinary case, the content of the planning includes **a plot summary, character design, the main settings, and a statement of the concept**."

### 10.1 The Plot Summary = the Logline: Definition and Three Functions (Yao p0329)

The definition (as written, with three renderings of the term): "a concise summary of the plot (**logline**), rendered by some as **high concept** and by others as '**the log line**' — meaning a sentence able to summarize a story, possibly two sentences or three, stating as concisely as possible **the dramatic premise, the genre, and the thematic value**."

The three functions of the high concept (Yao p0329, as written): "1. to summarize the story concisely; 2. to locate the genre broadly; 3. **to show that the plot has unusual power, so that it is attractive without a star attached.**"

### 10.2 Six Loglines Transcribed as Written (Yao p0329–0330)

| # | Title | The logline (as written) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The film ***Schindler's List*** | "A businessman apparently sunk in wine and women rescues hundreds upon hundreds of Jews in the Nazi genocide." |
| 2 | The film ***Mr. & Mrs. Smith*** | "A married couple's cold war is like a competition between spies, playing out the daily conflicts and the therapy of a marriage as a violent operation of wits and courage between enemy agents." |
| 3 | The British series ***Anna Karenina*** | "Well-born ladies must suppress the passion in their hearts and submit to the commandments of high society, while Anna pursues her own happiness at the risk of her life, willing to be spat upon by society if she can be a real woman." |
| 4 | The American series ***Prison Break*** | "A structural engineer robs a bank in broad daylight and goes to prison to help his wrongly convicted brother escape, thereby uncovering a great conspiracy." |
| 5 | The American series ***Gossip Girl*** (Yao notes it is "called a teenage *Sex and the City*") | "It is about a group of wealthy boys and girls in New York — designer labels, exclusive parties and the other fashionable elements of city life, together with the hypocritical friendships and the scheming among them." |
| 6 | The American series ***Breaking Bad*** | "A high-school chemistry teacher with a wife and a disabled son at home is diagnosed with terminal cancer, and decides to use the chemistry he is good at to make drugs, so as to leave his family some property after his death." |

→ The method inferred from the six (where Yao's "three elements" at p0329 land in them): **examples 1, 4 and 6 are the three-part "identity + abnormal action + cost"** (a businessman → rescuing Jews / an engineer → robbing a bank and going to prison / a chemistry teacher → cooking drugs); **example 2 is the "genre displacement"** (displacing a couple's daily life into a spy operation); **examples 3 and 5 are "the situation of a group + one exception"** (every lady submits, Anna does not). **Example 5 is worth noting: it writes only a world and an atmosphere, no action, and is the only one of the six that does not contain "somebody does something" — and Yao includes it anyway, which shows that "an ensemble plus a list of elements" is a legitimate logline form for a series.**

### 10.3 The Exception for Series: the Limit on Length (Yao p0330)

As written: "a television drama can sometimes also be summarized in a sentence or two, **but sometimes, because the content is complex and covers a great deal of ground, it takes rather more space to set out clearly**." Yao then gives five complete "plot summaries," each about **200–400 characters** — this is the book's practical standard for the length of a story synopsis.

### 10.4 Five Sample Plot Summaries

**Sample 1: *A Native of Beijing in New York* (Yao p0330–0331, transcribed in full as written)**

> Wang Qiming, a cellist in a Beijing symphony orchestra, and his wife Guo Yan come to New York in America in search of their artistic dream.
> Wang Qiming works in a restaurant and gets caught up in an emotional entanglement with the owner, A Chun. Guo Yan goes into a sweater factory, and the owner, David, takes a liking to her and presses his suit again and again. Wang Qiming cannot make a living and prepares to go home, but before boarding the plane he decides to divorce his wife, make some money in America first, and then devote himself to art. Wang Qiming throws himself into A Chun's arms and, with her help, comes to run a sweater factory. After parting from her husband, Guo Yan marries David. **Wang Qiming exploits Guo Yan's old feelings to extract commercial intelligence and ruins David's factory.**
> Having succeeded, Wang Qiming brings his daughter to America. The daughter is bitterly disappointed in her parents' situation and utterly repelled by A Chun; full of rebellion, she leaves home.
> **Abandoned by all, Wang Qiming ignores A Chun's warning, invests rashly in property, and goes bankrupt in the recession. At the last, to save himself from total defeat, Wang Qiming takes the only money he has left and goes to the gambling city.**

→ Four paragraphs, each ending in an irreversible change of identity or relationship: arriving in America → divorcing and changing camp → the daughter leaving → bankruptcy and the trip to Las Vegas. **Not one line of thematic comment appears anywhere; the judgement is handed to the reader through the closing sentences.**

**Sample 2: *Dae Jang Geum* (Yao p0331, a compressed three-part transcription)**

> ① The mother, Court Lady Park, discovers in the royal kitchen that someone is using food to plot, and is framed and killed for it; the father is caught up in a palace struggle and cruelly murdered; "**as she dies, Court Lady Park hopes that her daughter Jang Geum will enter the palace and become the highest lady of the royal kitchen, and avenge her.**"
> ② Jang Geum enters the palace and joins the kitchen, "and in the workplace struggle of the kitchen she is repeatedly framed by the clique that controls it, very nearly loses her life, and is banished to an isolated island."
> ③ "**Suffering creates another opportunity for Jang Geum**: she begins to devote herself to medicine and to fold it into the palace's cuisine, and enters the palace again… becoming Korea's first woman royal physician, granted the title Dae Jang Geum. At the same time she at last uncovers the truth and clears her mother's name."

→ **Three paragraphs = three identities (cook → convict → physician)**; each turn between them is driven by being cast out; and the mother's dying wish nails the whole drama's objective down in one sentence at the end of the first paragraph.

**Sample 3: *The White Tower* (Yao p0331–0332, the key points transcribed as written)**

> Associate Professor Zaimae Goro of the Naniwa University hospital, in order to realize his ambition of becoming professor of the first surgical department, breaks with his mentor Professor Azuma, who has been squeezing him out, and draws on the money and connections of his father-in-law Zaimae Mataichi… launching a fierce struggle to win votes… **Just as he is at the height of his satisfaction, a latent crisis slowly emerges: because of his careless attitude toward ordinary patients, the patient Sasaki Yohei dies of his misdiagnosis. And at that moment he is in Germany at an academic conference.** …After Zaimae wins at first instance, the Sasaki family insists on appealing. With the help of the upright lawyer Sekiguchi and Dr Satomi, they win a final victory. **Zaimae wins yet another election — election to the membership of an academic body — but falls under the double blow of losing the lawsuit and being seized by disease.**

→ **A synopsis built on two interlocking lines**: one is "an unbroken run of electoral wins," the other is "being held to account for a misdiagnosis," and the two close in a single sentence ("he wins… but falls"). **The sentence "and at that moment he is in Germany at an academic conference" is the model for hiding a moral judgement inside a scheduling detail.**

**Sample 4: *Shanghai Past* (a life of Eileen Chang) (Yao p0332, the frame transcribed)**

> The frame: **1955, going to America → the trunk left by her mother's death opens her childhood memory → 1937, fleeing the family → the University of Hong Kong → 1941, returning to Shanghai to live by her pen → 1944, meeting Hu Lancheng → 1955, going to America → 1995, dying alone in America.**
> The key lines: writing the marriage document, wishing for "**quiet years, a settled world**"; "having left Hu Lancheng, Eileen Chang could only wither."

→ **A biographical synopsis = a string of years plus a frame that closes on itself** (1955 appears twice: once as the narrative present of the opening, once on returning to the same point of the timeline). **One object — the trunk left by the mother's death — carries the switch for the whole retrospection.**

**Sample 5: *Song of Everlasting Sorrow* (Yao p0332–0333, transcribed in three dated parts)**

| Year | What happens in this part | The irreversible change at the end of this part |
|---|---|---|
| **1943** | The sixteen-year-old schoolgirl Wang Qiyao → Mr Cheng photographs her onto a magazine cover → a candidate for Miss Shanghai | "**kept as a mistress**" |
| **1958** | Living by giving injections in the lanes → Kang Mingxun → the Kang family refuses → a daughter, Weiwei, born out of wedlock | Kang leaves for Hong Kong |
| **1978** | Weiwei is 18 → Mr Cheng, the "old Shanghai gentleman," becomes a regular at the Wang house → Kang Mingxun returns to help his daughter go abroad | "Mr Cheng contracts an incurable illness; he loves Wang Qiyao as he always has" |

→ **The executable format Yao gives (p0333)**: **a long-form drama's "plot summary" = 3–5 paragraphs, each with one anchor in time (a year) or one change of relationship; and each paragraph must end on an irreversible change of identity or relationship.**

### 10.5 The Two Pieces of Advice That Accompany These Five Synopses (Yao p0337)

- "**The writer's trick is to create one event after another with tension and power to hold attention, and to leave the synopsis flexible, as though it were a riddle, so that it can later be patched, perfected and made personal as your own creative strength allows.**"
- "**You can use a story tree or a flow chart to break down and abbreviate the whole plot, perfecting the plot and fermenting the thought as far as possible at the synopsis stage. Once the synopsis is confirmed, then when you sit down to write the first page you are no longer creating — you are typing.**"

### 10.6 A Sample Plot Flow: the Destruction of the Japanese Special Forces Unit in *Drawing Sword* (Yao p0336–0337)

Yao's template for the detailed version of a "plot flow" (p0335, as written): "**what happened, when and where; what subsequent events those touched off; how they are linked together; what the specific motive for the characters' action is; how the action proceeds; and what the result is.**" The accompanying example is the flow-form treatment of the destruction of the Japanese special forces unit in *Drawing Sword*, with these key details (Yao p0336–0337, the key points as written):

- Colonel Yamamoto "studied in Germany and was the first in the Japanese invasion force to create a crack special-forces unit";
- "to reform tactics, **a field observation group** made up of 200 officers came specially to observe special operations";
- "**Li Yunlong is precisely not a commander who sticks to the rules; he divides his force to lay an ambush as the occasion suggests, and the result is a willow planted with no intention that grows into shade: he wipes out the Japanese field observation group entirely, and at the same time lifts the extreme emergency of Yamamoto's attack on the Eighth Route Army headquarters**";
- Chu Yunfei and Li Yunlong, "with only their guards, throw the county town's Japanese military police into utter confusion"; "the military police had already been in secret collusion with turncoats inside the Independent Regiment";
- of the battle for Ping'an county town, "the two sides fought over an area of more than a thousand square kilometres, committing a total force of 300,000";
- the ending is written both ways: "**but Li Yunlong loses his new wife Xiuqin, and is severely criticized by the Eighth Route Army leadership for acting without authorization.**"

→ Set against the "plot summary": **a synopsis writes what happened; a flow writes because of what, therefore what.** A flow must contain connectives of the "precisely not… / the result is / not only… but also… / and is further…" kind, and must in its last sentence write **both the gain and the loss** (the enemy wiped out + the wife dead + the reprimand).

### 10.7 What This Section Does Not Record

- **Not one physical sample of the "main settings" is given** (Yao p0329 lists only the name of this document and supplies no example) — **not recorded.** To write it you can only work backwards from the nine fields of the scene-breakdown sheet in §3.4 to list the set names.
- **No complete sample of a "statement of the concept" is given** (what Yao gives at p0337–0340 is a string of self-check questions and two routes to innovation, not a finished document you can copy) — a complete sample is **not recorded**.
- **No complete sample of "character design" is given either**: what Yao gives at p0333–0335 is an analysis of three innovations in *Lurk* (the characters' values / the relationship model between the male and female leads / the form of the protagonist's struggle with those around him), which is analytical writing rather than the body of a planning document — usable as a template, but **not a sample as written**.

## 11. Worked Per-Episode Scene Tables

Two physical examples: one American series (a scattered-point ensemble, all 41 scenes listed) and one Chinese series (a dramatic single episode, broken down by passage). Both come from Yao Kougen's *An Introduction to Television Drama Writing*, and they are the only two full single-episode breakdowns in the book.

### 11.1 The 41-Scene Table for the *ER* Episode "Love's Labor Lost" (Yao p0289–0297)

A production note (Yao p0289, as written): "created by the well-known bestselling novelist M. Crichton (author of *Jurassic Park*), with the great director Spielberg the owner of the production company that makes the series." The figures (Yao p0290, as written): "each episode of the series handles **more than ten** cases… **what these patients meet with is not only bodily illness but, still more, psychological, family and social problems.**"

**The structural charter (Yao p0291, as written; the single most important sentence)**: "one working day in an emergency room, **five continuing events and five chance events**; the characters are many and the events complicated, and **all these unrelated fates turn suddenly and collide with one another**; the most brilliant and also the most crucial thing is that all this material is **gathered together under one theme**, connected to one another through the arrangement of the structure, and set off in contrast against one another."

#### Yao's summary analysis (p0297, the key points as written)

- "These 41 scenes take a series of apparently confused, elaborate and unconnected passages and, **through their linkage and echoing of one another, and through the setting and resolution of each one's conflict, compose a complete story**, expressing each character's personality and emotional exchange, and fusing finally into the profound theme of love."
- **The design of the opening metaphor**: "the episode opens **by setting a metaphor with the orderlies' excitement over rehearsing *Romeo and Juliet***. Before it comes Dr Mark's marital trouble; after it comes the patient who pulls at Dr Susan's failed love affair. **These two doctors will later come together, and the sidelong mention here lays the ground for their love story to come.**"
- **The unity of the theme (the episode is precisely "love's labour lost")**: "the fear and hesitation before marriage; the separation and endurance after it — **love, like a flowing river, stirs up eddies and waves in everyone's life**… **not one of these pieces is exactly sunlit; on the contrary they are full of gloom, striving to show a thread of warmth in the cold wind and fine rain.**" Point by point: Hathaway "really worries about whether the person she is marrying is the one meant for her"; Susan and her sister "are in fact both failures in love"; Doug "doubts whether he can be accepted"; the suicide patient's wife resolves to leave him; the nun "already has a boyfriend and still hesitates over whether to marry."
- **The name for the structure (the key term)**: "a brilliant ensemble, in which each character's independent fragment of life is switched back and forth in a '**simultaneous**' space, **rendering the same emotion over and over**, weaving a perfect net of plot… **This scattered-point structure of multiple lines and intersecting events has an extremely strong artistic tension.**"
- → **The recipe (Yao's executable version at p0297)**: one episode = **5 continuing cross-episode lines + 5 chance single-episode lines**; the 10 lines share one abstract theme; the theme is stated by one "rehearsal / prop" metaphor at the opening; and **scenes 1 and 41 echo each other at the two ends in the same domestic setting.**

### 11.2 A Scene-by-Scene Breakdown of Episode 1 of *A Native of Beijing in New York* (Yao p0236–0252; the book's most complete analysis of a single Chinese episode)

The first episode's story line (Yao p0236, as written): "Wang Qiming and Guo Yan arrive in America… they get off the plane and wait for the aunt to meet them. The aunt helps the young couple rent a basement room in a slum district, and also gets in touch with a restaurant called the Xiangyuan Lou to introduce Wang Qiming for casual work." Yao's structural verdict: "'the airport pickup,' 'finding somewhere to stay,' 'going to work' — everything is within the order of ordinary life… **but it clearly expresses the subtle change in the relationships among the characters: Wang Qiming and his wife Guo Yan move from trust to the indignation, disappointment and estrangement that follow their discovery that they are being treated with indifference. This is a complete dramatic scene.**"

The one line of English at the opening (Yao p0236): "New York airport; Wang Qiming clears immigration with his visa, the officer bangs the stamp down on the passport and says '**Welcome to New York!**' Wang Qiming is taken aback, chews on it with an effort for a while, and at last works out this common piece of English."

#### The passage table

| Passage | Content | Narrative type (Yao's original table, p0251) | Means | What it seizes on | The effect (as written) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | Clearing immigration at the airport: "Welcome to New York!" | — | — | — | The starting point of the episode's two-ended echo |
| **1** | The ecstasy of riding into the city (a 20-shot breakdown, transcribed as written at Yao p0237–0238) | **Audio-visual narration** | The language of film (aerial wide shots, symphonic music, extreme close-up to extreme long shot) | **Point of view** | "It has the air of a Hollywood blockbuster, sparing nothing to exalt the ecstasy of a first sight of the New World" |
| **1b** | The actual welcome (the aunt drops them at the basement door, leaving 900 dollars of debt and a letter of introduction for work) | Dramatic narration (transitional) | Dialogue | Contrast | Yao's simile: "**like a conductor drawing the baton up from below, raising it high, and then sweeping his arm down**" |
| **2** | The couple's night talk in the basement | **Prose narration** | Literary technique | **The emotional point** | "As quiet as an idle flower falling to the ground, as fine as a mizzle wetting a man, it expresses a long, drifting homesickness and a faint anxiety to the full" |
| **3** | The little pigtail in the kitchen (a transitional scene also has real content, Yao p0243) | Dramatic narration (minor) | Props and fastidiousness | Character | Three small dramatic points: the pigtail = the artist's air; being fastidious about the name "Xiangyuan Lou"; refusing to wear the suit |

#### Passage 1: the 20 shots of the ecstatic drive into the city (Yao p0237–0238, transcribed as written)

> (1) The car drives toward the city. (2) The camera pulls from a close-up of Wang Qiming to a close two-shot of Wang Qiming and Guo Yan; they look at each other, embrace, smile. Wang Qiming holds Guo Yan excitedly and shouts, "New York! Manhattan!" (3) High-angle, New York by night. (4) The car goes forward. (5) In the car, the melody comes up; Wang Qiming grows excited to the non-diegetic music, waving both arms as if conducting. Guo Yan looks out, unable to take it all in. (6) New York by night, high buildings ranked together. (7) Wang Qiming as if conducting an orchestra. (8) The streets of New York by night, a blaze of lights. (9) Wang Qiming goes on conducting; Guo Yan smiles in excitement. (10) The streets of New York by night. (11) Wang Qiming shouts aloud: "America, New York!" (12) The streets of New York by night, neon billboards. (13) Wang Qiming shouts: "Wang Qiming is here!" Guo Yan leans against him happily. (14) The camera follows the car forward; a wide travelling shot of the pedestrians at the roadside. (15) Wang Qiming waves out of the car. (16) **The back of the aunt's head; she turns slightly to look behind her, faintly puzzled by this young couple's fuss over nothing.** (17) Wang Qiming and Guo Yan have no sense at all of the aunt's reaction, wholly immersed in their excitement. (18) Low angle, New York by night. (19) Wang Qiming conducts still more vigorously to the non-diegetic music. Guo Yan still looks about her, absorbed in her own world. (20) The car goes forward.

Yao's three comments (p0238): ① **display the foreign scene, but the scene must be clearly connected to the plot** — "it avoids what happens in some films and series, where attention goes purely to foreign scenery, so that the display of the scenery and the story and the characters' psychology are two separate skins"; ② **set an image-motif in the music** — Dvořák's *New World* symphony "takes on a narrative function, as an aural image-detail running through the whole drama," and when it returns in the second half "the same melody no longer has the old excitement and exhilaration, but issues slowly and in sorrow"; ③ **change the narrative rhythm** — "the colour moves from the monotonous to the rich; the space from the enclosure of the airport to the openness of the street." And Yao states this passage's real function outright (highly executable): "**the change of rhythm, from the oppression of the airport to the exaltation of the street, also lays full and forceful ground for the further oppression of the slum basement to come. In terms of narrative technique, this passage exists entirely to set off the next scene. The unrestrained exaltation of the journey is only an audio-visual sensation; the reticent dejection of the destination is real life.**"

#### Passage 2: the basement night talk — Yao's defence of the "scene without an event" (p0240–0242)

As written: "saying a few things of no consequence, doing a few things with no connection, expressing a feeling of no consequence, is not what dramatic narration attends to. Those scattered fragments — the feel of a memory, the worry about the future, a tender sense of defeat, an idea never explicitly stated, with no detectable causal connection — cannot drive the plot forward… **But this prose-like narration, and the contradictory feeling it lets slip, looks like idle writing and is in fact stillness defeating action: it moves toward the romantic, the blurred and the free, and has a particular aesthetic value.**"

The three-clause summary of the technique (Yao p0242, **usable directly as a writing instruction**): "this night talk in *A Native of Beijing in New York* **writes psychology where there is no event, writes the heart moving in a quiet setting, and writes emotion where there is no passion**. Emotion is a state of the heart, and nothing is so infectious, nothing so catching."

#### Passage 4: the four rounds of meeting A Chun (Yao p0244–0250)

Why it must be written out in full (Yao p0244, two points as written): ① "**the first scene between two important opposing characters** has a landmark function that leads the whole drama… their first meeting probes and guides. Once interest is established, the audience will have expectations." ② "**As the final scene of episode 1, it has the function of tempting the audience into the next episode. The last passage in the structure of a television episode is an important point of viewing excitement, and serves to set a suspense. And the ending of the first episode in particular is an introduction to the whole drama's question, pointing the way to the course of the whole drama's climactic conflict.**"

The set-up by contrast (Yao p0244): "the director gave a shot a minute and a half long of him walking across the road… **plot like this also relaxes the audience's viewing nerves and lowers expectation to zero.**" The planted prop is **a glass of beer** ("Frank produces a bottle of beer; Wang Qiming nods") — "**in this scene that one glass of beer, as a hidden pretext, strings together a series of finely made exchanges.**"

| Round | A Chun's move | The key lines (Yao p0245–0250, as written) | Yao's analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One** | Polite probing, rebuffed | A CHUN: "**Surely it isn't only people from Beijing who can speak Mandarin.**" / WANG QIMING: "Do you people just play whatever you can lay hands on?!" "I find it a bit noisy, a bit careless." / A CHUN: "Excuse me, I must leave you." | She "**gives a shove and immediately pulls him back**"; Wang "climbs the pole she offers… **to cover his own shabby awkwardness**," and "without meaning to, he sneers at her as an artistic ignoramus, which wounds A Chun's pride — **after all, every blade of grass and every tree here is her territory**" |
| **Two** | Politeness turning to anger | A CHUN: "**Here, I make the decisions.**" / WANG: "Call it a stopgap." "Sorry, when I used to come to a restaurant I always came to eat; it never occurred to me…" / A CHUN: "**Then please leave this place at once.**" | Showing him the door once her position is slighted |
| **Three** | A frontal attack | A CHUN: "**It costs money to sit here.**" "**I never said I would be paying you wages.**" "What I mean is, **if you can still manage a song or two, I really might consider it.**" (general laughter) / WANG QIMING bursts out: "**You still know what your own name is!**" (turns and goes) | "'If you can still manage a song or two' has entirely the sense of tit for tat… **I too take whatever I can lay hands on**"; "**at this moment the dialogue between these two people is not the negotiation of a matter but an exchange of feeling. "How much do you think this watch is worth?" / AN EMPLOYEE: "Two dollars." / A Chun throws the watch back: "**I'm sorry, Mr Wang, we are not a pawnshop here.**" / WANG: "Then what exactly do you want?" / A CHUN: "**Very simple. You come and work for me!**" | "**A feint nobody expected, landing exactly on the weak spot of this man who fears nothing under heaven**"; "**she never says outright that she wants to keep him, but makes her point by way of the beer money, and talking about the beer money suits her position and her trade**"; "to that question A Chun replies like lightning — **the turn had long since been completed in her mind**" |

**The "first and last line" structure of the whole episode (Yao p0250; the finest observation in the book, as written)**: "in this episode, the first sentence the protagonist understands is '**Welcome to New York!**' And the last sentence the protagonist hears is: '**You come and work for me!**' Between the first sentence and the last, the episode's main passages form several points, and every one of them is made on Wang Qiming's feelings after arriving in America. **The real meaning of 'welcome' is 'work for me,' and once Wang Qiming has understood that subtext he falls at once from the peak of his most inflated self-regard to the trough of his most wounded self-regard.**" And the method is added: "the writer establishes a starting point and a landing point, and then, taut and slack by turns, unhurried, forms a process… **through several changes of location, Wang Qiming is thrown high in the air and then dashed hard down. This last point of the process is on no account to be given up lightly by a writer, nor allowed to proceed in the natural order of time. Having reached such a dramatic point, the episode stops short.**"

**The two levels of "the passages must form a composite whole" (Yao p0251, as written)**: "one, **the passages may use every different artistic means, pursuing the different effects of film, of prose and of drama**; two, **the passages must submit to one unified significance — to the meaning of the episode, and to the meaning of the whole drama.**"

**One important permission (Yao p0252)**: Wang Qiming's real objective is not established until, just before boarding the plane, he is stung by A Chun's contempt (her famous line: "**Let me tell you — America is neither hell nor heaven. It is a battlefield!**") — "one might say that only when Wang Qiming has been called a 'loser' does the writer give the protagonist a clear objective to strive for: to be a winner!" → **the protagonist's real objective may be established after episode 1.**

## 12. The 36 Situations and the 36 Comic Situations

Both stock lists are in Yao Kougen's *An Introduction to Television Drama Writing*. **The 36 situations** = Georges Polti's list, whose full set of names Yao gives in chapter 9 at p0207, and which he rewrites item by item for a Chinese context in appendix 1 (p0349–0363) with 1–3 examples attached to each. **The 36 comic situations** = Li Tianji's list from *On Film Comedy*, given at the 1981 Nanjing film-writing workshop, which Yao transcribes in full at p0207–0208.

Yao's attitude to the two lists (p0207, as written): on one side he criticizes them — "Georges Polti's claim that the whole savour of life lies in these 36 dramatic situations goes rather too far… **confining the rich variety of life within certain situations in fact strangles human discovery and creation**"; on the other he permits their use: "but… these situations have not entirely lost their charm in the life of today, **and they can still be borrowed**." This is an explicit divergence from the American how-to books, which stress originality and warn against formula.

### 12.1 The 36 Situations (Yao p0349–0363, keeping the original numbering)

| # | Situation | A one-line account (the original's key points) | Examples (as given in the original) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Chance** (coincidence) | "Without coincidence there is no story," but "**the coincidence must be reasonable, natural and meaningful**." The iron rule: **using coincidence to land a character in difficulty is a flower blooming from the pen; using coincidence to rescue a character from difficulty is a botch**; "coincidence is fairly natural at the start of an event, while the resolution of an event must observe the law of necessity" | *Forrest Gump*; ***Crash* "has 16 coincidences and won the Academy Award for Best Picture — that is an exception"** |
| 2 | **Supplication** (pleading / appealing / crying for help) | "**To appeal to a friend for help is one thing; to appeal to an enemy is quite another**" — appealing to a foe to deal with a common enemy, begging forgiveness of the devil, pleading with an enemy on a relative's behalf | A sister with a terminal illness appeals for marrow to the selfish younger sister she has not spoken to for 20 years — "**what she needs is an appeal of the soul**" |
| 3 | **Deliverance** | Includes **self-rescue = redemption**; cites Jaspers, "**the loftier and greater the hero and the idea on which he rests, the more tragic the course of the event**" and "**destruction itself is the real victory**" | *Schindler's List* ("**to save one man is to save the whole world**"); *Run Lola Run*'s three rounds (shot dead / the boyfriend run over / winning the money and becoming a couple), "in a virtual game, though a move once made cannot be taken back, you can knock it over and start again" |
| 4 | **Rivalry** | "**The new generation does not fear competition; it fears having none, a hero with nowhere to use his strength. The escalation of competition is war.**" Four wars: military, political, commercial, emotional; and newly added: courtroom battles of wit, rivalry between kin, rivalry between two forces, man against beast | — |
| 5 | **Revolt** | "The struggle of man against god; **god stands for a power greater than the individual**" — society, the group, one's superiors; "**the greatest enemy is oneself**" | — |
| 6 | **Vengeance** | "Hatred can rouse a man to extraordinary action"; **the chief situation of the Chinese knight-errant**: "the knight avenges his employer — in other words, kills for a fee" | *The Count of Monte Cristo*, *The Book and the Sword*; the reversal in Kundera's *The Joke* (seducing the enemy's wife, "**only to find that she is the very person he is about to discard**") |
| 7 | **Pursuit** | "Chasing stars, chasing fashion, chasing men, chasing women, hunting down an enemy, and also hunting a criminal, **and also hunting somebody while being hunted yourself**" | *The Fugitive* |
| 8 | **Abduction** | **A three-sided structure**: "one, inside the kidnappers; two, the kidnapped and their family; three, the police and the investigators. **These three sides can form every kind of relationship and conflict**" | *The Shanghai Kidnapping*; the turn in *Ransom* ("**the kidnapped boy's father suddenly announces that the million in ransom will become a reward**" → the family takes the initiative / the police are thrown off and under public pressure / **the kidnappers fall out among themselves over the reward**) |
| 9 | **Rape and murder** | "An act of violence. The rape and murder of a young girl is particularly apt to rouse public fury" | The Spanish play *Fuenteovejuna* |
| 10 | **Fraud** | "Iron fears the furnace and a man fears the trap"; in the contemporary form it subdivides into **the cop show, the crime show, the detective show, the spy show and the courtroom show** | The long example of the instrument salesman (in the manner of *The Music Man*): "**a salesman who cannot read a single note takes the music teacher's baton and in despair conducts a minuet in G… and the townspeople feel this is the greatest performance they have ever heard in their lives**" |
| 11 | **Adventure** | Two key words: **prohibition and leaving home**. "**The essence of a prohibition is the temptation of adventure; nothing in this world tempts people like a prohibition!**" "**To leave home is to break the normal state, and to meet a great many problems you would absolutely never run into at home**" | *Star Wars* ("**it turns out that adventure is every people's favourite**"); paradise lost ("you shall not eat the apple!"); Romeo trespassing on the territory of the family feud; Columbus, Robinson Crusoe |
| 12 | **Misfortune** | "**Losing one's mother in childhood, one's father in youth, one's wife in middle age, one's son in old age — these are called the four great misfortunes of life**"; and another three: "**early success; a life without setback; mediocrity in everything.**" The maxim: "**misfortune is indeed the writer's fortune, not the sufferer's**" | The list of misfortunes in *The Happy Life of Talkative Zhang Damin* (the father killed by a boiler explosion, the mother with dementia, the eldest sister-in-law abandoned, the second son's wife unfaithful, the elder sister infertile, the younger sister's boyfriend killed in the line of duty and the sister herself with leukaemia) |
| 13 | **Disaster** | "A man sits at home and calamity falls from the sky"; the core question: "**when catastrophe comes, do the birds in the wood each fly their own way?**" With the caution that "beyond showing off technical tricks, it must also have sufficient dramatic potential" | *Titanic*, *Armageddon*, *Deep Impact*, *Battlefield Earth*, *Twister*, *Dante's Peak*, *The Perfect Storm* |
| 14 | **Daring enterprise** | "An extraordinary, godlike strength… **the daring enterprise is the act of a hero**" | The Helen Keller type; two "new hero" examples (the bomber pilot who grew up on video games, of whom the general says "**this is the new hero**"; the chairman who rejoins the ranks to work on economic crime, "**this is the new hero thrown up by the tide of the economy**") |
| 15 | **Revolution** | "A violent action to break free of restraint… **it cannot avoid harm to individuals in the great tide of revolution**"; with a criticism of "**the formula of revolution plus romance, reform plus a mistress**"; revolution is also "**destructive innovation**" | *The Gadfly* ("what it entangles itself with is more the fate of the individual"); *Doctor Zhivago* ("**what kills him is his love for Lara**"); a history of archetypes: "the '**phoenix reborn**' archetype current in May Fourth literature, and the '**sun**' archetype current in the Cultural Revolution" |
| 16 | **Love** | "'The hero and the beauty' can turn into '**the hero's spirit shortened by the lovers' long feeling**' and '**beauty is ill-fated, the beauty grown old**'"; "**real love… has no rules and no pattern; its course is like a psychological drama full of suspense and sudden turns**"; an industry observation: "**some think love is written about too much, written until it is greasy, and has lost that cautious, bewildered feeling**" | Cites Ellen Key's *Love and Marriage*: "**however one marries, there must be love for it to be moral**" |
| 17 | **Love unsuccessful** | Two structures: "**A loves B, B loves C, but C does not want to accept B's love**"; "**and when the situation has passed… B has a change of heart and comes to love A, and A finds he no longer loves B**." Extended also to the "psychological dislocation" of friendship and career | *Rickshaw Boy* (Tiger Girl → Xiangzi → Little Fuzi) |
| 18 | **Love obstructed** | "Obstructing love is the cruellest and hardest war in human nature, **and there is no winner**"; **the triangle of beating the lovebirds apart** = "the complementarity of romantic love, the result of an internalized cultural tradition." **Three levels (usable directly as a scale for a character arc)**: ① is there love at all ② is the love returned ("**this coefficient of success cannot be sought, only met with, but everyone longs for it**") ③ "**how long can love last?**" "**Love obstructed is the touchstone of love**" | Romeo and Juliet; the Chinese source in *The Cowherd and the Weaver Girl*, and *Southeast the Peacock Flies*, *The Western Chamber*, *Liang Shanbo and Zhu Yingtai*; contemporary: "'**the shut-in man and the shut-in woman,' 'the left-over woman,' 'the holy warrior' — in a blink the season for love is missed**" |
| 19 | **Adultery** | "It adds a process of flirtation, and a certain amount of incest, licentiousness, moral decay and love without any sense of good and evil"; "stories of second wives, third parties and one-night stands are everywhere… **which only shows that the nation has begun to re-examine its own life, marriage, family and feeling**" | *Anna Karenina*, *The Piano* |
| 20 | **The search** | "A journey in search of a dream… **its purpose is to let the 'process' express the meaning**, that is, the arduous 'journey' as the vehicle of the meaning" | The wooden board in a hit-and-run case reading "**seeking a witness**"; *The Promise* ("**he is searching for himself, searching for trust, and searching for real love as well**") |
| 21 | **Discovery** | "Discovering that the one you love is dishonoured; discovering a crime" | Newton; *The Truman Show* ("**a studio fitted with more than five thousand cameras**… **he has been acting in the comedy of his life for 30 years without knowing it**") |
| 22 | **Solving the riddle** | **The seven steps of a detective drama**: ① the crime erupts and the result is presented ② the investigation opens, "in which the social and psychological ground involves a conflict of morality and law" ③ **the criminal lays a maze, and the detective is misled by fragmentary information and breaks through it** ④ a further descent, unlocking motive and plan ⑤ **the interrogation, the two sides matching wits face to face** ⑥ new evidence found, the case ironclad ⑦ the truth out | The riddle of the Sphinx; Turandot's three riddles ("what is the phantom born in the day and dead at night?" "what is it that grows cold when you die and burns when you are hot?" "what is the ice that sets you ablaze?"); the inscriptions on the three caskets in *The Merchant of Venice* ("**the lead casket: who chooseth me must give and hazard all he hath**") |
| 23 | **Seeking and obtaining** | "**It is also fleeing**… **in reality you want what you cannot have, and you get what you do not want**"; the formula is named "**one wolf chasing three sheep**" or "**three wolves chasing one sheep**" | — |
| 24 | **Ambition** | On the positive side it is aspiration ("**the ugly duckling wants to be a swan**"), on the negative insatiable greed ("**the toad wants to eat swan's flesh**"). The test: when a man says "**how should a swallow know the ambition of a swan**" or "**a real man should be like that; I could take his place**," "**he will do everything possible and use every means, whether by diligent effort or by dark scheming, to realize that ambition or aspiration.**" | The order of situations in *Gladiator*: "**first there is his ambition, then the gladiator's vengeance**" |
| 25 | **Sacrifice** | "Sacrificing one's life for a cause; sacrificing one's happiness for one's kin… **giving one's precious time to help another is also a kind of devotion**" | *Family Ties*, *Our Dad and Mum*: "**when your family need you, are you willing to sacrifice your time and your interests for them?**" |
| 26 | **Loss** | "**Everything a man gains is gained at the cost of a loss**"; and in old age one must further "actively give up one's authority over one's children… **the drama lies between passive loss and active renunciation**" | *Dream of the Red Chamber*: "the point is in the two words '**flowing fragrance**' — the flowers of the garden drift away on the water" |
| 27 | **Misunderstanding** | (the original gives no defining sentence) | A long example: a daughter deliberately makes up to the police chief to save her father, "**and her father is unwilling to tell the truth either, and watches while others humiliate his daughter at will. Several times the daughter is about to speak out, but seeing her father's selfishness and baseness she in the end has no wish to expose the facts. This too is a kind of misunderstanding.**" |
| 28 | **Fault** | "Like a train off the rails, it always brings harm, intentionally or otherwise, to oneself and to others"; "**when an adult is at fault, others can rarely understand it, which is why the faults of youth become a beautiful sorrow and pain**" | — |
| 29 | **Reunion** | "Enemies meeting in a narrow road, the broken mirror made whole, the reunion of kin after separation — all bring great emotional turbulence" | **The "dramatic core" of *Thunderstorm* = Zhou Puyuan and Lu Shiping meeting again after 30 years**; Lu You and Tang Wan's reunion and the writing of "Phoenix Hairpin"; the long example of "the invisible reunion" (the maid quietly leaves before the rich young man's sight is restored, "**and when they meet he does not recognize her — so every one of their reunions becomes a point of excitement for the audience**") |
| 30 | **Running in** | Also called "**the fate of being thrown together**": putting strangers together, so that "**one must cross in the same boat, live under the same roof, with the very people one can least bear.**" "**Hell is other people, and running in is porcupines and hedgehogs finding the best distance to live at.**" | A group holding a strongpoint / taking a hill; *The Bodyguard* (the singer and the former presidential bodyguard); *Li Shuangshuang* ("**married first and courted afterwards**"); *Chapayev*, *Azalea Mountain* ("the running-in of the rough-hewn hero and the party representative") |
| 31 | **Madness** | "Throwing everything aside for the impulse of desire… **anxiety, restlessness, depression, dread, fear of commitment and the other psychological ailments are all knots in the heart that need to change.**" With **a table of emotion words (usable directly as writing prompts)**: liking, loving; disliking, hating; blaming, feeling wronged; respecting, admiring, being convinced, being unconvinced; deference, courtesy, negligence, warmth, coldness; envy, jealousy; doubt, trust; regret, grudging; worry, valuing, ignoring, despising; modesty, inferiority, deference, pride, arrogance; remorse, self-examination | — |
| 32 | **Recklessness** | "**Credulity is a kind of recklessness, carelessness is a kind of recklessness**… recklessness leads to others' misfortune and brings humiliation on oneself… **recklessness also produces comic effects**" | The tragic form: Zhang Fei in *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*; the comic form: Li Kui in *Water Margin* |
| 33 | **Jealousy** | "**The one who is jealous, the one who provokes the jealousy, and the one who is envied make up the human entanglement of jealousy**" (a triangular structure). **The rule of range**: the object is "usually confined to **schoolmates, colleagues, friends, close friends and neighbours — familiar people of the same rank**; those **too far away, too well-endowed, too able or too brilliant are not casually compared with**" | Othello; Medea; a particular example: "a girl… **goes to seduce a friend of her boyfriend's friend, trying to make the boyfriend jealous**" |
| 34 | **Remorse** | An important dramaturgical judgement: "**remorse cannot be a situation that urges action; it is the result of seeing a less than ideal outcome after the action. Fear of remorse is indeed an emotion that troubles people, and this emotion affects behaviour itself.**" | A string of proverbs: "if you do not exert yourself in youth, you will grieve in vain in age"; "**gather the flower while it may be gathered; do not wait until there is no flower and break an empty branch**"; "**if you weep because you have missed the sun, you will miss the stars as well**" |
| 35 | **Fear** | "**A ghost frightening a man is nothing; a man frightening a man takes half his life** — the key to terror is that hell is other people"; and it has two faces: "fear can strengthen the ability to protect oneself and avoid danger, **and it can also become an illness, even a loss of reason and self-control**" | The list of fears in *The Silence of the Lambs* ("a high-IQ cannibal and a skin-flaying psychopath, being kidnapped, being trapped down a deep well, giant insects on a corpse, being chased by someone you cannot see, losing others' trust in you, and a door closing behind you, and a pair of eyes in infrared goggles in a dark room"); *Primal Fear* ("**a deconstruction of fear**"); the long stage example of pre-wedding fear (the bride locks herself in the bathroom and neither parent can talk her out; "**at last the anxious groom bursts into the room, asks nothing at all about the situation, roars once at the bathroom door and turns and goes. Unexpectedly, the door opens**"); *Runaway Bride*, *Pink Ladies* |
| 36 | **The ludicrous** | "**Its essence is abnormal behaviour and inverted thinking. A mouse playing with a cat is ludicrous; a man who cannot get the better of a mouse is ludicrous; a great man made a fool of by a little man is ludicrous. When a person does not fit his environment, when human relations are turned upside down and thrown into confusion, the world becomes ludicrous.**" "**Only change the angle from which you look, or look with your head down**, and you will see not only the ludicrous but a great many unexpected effects" | — |

**The closing of appendix 1 (Yao p0363, as written)**: "most television dramas today use the '**principle of composition**': one nested inside another, one after another, or several cases breaking out at once, keeping the protagonist in a state of constant change. For example, **a wrongly convicted policeman escapes from prison and, while being hunted himself, investigates and hunts the real criminal — this one plot puts the protagonist in several situations at once: 'misunderstanding,' 'pursuit and flight,' 'solving the riddle.' The principle of composition allows more of reality into a television drama.**"

→ The two natures of a situation (Yao p0207): ① "the emphasis falls on human relations entangled in a conflict. 'Supplication' is the action and counter-action of the supplicant and the one in authority; 'deliverance' is the connection of interest among the deliverer, the unfortunate and the threatener; 'vengeance' has the struggle between the avenger and the wrongdoer"; ② "relations between characters change in the course of a change in thought or feeling." **When you consult the table, first decide which nature the item has: the first kind must be matched with characters, the second with time.**

### 12.3 The Difference in How the Two Lists Are Used

| | The 36 situations | The 36 comic situations |
|---|---|---|
| Level | **A character's predicament** (what situation a person is put into) | **The shape of the outcome** (how the thing finally went crooked) |
| What to match | Match with character relations (who against whom) and time (how long) | Match with the moment of **coming to nothing / dislocation / backfiring** |
| How they compose | Yao's "**principle of composition**": one protagonist in several situations at once (p0363) | Can be layered onto a situation: change the comic situation and the same situation turns from tragic to comic (the example for item 32, "recklessness," gives both the tragic Zhang Fei and the comic Li Kui at once, Yao p0362) |
| When to consult | At the approval and character-relations stage | At the episode-breakdown and scene-breakdown stage, especially when writing sitcom and light-comic elements |

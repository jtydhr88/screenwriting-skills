## 1. The Nature of Television Drama and Its Taxonomy (Yao Kougen)

### 1.1 Three Natures (Yao ch. 1)

**Performance art plus screen art plus the television medium**, "interdependent, interacting, mutually influencing, and inseparable" (p0050). The five dramatic elements = **action / conflict / situation / reversal / suspense** (p0037). Three things to remember:

1. Television drama "**can write several conflicts into one episode**, and the intensification and resolution of contradictions are relatively slow, like life itself" (p0037).
2. Television drama "**identifies with singular characters, sympathetic characters and active characters**" (p0038) — the exact opposite of "the anti-hero is the mark of a golden age."
3. **Fragmentary structure**: "television attends to small climaxes inside a section and inside an episode, while stage plays and films attend more to the climax of the whole work" (p0045).

(The complete lists of 6 differences from the stage, 10 from film and 9 of medium are in the reference.)

### 1.2 The Taxonomy (Yao ch. 14)

Yao states plainly that "measuring television, a mass medium, by the classification standards of stage and film is no longer suitable"; the six classification systems are in the reference, and his own is:

- **The single play**: 45 minutes an episode, possibly in two or three parts. The seven-part maxim: "**pan the material like gold from sand, take one leaf to know the autumn in the theme, let the structure outdo nature, win by surprise in the plot, dot the dragon's eyes in the characters, spare ink like gold in the language, and leave an aftertaste when it ends.**" The current state: "under the impact of long-form series, its creative momentum is less than before."
- **The anthology series**: little continuity between episodes, and it can be broadcast separately or consecutively (*The Man from Atlantis*, the first foreign drama imported in 1979, was an anthology series). **Four constructions**: ① thematic or framework type (*Liaozhai*, *Ten Great Women*); ② fixed characters plus self-contained episodes = a procedural (Hunter; *Prime Minister Liu Luoguo*'s "three-way contest," *Iron-Teeth Bronze-Tongue Ji Xiaolan*'s "three-way banter"); ③ a two-layer character line plus event line = a hybrid (ER); ④ a series of supporting-character stories (the same event repeated N times plus a reversal on the N+1th).
- **The medium-length serial**: **3 episodes or more, within 8.** "It suits both one episode a day and being broadcast all in one day"; "Japanese dramas usually stay around 11 episodes for the same reason."
- **The long serial**: **more than 8 episodes, currently around 20** — "**this is determined by the tight relation between television-drama sales and advertising effect**" (p0320). Three structural features: character as warp and event as weft, with one main line pulling countless branches, "each episode solves a problem on one branch and moves a step forward… and so it can continue endlessly"; each episode has relatively concentrated characters and events and a relative climax; "each episode interlocks with the next, with suspense guaranteeing the join between episodes." The warning: high investment and high risk mean that "**the dramatic structure is generally dominant in investors' planning.**"
- **The docudrama**: its origin is the BBC's drama-documentary group formed in **1946**. Three subtypes = the reportage drama / the biographical drama / the public-security drama. The test: "a documentary… does not permit the setting and re-enacting of environments and events. A docudrama uses actors… and the environment and events may be arranged and re-enacted according to the director's design"; a biographical drama's "**principal events may not be invented. Invention is mainly in detail and in secondary events**"; and the difference between a public-security drama and a cop thriller is that it "unfolds the case in the manner of a news investigation, extremely detailed and extremely slow… **a bit like reading out a ledger.**"
- **The sitcom**: Mintz Larry's definition — half an hour, characters who never change, resolved within an episode, a live audience or canned laughter, "**its most important feature is its circular narration… in the end the conflict is resolved, the pressure vanishes, and everything returns to the state it was in at the start.**" Six requirements are in the reference. **Rhythm data: it must be funny from the opening, and thereafter "there must be a spark within every 3 minutes"** (p0325); the week's problem must be solved inside the episode. General half-hour method is in `sw-sitcom-comedy`.
- **The studio drama**: classified by shooting setting, and it can be a single play, an anthology or a serial. Three features: concentrated settings (one or two interiors plus built sets plus multi-camera plus live sound plus live switching) / complex relationships ("almost everyone can play a scene against everyone else") / **strong in dialogue — "a dialogue-driven script requires about 18,000 characters an episode, while an action-heavy script runs 12,000 to 15,000"** (p0327).

## 2. The Extent and Mandatory Contents of the Six Planning Documents (Yao Kougen p0328–0348)

| Document | Extent and mandatory contents |
|---|---|
| **The plot summary** (logline; Yao gives three Chinese renderings: "a concise summary of plot," "high concept," and the literal "log line") | One sentence, stating the **story's premise, its genre and its thematic value**; three functions = summarizing the story / positioning the genre / "**showing that it possesses unusual plot force and is attractive without star participation.**" A series usually needs more: the five physical examples Yao gives run **200–400 characters** (samples in the reference). **An executable format: 3–5 paragraphs, each with a time anchor (a year) or a change of relationship, and each ending on an irreversible change of identity or relationship.** |
| **Character design** | "Chiefly sort out the protagonist's traits and their relationships with the other characters, **and not too many, to avoid confusion and blurred memory.**" The template (reverse-engineered from his analysis of *Lurk*): for an existing genre, list **three innovations — the character's values / the relationship model between the male and female leads / the form of the protagonist's struggle with those around them** — with one sentence each against the genre convention |
| **Principal settings** | List the show's principal settings (directly connected to §8's "fix the set names, concentrate the settings") |
| **Statement of concept** | "Emphasize your own innovations, and state the valuable thoughts that might otherwise be overlooked"; "**a concept is by no means a flash of inspiration… its novelty comes from depth.**" Two routes: innovation on an archetype, motif or genre (Cox counted 345 versions of Cinderella worldwide); and absorbing the latest practice and research (*Criminal Minds* uses borderline personality disorder; *The Big Bang Theory* uses string theory and Schrödinger's cat) |
| **The plot flow** | "**What happened when and where, which subsequent events it touched off, how they connect, what the characters' concrete motives are, what the course of the action is, and what the result is.**" The working method: "you may use **a story tree or a flow chart** to break down and abbreviate the whole plot, and **once the synopsis is confirmed, by the time you sit down to write page one you are no longer creating, you are typing.**" |
| **The sample script** | "A production company needs to read the script of **the first episode or the first two.** **These first episodes function like the pilot used abroad.**" Their duties = setting the situation, creating a rich world, building the relationships, producing the inciting incident, giving the action a clear direction, strong suspense, compliant format, settings and dialogue, showing cultural literacy and authentic detail, and showing your voice |

## 3. Chinese Script Format: Sectioning, the Four Formats, Revision Numbering and the Scene-Breakdown Sheet

### 3.1 Sectioning and Scene Headings (Yao p0063–0064)

- In writing television drama, "**the most basic concept is to divide into natural sections by setting and by scene.**" A **setting** is the place where the action or event occurs ("inside one setting characters may enter and exit and time may change, but the setting remains the same one"); a **scene** is the basic unit of the same time, the same place and the same characters ("once anyone exits, it moves to the next scene"). **If you use free cinematic time and space, section by setting; if, like a studio drama, it unfolds in one place, you must section by scene.**
- A scene heading must state: **the place; interior or exterior; the time; day or night**; "some also give the scene's number and the characters appearing in it."

### 3.2 Four Coexisting Formats (Yao p0064–0081, citing Zhu Baishui's *How to Write for Television*, TTV Culture 1984)

Yao states plainly: "for the 'body text' inside a scene there is **no unified standard**, and four are currently common." — this is the source for "Chinese scripts have no single industrial standard," and **consistency within one draft is enough.**

| # | Format | Method | The book's sample | When to use it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Picture and sound separated** | Image and sound written side by side across the page, "so that when a director, actor or crew member holds the script in hand, everything is visible at a glance" | *April Rhapsody*, episode 1 (written by Wang Huiling, 2000) | Shows where sound design carries narration |
| 2 | **The stage-play format** | "Essentially composed of dialogue, narration and a small amount of cueing vocabulary, **with very little elaborate description of appearance, psychological activity or environmental atmosphere**," with stage directions in square brackets | *The Story of Oshin*, episode 1 (Hashida Sugako, 1985) | **"Particularly suited to dialogue-driven studio drama"** |
| 3 | **The screenplay format** | "**Arranged with one shot or a group of shots as the sentence unit**… setting out picture and sound in the most economical language" | *Sixteen-Year-Old Season*, episode 1 | Visual, action-heavy, fast-paced shows |
| 4 | **The television-novel format** | "Attending on one hand to describing the television image and on the other taking a nearly novelistic narration… **this format is currently the most widely used, providing both a script for shooting and a foundation for publishing the television novel**" | *New Shore* | The default option; projects publishing a novel of the same name simultaneously |

Abridged samples and the side-by-side comparison of *New Shore*'s "visiting the sick" scene, literary script against 10 shots, are in the reference.

### 3.3 Numbering During Revisions (Zhang Mingzhi p0121, as written)

> Once a scene has been assigned a number, **that number must always represent that scene.** So an added scene gets a name mixing a number and a letter. For example, if two scenes are inserted between scene 19 and scene 20, they are written as **19A and 19B.** If scene 66 is deleted, a note is added between scenes 65 and 67 reading "**66 deleted.**" The standard adopted keeps the page numbers consistent in the same way, **adding an A or B between page numbers rather than repaginating or reprinting the script.**

The premise: "**once a script goes into production, revisions will continue right through.**"

### 3.4 The Nine Fields of a Scene-Breakdown Sheet (Zhang Mingzhi p0112–0113)

Made by the **script supervisor** after the lock, it is the basis for the shooting schedule. Nine fields: **episode and scene number / page count ("indicating the weight of the scene") / atmosphere (interior or exterior plus morning, day, noon, dusk, evening, night) / set name / content / principal actors (including "characters not named in the script but who must logically be present") / extras (type and number) / special notes on costume, hair and props / remarks.** The sort keys are set name, atmosphere, episode-and-scene number or actor, with the aim of "gathering scenes with the same shooting conditions together… **so that the whole show can be shot in blocks.**" Three cautions: omit no scene, "**not even a single line or a transitional scene with no actors**"; omit no character or prop that must be present though the script's text does not mention it; and set names "must use consistent terms from beginning to end."
→ **The conversion for the writer**: whether set names are consistent, how many scenes one set carries, how many pages each scene runs, the proportion of night scenes, and whether there are extras — those five directly determine budget and schedule.

## 4. Per-Episode Metrics, the Scene's Three Duties, and the Double-Line Formula

### 4.1 Per-Episode Metrics

| Metric | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Settings / scenes | **20–40 settings** an episode, generally **25–35 scenes**; "if four or five of them are compelling major scenes, the episode essentially works" | Yao p0227 |
| Plot points | **About 13 plot points an episode** (the requirement the writer Liu Heng set himself on *The Happy Life of Garrulous Zhang Damin*) | Zhang Wei p0060 |
| Main events | **At least one main event an episode**, supported by small events and detail; **stagger** the slow passages against the high-capacity ones | Zhang Wei p0035, p0060 |
| Spacing of climaxes | **An emotional or event climax every 3 to 5 episodes, and sometimes the two coincide** (the pattern of plot distribution across episodes in *Go Lala Go*) | Zhang Wei p0113–0114 |
| Number of reversals | **At least two reversals** across the show (the industry phrase is "doubling back") | Zhang Wei p0062 |
| Word count | Dialogue-driven about **18,000 characters an episode**; action-heavy **12,000–15,000**; a 30-episode show ≥ **400,000** with dialogue ≥ **50%** | Yao p0327; Zhang Wei p0076–0077 |

**A scene's three duties** (Yao p0252): carry over from before and lead on to after / advance the plot / prolong the suspense — "**prolong the suspense, not end it; advance the plot, not display it**"; and a scene that does none of the three, "however good and however brilliant, is a pair of hands on a Balzac statue that spoils the whole and must be cut."

### 4.2 The Career Line Plus the Private-Life Line (Yao p0285, the double-line formula, as written)

> Usually one is **a romance, a family story or a private life**, and the other involves something else such as **work, war, a mission or an expedition**. **Each line has a goal, an obstacle and a climax. In most cases the two are causes of each other: touch one and it triggers the other, and without resolving one you cannot resolve the other, constituting a double causal structure.**

Examples: *A Native of Beijing in New York* (emotional entanglement plus survival abroad), *A Shanghai Family* (struggle in old Shanghai's commerce plus romantic entanglement with three childhood friends, "writing both the social level and the personal level… which makes the characters three-dimensional"). The fourth section of *Drawing Sword* (episodes 26–30) writes it as an explicit main and supporting line: the main line is integrating into the military academy, the supporting line an extramarital affair, each with a complete arc and both pointing at "**return**" and "**finding the way back**" (Zhang Wei p0303–0306).

## 5. Adapting Into Long-Form: the Five-Source Typology, Sell-Point Migration, Theme and Expansion

### 5.1 The Five-Source Typology (Zhang Wei)

**Novel / film / a life (biography) / stage play / localizing a foreign series**:

| Source | Structural handling | Character handling |
|---|---|---|
| **Novel** | **Follow the novel's framework as far as possible** (the Chinese classical chaptered novel's "manner of narration is extremely close to television drama"); remove the long passages of psychological description; add threads; for a novel interleaved in time and space, "in television drama it is often more suitable to interleave the different times more frequently" | Three ways: follow directly ("lift the whole pot" / "translating the roles across," *Drawing Sword*); break with the original character and remake them (Huang Yiyi in *Plot Against*); or add and subtract elements and threads (a compromise fine-tuning, the commonest) |
| **Film** | **Break the film's original structure** (film-within-a-film, nested layers, pure flashback and fragmentary forms "are mostly unsuited to television drama"); "**it is extremely rare to value the source film's narrative structure.**" The counter-example: the double-line parallel montage of *The Spring River Flows East* "is very well suited to television use," and the series version kept it | Extend and expand the characters' fates (and "**add events, applying resistance to their dramatic motive and capacity for action**") / create new characters to make a multi-sided force / refine and adjust the characters' own traits |
| **Biography** | Generally **chronological in time and space**; the biggest problem is selecting material. The "novelistic structure" can be borrowed: a framework of several main sections, each a stage of changed fortune and developed character; "a novelistic structure generally has more than one central dramatic conflict" | Two ways: a panoramic presentation of a whole life (the chronicle, *The Legend of Bruce Lee*) / foregrounding one stage or one strongly plotted event (*Anita Mui* takes the end of her life) — the latter "must use the one selected event to pass a final judgement on her whole life" |
| **Stage play** | Domestically there are only two major resources, **Lao She and Cao Yu**; Xia Yan's test of adaptability: "**works with a strong story (or dramatic quality), relatively distinct characters and not too many threads are relatively easy to adapt**" | Make the stage characters' dramatic space televisual (invent the living space outside the invented main setting) / extend the lines of fate and invent new supporting characters |
| **Foreign series** | **The institutional reason the structure must change** (p0042–0043): Japanese drama runs 11 episodes; American drama is weekly and can run indefinitely ("the story's direction is adjusted according to its ratings" → an open structure), whereas "Chinese television dramas are essentially shot and completed in full… and broadcast in one complete run" | Replace the setting, reset the characters, adjust the plot. A success: *Home with Kids* (a psychologist → a children's playwright; a career woman → a housewife; many children → **a remarried blended family**). The three causes of *I Want to Fall in Love*'s failure: feminism "clearly has no cultural root in China" / "characters enter and speak at very high frequency," violating Chinese viewing habits / a loose consciousness of marriage and romance "somewhat detached from Chinese reality" |

### 5.2 Sell-Point Migration (Zhang Wei's most original concept, p0028, p0082–0085)

- **A sell point = "a meaning that can circulate among the widest possible population"** — "a sell point is not a meaning that interests a few, but one with the widest potential circulation."
- The mechanism: the source's audience ≠ the series' audience. *Go Lala Go*'s readers are "precisely located among the better-educated 20–35 age group," "while the age range of China's television audience sits exactly above that of the novel's readers"; the novel's structure, built on workplace rules, "cannot be used by a television adaptation"; and the novel's "TIPS"-style workplace maxims "can under no circumstances be copied into a television drama."
- **The executable action**: list the differences in age, education and viewing habits between the source's audience and the target series audience; **replace every sell point that works only for the source's audience (workplace tips, philosophical argument, essayistic passages, semi-classical prose style) with sell points that work for the broadest audience (the emotional line, the family line), while keeping the source's keywords as the shop front** ("workplace," "white collar," "foreign firm," "metropolis" are all kept, because "they do not much subvert the original novel's theme or style").
- The same judgement from the platform side (Zhang Mingzhi p0056): "in domestic publishing, selling over 30,000 copies counts as a bestseller… **but a million viewers is trivial in television, so do not be easily dazzled by the word 'bestselling.'**"

### 5.3 The Thematic Coordinate System and the Environment of Thematic Expression

- **Theme = the coordinate system of an adaptation** (p0023): "in what form the old text will be placed inside that coordinate system is the main problem the theme has to solve. **Theme is a guiding principle of adaptation**… settling the theme settles the scope, the genre and even the relationships." The dimensional image: "the coordinate system of written expression is a two-dimensional plane of a horizontal and a vertical axis, while the image introduces a third, temporal axis to make a three-dimensional system."
- **Which themes cannot be adapted** (p0025): "the theme chosen must have the property of being **usable across all three texts.** A theme peculiar to one particular text cannot serve as the expressed theme." (The Bible; *One Hundred Years of Solitude*.)
- **The three elements of a theme's expressive environment** (p0232–0233, the book's most reusable theoretical tool): ① **the enlightener** — "**usually must not be the protagonist**… the protagonist often cannot accept or understand it at once, and must go through a series of events, grow, and only then realize / accept / transcend / reject the value the enlightener told them years before"; ② **the reward-and-punishment mechanism** — "any behaviour that does not conform to or violates the thematic idea will certainly be punished accordingly, and any conforming behaviour will certainly bring a positive result"; ③ **a protagonist who does not stray** — "the protagonist's motive must itself be hooked to the thematic value."
- **Three operational judgements about theme** (p0026–0029): conform to mainstream values ("cause and effect," "good and evil both get their due") / predict the "hot issue" so as to answer the audience's present anxiety (the long cycle of film and television "amounts to losing topicality. **This supplies an ample reason for adaptation, since the social response to the existing work can supply that prediction for the screen**") / grasp long-selling genres accurately and compound several genre elements under one theme (per CSM12-city data from May 2009 to May 2011, **espionage-war, legendary lives and family melodrama are China's long sellers**; *Lurk* "borrowed the shell of the spy drama and actually told the story of **office politics**," and *Borrowing a Gun* is "**a family melodrama** wrapped in spy-drama clothing").

### 5.4 The Character Trinity and the Passage Method

- **The trinity** (p0050): **the reference text (the source work) — the adaptive creation (the script) — the adapted product (the series)**, "like three large circles, interlocking, with overlapping parts and areas independent of each other, **and those unrelated areas are precisely the character information the adapter adds.**" The book uses "reference text" rather than "the original," to avoid presupposing "fidelity."
- **"Faithfulness to the original" is judged an obsolete question** (p0051–0052): "**there is no character absolutely unsuitable for adaptation; there is only the feasibility and reasonableness of the adaptation, the difficulty of actually doing it, and the overall effect.**"
- **A counter-intuitive judgement about extent** (p0043): "**a long novel, however sufficient its word count, may not have enough effective writing on portraying and narrating character; a short story or a two-hour film may not lack material to expand, and often gives the adapter more imaginative space and more usable material for building character.**" (*Ma Wen's War*: a 30,000-character novella with 6 characters → 24 episodes.)
- **The passage-by-passage copy/delete/adapt method** (table 4, p0191–0192): cut the source into passages by timecode or chapter, tag each passage [copy] / [delete] / [adapt] into a comparison table, and use the table to decide where to expand and where to cut. An excerpt of the full *Myth* table is in the reference.

### 5.5 Four Methods and Three Steps of Expansion (p0039–0040, p0213–0216)

"A television drama's length is generally **more than 10 times** a film's" (*Myth* is 20 times). The four methods: ① **increase the weight of the subplots** ("staying inside the main line the whole time actually makes it easy for the audience to feel it monotonous and dull"); ② **expand and complete the characters' lines of fate**; ③ **increase the number of protagonists** ("a film usually has only one or two principal characters, while it is very common for a television drama to have **three or four**," including considerations of star casting); ④ **develop the secondary characters** ("a film can tell only one couple's story; a television drama absolutely cannot").

The three accompanying steps: **increase the number of characters → complicate the relationships → extend the span of time over which fates develop.** The formula (p0216): "the television drama's length ÷ the film's length = the drama's average character-time ÷ the film's average character-time." **The taboo: do not build a purely platonic line between two new characters** (it generates no conflict and shifts the centre of gravity); either add romance or turn it into a conflict.

Three questions testing a new character's legitimacy (p0091–0092): does he have causal links to two or more existing characters? Is his function continuous, or a one-off action? Which line breaks if you delete him? Three tests for deleting a character (p0097): he does not advance the protagonist's fate; the audience has to pay a cost to learn him and he does not run through the show; his contrasting function has already been absorbed by a change in the protagonist's character. **Fix loose relationships onto a social attribute** (p0098, p0139–0141): ex-girlfriend → ex-wife, friend → primary-school classmate, colleague → siblings — "which makes both wild behaviour and sudden intimacy plausible at once, and automatically adds resistance."

### 5.6 Three Disciplines for Dual-Timeline Shows (p0034, p0197–0198)

① Each single line must connect completely front and back with nothing missing; ② a single line should be neither too long nor interleaved too frequently; ③ **switching between threads must be stitched by "the same object / the same place / the same scene,"** with no unmotivated hard cuts (the *Myth* film uses "a transition on the same object (a splash of water)," and the series uses "a transition on the same object (the treasure box)"). The iron rule: "the two timelines must be connected to each other, corresponding at the level of the story's interior, as one organic whole."

### 5.7 IP Assessment

- **Producer Zhao Junkai's three foundations** (*Drawing Sword*, Zhang Wei p0274–0275): "**one is the theme, the untainted banner of the soldier; second, distinct characters; third, a brilliant story.**"
- **Zhang Mingzhi's two judgements** (p0056–0057): **judge the market space** (what are the demographics of the audience group? Is there any possibility of enlarging that market?) plus **judge the degree of visualizability** ("some works that reach the peak of literary and artistic achievement… may have a diffuse plot, lacking clear causal logic… and characters who may lack the capacity for action"). The conclusion: "literature must above all be readable… **but a television drama must be viewable, and must be popular spiritual nourishment, a cultural product easily understood by the general public.**"

## 6. Fragment Adaptation: Four Laws, Four Techniques and the Delivery Format

Zhao Binbin's book runs in **exactly the opposite direction** to section 5: a finished film → **an 8–20 minute stage fragment for 3–8 people** (rehearsal material for vocational-college acting programmes). The two share the floor of "the core facts cannot be moved," but one enlarges and one shrinks, and **they cannot be merged into one process.** Details of staging and page conventions are also in `sw-format-adaptation`.

**Four laws** (chapter 3, p0018–0023):

1. **Rationalize the stage setting (merge the main settings)** — merge multiple settings into "**one main setting plus one adjoining transitional area**" (home plus the doorway, a room plus the corridor, a bedroom plus a dark room), with the characters' first entrance set directly in the transitional area. Two hard rules: **important material must take place in the main setting**; and **a setting may be deleted only when it has no necessary connection to the core facts**, or "you will get… content in the adapted fragment that does not fit the given situation."
2. **Material selection (the veto gate)** — if a candidate scene has "two people sitting talking plus a single enclosed small space (a café, a living-room sofa) plus no physical task," **veto it outright** ("**stage performance takes action as its core**… even if the leads want to act, the given situation prevents them from acting"). A qualifying scene must have at least two of **distinct character traits / strong conflict and event (antagonism) / complex entanglements among the characters.**
3. **Event and conflict** — the fragment must revolve around **one core event**; **the cut point is set at "the moment the protagonist's motive has just been established,"** with everything before it compressed into the story summary and the character biographies. The structural template (*Where Is Happiness*): one main fact (the lay-in section) plus one "small interpolated matter" to prevent flatness plus an external conflict of the kick-the-door kind (the climax section) plus an ending with action in it. The **"double precondition" test** of psychological motive: before writing a character's decision to do something, list the two conditions that must both hold (the goal plus trust in the other person); miss one and the action does not work.
4. **Completeness** — "though it is a fragment, it still needs **a beginning and an end.** Generally a fragment begins by conveying the relationships, lays in the plot for the event to come, opens the conflict, sustains the antagonism and conflict, and so to the end." The common defect is "no ending, or a weak sense of an ending," caused by "**not writing an ending** for the adapted film or drama" — **the ending usually has to be written yourself, and you cannot copy film and television's "to be continued."**

**Four concrete techniques**:

- **Turn a process scene into a delivery**: where the film has a character leaving to do something, rewrite it as another character bringing the result on (Xiang Yuan specially making stewed pancakes for Mai Qiu → changed to **Auntie Wang** bringing the pancakes in), on the grounds that it "makes the script's structure tighter and is convenient to perform on stage."
- **Four steps for an unplayable scene going offstage**: exit into the offstage area → an off-stage line → a prop thrown out from the wings → the character entering with a visible consequence.
- **The connector character**: when you need someone to run an errand, carry a message or fetch a person, simply add one, and merge several supporting characters of the same function in the film into them (the note in the character list for Secretary Yang in *Coming Home* reads: "this role was added to connect entrances and exits, and also merges several characters into one"); a supporting character's gender may be changed to suit rehearsal needs, keeping only the dramatic function (the seventh uncle → the seventh aunt).
- **Only two devices for changing "acts" in one setting**: a powerful character clearing the room with one line; or **a knock or an interruption** cutting off a situation about to become irretrievable. Do not use a blackout to change the set.

**The delivery format** (reverse-engineered from the uniform convention of the 26 fragments; detail in the reference): the title plus "adapted from the film / series X of the same name" → one paragraph describing the zoned set → time / place / characters (one line of biography each) → a story summary (3–6 lines) → the script (`【】` action cues on their own lines, `CHARACTER: (parenthetical) line`) → `reference video source` plus `note: the source / the original writer`. **No scene numbers, no interior/exterior/day/night, and no camera terminology or transitions at all.**

⚠️ One conflict must be flagged: Zhao's book holds that the montage structure of a screenplay requires the writer to "**describe the story in shots**" (p0014), while `sw-format-adaptation`'s hard rule is "never mention the camera." The reconciling reading is that Zhao means "conceive in shots," but that is genuinely what the text says — this is the position of a Chinese acting-programme textbook, and **it should not be taken as a formatting rule.** Likewise, a character list with biographies is mandatory in fragment and Chinese industry drafts, and explicitly forbidden in Anglo-American specs.

## 7. The Production Chain and Censorship: Administrative Regimes, Delivery Milestones, Red Lines, Contracts and Copyright

### 7.1 The Eight Administrative Regimes (Zhang Mingzhi p0278, as of 2016, as written)

> The entry regime, the licence regime, the subject-planning and project-approval regime, the production-filing and publication regime, the finished-programme review regime, the distribution-licence regime, the broadcast-management regime, the advertising-management regime

→ The whole administrative chain a writer must understand: **may you do it (entry/licence) → may it be approved (subject planning) → may it be shot (filing and publication) → may it pass (finished-programme review) → may it be sold (distribution licence) → may it be broadcast (broadcast management) → may there be placement (advertising management).**

- **Project approval may come before or after the script** (p0253): the flow is "the investment is basically in place → complete the administrative approval or filing of the subject plan → identify the script (or a suitable writer) → settle the principal creative personnel → form a production unit → actual shooting → post-production → publicity → distribution," but the book notes that "the procedures in the preparation stage are not immutable… **for example, you may have the script first and then file the subject plan, or the reverse.**"
- **The precondition for a legally constituted production unit** (the *Regulations on Safety Management for National Film and Television Production Units (Trial)*, the Television Production Committee plus the China Film Producers Association, **5 November 2012**, p0295, as written): "the basis for forming a production unit is that the screenplay of that film or drama **has passed government filing and project approval, and has sufficient funding and adequate market forecasting.**" → A hard indicator for a writer judging whether a project is real.
- **Two stages of review** (p0049): the **content review** (also called the artistic review) examines "the theme, the internal logical relations of the plot, whether it contradicts the conventional worldview, values or historical fact, and whether it exceeds the limits on pornography, violence and sensitive historical events," and non-compliance is revised "**until the content review passes**"; the **technical review** examines picture stability, levels, saturation, subtitle position, sound balance and so on. Only after both does the **Television Drama Distribution Licence** issue (p0131; the 2016 name of the reviewing body has since changed).
- **The dual-review special channel** (p0281): the *Regulations on Project Approval and Finished-Programme Management for Screenplays of Films and Television Dramas on Major Revolutionary and Major Historical Subjects* and the *Regulations on Project Approval and Finished-Programme Management for Screenplays of Major Theoretical-Documentary Films* — these two categories carry **both script project approval and finished-programme review**, and taking on such a subject means preparing for submission at the script stage. Also: dramas touching ethnic or religious policy are reviewed by the ethnic affairs commission and the religious affairs bureau; co-productions or the engagement of foreign actors are reported to the foreign affairs department (p0174, with 2016 institution names).

### 7.2 Script Delivery Milestones and "the Script Must Lock Before Shooting"

- **The six-stage payment table** (the *Commissioned Creation Contract for a Television Drama Script*, 1.5, p0318, a 2016 model): **10%** (a deposit) within 10 working days of signing / **10%** on written acceptance of the **story outline** / **20%** on written acceptance of the **episode outline** / **30%** on written acceptance of the **first draft** / **20%** on approval of the **second draft** / **10%** on final written confirmation of the **locked script before shooting.** Two procedural clauses: stage deliverables are "subject to **the joint signature of Party A's producer and director**"; and "**only after receiving Party A's written confirmation may the next stage of creation continue**" (advancing through gates).
- **The script must lock before shooting**: *Xin Shu* disbanded its unit two weeks into shooting in 2010, "because the last episode's script was delivered only **a week before shooting began**, leaving inadequate preparation for the shoot, and revision notes from every quarter arrived in the middle of production, so the unit had to stop and revise" (p0076). The institutional reason (p0194): a Chinese television drama "has no possibility of a trial run (**Chinese dramas are generally distributed during or after production, unlike America where you can adjust while shooting**)" — **China has no pilot and no adjust-while-airing, so all the risk sits on the script stage.**
- **But "rewriting until the wrap" is still the norm** (p0121): the causes include actors, locations, weather, budget considerations and artistic considerations. The model contract's default is "revisions during shooting are not separately paid" (2.2.5) — extra payment must be separately agreed.

### 7.3 Script Evaluation and the Producer's Notes

- **The script review meeting is a budget line** (p0145–0146): "having settled the subject or completed a first draft, a producer will often find experts to appraise the subject or the script, or convene a seminar… **and these activities also cost money.**" → The **script appraisal meeting / seminar** is a formal review stage specific to China. The contract also has an arbitration clause: "where the parties dispute whether the script meets the requirements for shooting, they may commission mutually acceptable experts to appraise it. **If the script passes review by the relevant state authority, it is deemed to meet the standard**" (p0317).
- **Seven elements of a good script** (p0071–0074, directly usable as a checklist): subject and angle ("**some subjects are considerably harder to write, especially those restricted by policy, and charging straight at them may be fatal**") / theme (*Soldiers Sortie*'s "never abandon, never give up"; "an army show with no women that ended up sweeping the country… **the story is fresh, and the theme is eternal**") / style (consistent throughout, landing on characterization, narrative method and plot pace) / character ("**a television drama's motive force lies in the character's pursuit of his or her 'desire'**"; "**the antagonist must be as strong as the key character. The 'antagonist' need not be a person; it may be a predicament**") / structure (four types, see SKILL.md 5.6) / plot ("a watchable drama contains a dramatic progression of introduction, development, turn and resolution inside every scene's plot") / pace ("**pace depends on the speed at which the story advances and the number of events happening at the same time**").
- **A real producer's notes** (*Before the Dawn*, three notes from producer Liu Dehong to writer Huang Ke, p0060): ① at the outset he required the story's environment to be set on the Nationalist side (character design is unrestricted, the images look good, the emotional material is easier); ② once the story outline was done, **cut the literally depicted "Trojan Horse operation"** — "that plot is only background to the event… and depicting and shooting it literally **would take a substantial amount of screen time away from the protagonist's line of action**"; ③ after the first draft, **change the male lead from "Sailor" to Liu Xinjie** — "'Sailor' has a concealed identity and cannot act openly, so his material and the Eighth Bureau's material cannot be tightly interlocked structurally, **which would produce a story structure of two flowers on separate branches.**" → The shared logic: **the protagonist must be able to act openly; he must sit inside the same plot structure as the institution of the main battlefield; and background events may not be literally depicted.**

### 7.4 The Red Lines of the *General Rules for Television Drama Content Production* (31 December 2015)

Issued by: the **Television Production Committee of the China Radio and Television Association and the China Television Drama Production Industry Association**; nature: an industry self-regulation code (not an administrative regulation), based on the *Provisions on the Administration of Television Content*.

- **Article 3's eight advocacies** (the positive sell-point list): of these, "**take contemporary subjects as the main body**, close to reality, close to life, close to the people" most directly affects subject choice.
- **Article 4's eleven categories that may not be produced** (broad prohibitions: the constitution / national unity / state secrets / ethnicity / religion / social order / obscenity, gambling and violence / insult and defamation / social morality / minors, and so on).
- **Article 5's ten classes and sixty-odd items that may not appear** (the red-line table; the full classification is in the reference). The ten class headings: ① not in keeping with national conditions and the social system, damaging to the national image, endangering national unity and social stability; ② damaging to ethnic unity; ③ contrary to national religious policy; ④ promoting feudal superstition, contrary to the scientific spirit; ⑤ dwelling on terror and violence, displaying ugly behaviour, possibly even inducing crime; ⑥ dwelling on obscenity, pornography and vulgar low taste; ⑦ insulting or defaming others; ⑧ distorting and belittling the fine cultural traditions of the nation; ⑨ endangering social morality and having a bad influence on minors; ⑩ other content prohibited by law, regulation and state provision. **The seven with the greatest effect on writing**: making a negative character the main object of portrayal; dwelling on and exaggerating social problems and over-representing the dark side of society; promoting spirit possession, reincarnation, witchcraft and spellcasting; **exposing investigative methods and detection detail**; **blurring the value judgement between true and false, good and evil, beauty and ugliness**; displaying and promoting unhealthy views and states of marriage and romance (extramarital affairs, one-night stands, sexual freedom) and irregular sexual relationships; and **"reversing the verdict" on historical figures and events already judged, tampering with classics, and treating revolutionary history with excessive entertainment or playfulness.**
- **Article 6's three categories requiring expert vetting**: ① minority languages and scripts, the images of revolutionary pioneers and other important figures, particular symbols and insignia, and graphics and charts; ② plots portraying the people's army, armed police, state security, public security, judicial personnel, doctors, lawyers and other specific professions, groups and social organizations; ③ the costumes, sets and props of a particular historical period or occupational group. → **Touch any one of these and the script enters "expert reading," and time for revision must be left in the schedule**; content touching the law must also pass the unit's legal adviser (p0280).
- **Article 7 on textual norms**: subtitles, other than a calligraphic title, should be standard Chinese characters; lyrics, foreign-language lines, and words and symbols with a specific meaning should carry Chinese subtitles.
- ⚠️ **The 2015 version.** Since then the competent authorities have issued further documents (including regulation of "danmei adaptations," "idol cultivation" and "fandom"), and items have been added and removed; **the framework and phrasing of the ten classes remain in use, but any citation must carry a year and be checked against current documents.**

### 7.5 Workaround Techniques (actual practice from Zhang Wei's book)

1. **Cut the affair and the "third party," and make both parties already divorced** (in *Cell Phone*, Wu Yue becomes a female editor whose feelings "stop at propriety"; Fei Mo's improper romance with a female graduate student becomes "the female graduate student falls in love with his son," p0066). The admitted cost: "changes like these do **weaken the film's sharpness and impact.**"
2. **Cut incest and sexual violence and turn it into deep family feeling** (*Aftershock*, adapted from *Aftershock* the novella, cuts "the adoptive father's rape" and "replaces it with an adoptive father's deep love for his adopted daughter," on the grounds that the audience had just lived through the collective trauma of the Wenchuan earthquake, p0028).
3. **Convert antagonism involving political position into conflict inside a family** (*Tie Lihua* carries four genre styles — the mansion drama, bandit suppression, the war of resistance and espionage — on the logic of family melodrama, p0228–0232).
4. **Take a "punished the moment it appears" critical stance toward a sensitive industry, and replace scenes with dialogue** (*Tie Lihua* avoids tomb-robbing scenes, p0231–0232).
5. **Let excessively sharp or grey themes give way to family feeling, friendship and love** (the principle sentence at p0066).
6. **Assess the audience's emotional reaction to a tragic ending and the investment risk** — the complete reasoning for cutting the Cultural Revolution section from *Drawing Sword* (p0291–0292, the frankest industry record in the book): "**it involves the Cultural Revolution and cross-strait relations**… it probably would not get past the broadcasting bureau's review… **this was cut not because it was untrue but because it was too true.** It gave a real sense of history, but it was too bitter, too raw, past the line. **For a production group that must think first about economic interest, that risk cannot be taken.**" The solution: **distil a spiritual slogan that can run through the whole show** ("the drawing-sword spirit"), and move the narrative's centre of gravity to the writable period, "not only preserving the essence of the characters but shortening the length and concentrating the narration."
7. **Costume drama as a workaround** (Zhang Mingzhi p0069): "some creators believe that setting a story in 'antiquity,' as a historical space, **avoids many of the minefields of contemporary drama and gains freer room to work.**" The frank reason for war-of-resistance dramas: "**in an era that advocates harmony, presenting sharp conflict on screen is genuinely hard, so 'fighting the Japanese' became the dramatic conflict audiences enjoy and creators find easy to present**" — ⚠️ with a note that in **2013** the authorities had already moved to clean up "absurd anti-Japanese dramas."

### 7.6 The First, Second and Third Stages of Creation, and Where the Writer Sits

- Yao Kougen (p0063): **the first stage of creation = the script; the second = performance and shooting; the third = editing and assembly.** A writer must recognize that "writing a script is not desk-bound literary creation but **a catalyst by which another art form comes into being.**"
- Zhang Mingzhi's identical structure (p0126): "in completing a television drama there are **at least three processes of re-creation**" — the writer conceiving and writing it → the director converting it into images → post-production editing. And a warning: at the "locking" stage of the edit, "**the producers and the distributor are invited to view it**, and they will raise revision notes from their own experience and angle" — **the story structure gets moved once more on the editing bench.**
- **Where the writer sits in the organization** (a structural fact): the organization chart of a production unit's five departments (production / directing / camera / art / sound), figure 4-1, contains **no writer** (p0079); yet the fee table 7-1 formally lists the writer among the "**principal creative personnel**," and "cast and crew fees are usually **calculated per episode**" (p0146); a writer's fee is a **zero-based budget item** in the budget, with "no clear expenditure standard," negotiated case by case at market rates (p0151–0152). The one place a writer appears in the on-set process is the rehearsal (p0117–0118): "the director, the actors and the writer can take the opportunity to discuss the script together." The difference between collective creation and the American room is in `sw-writers-room`.

### 7.7 Ratings, Platforms and Scheduling Constraints (2014–2016 data, out of date; the structure still holds)

- **The platform determines the content** (p0021–0022): CCTV (and especially CCTV-1 prime time) requires that "both the choice of subject and the construction of the story be strictly required and 'specially treated,'" with an older audience and demands for dignity and scale; provincial satellite channels "consider ratings potential above all, and prefer something slightly lighter and more entertaining"; and the internet "has relatively lower requirements on subject matter and artistic quality." The platform's three-tier decision (p0064): "**acquisitions staff — the review panel — the station management meeting**; buying a drama is a group decision."
- ⚠️ From New Year's Day 2015, "**one drama, two satellite channels**" and "**two episodes a night**"; in 2012 the subject proportions were **major subjects 1% / historical 43% / contemporary 56%** (p0188); annual output is around 15,000 episodes, of which "fewer than 80% achieve a transaction, and **only 30–40% actually reach viewers through a television station**" (p0189).
- **The standard operation for enlarging an audience** (p0063): "a spy drama's target audience may be older male viewers, **but weaving an interesting emotional line (especially a romance) into a spy drama may extend the audience to women; and creating several young, attractive characters and introducing fashionable elements may extend it to the young.**"
- **The ratings cost of dialect** (Zhang Wei p0224): the humour of *Borrowing a Gun*'s dialogue "has marked **north-China dialect characteristics**," and the finale rated **8–10%** in Beijing and Tianjin against **under 5%** in Shanghai and Hangzhou at the same time; the background judgement is that "this dialect culture has produced **marked regional ratings differences between north and south along the Yellow River.**" The response: weaken the dialect feel in a show aimed nationally; increase the dialogue's regional address in a show aimed at a particular region. **Distinguish the camps by the feel of the speech, not by transliterating the dialect** (Zhao Binbin on *Double-Sided Tape*: a Shanghai speech-feel against a northern one).
- **The schedule constrains the writing in reverse** (Zhang Mingzhi p0112–0115, p0122): day and night scenes at the same location must be groupable ("shoot all the day scenes first and then the night, to reduce the number of lighting changes"); deliberately write a batch of small scenes with **few actors, no special location requirement, and unaffected by weather and environment** for flexible scheduling; put big scenes and scenes with many actors early ("so that the further into the schedule, the smaller the unit and the more flexible the logistics"); do not schedule a heavy scene on day one; **a drama has dozens or even a hundred interior and exterior locations, and generally each costs money**, so "settings should not be too scattered and should be concentrated as far as possible"; and "**some dramas require the destruction of expensive props for plot reasons**" is a cost the script can avoid (p0147).

### 7.8 The Screenwriter's Contract (a 2016 model, based on the 2010 Copyright Law)

**Six clauses to fight for** (the *Contract for Engaging a Film (Television Drama) Screenwriter*, p0329–0338, the most writer-protective of the models):

1. **A review deadline and a default clause** (1.5): "**if Party A does not notify Party B of the review result or revision notes within the period agreed in this clause, Party A is deemed to have confirmed and accepted the script files Party B submitted.**"
2. **A limit on major changes** (1.7, the most writer-protective clause): "in order to protect Party B's right of integrity in the work from infringement, **Party A may not demand revisions contrary to the creative intent of the agreed writing plan, or to the script's principal characters, principal events, form or other principal aspects.** Unless Party A is willing to pay an additional fee and Party B agrees." → Four protected objects: **creative intent / principal characters / principal events / form.** Use 1.7 to limit clause 6.1, "Party A holds the final right of revision over the literary script."
3. **Reversion of rights** (4.11): "if within ＿ years from the date Party B… delivers the script, **Party A has not shot a film (television drama) based on that script, Party A's… production right and copyright are declared terminated, and those rights vest in Party B.**"
4. **A remake requires separate permission** (4.7): "if Party A remakes the television drama under this contract, **it must separately obtain Party B's permission.**"
5. **Three tiers of downstream royalties** (2.4): ＿% of gross cover price on audio-visual product distribution; ＿% of the licence contract's value on licensing to a third party; and for a television broadcast licence, "**for each occasion of income received, a royalty of ＿% of that licence contract's value shall be paid to Party B.**" Plus additional payment for episodes beyond the contracted count (2.3).
6. **Credit remedy** (8.1(4)): where a credit is omitted, replaced, or a co-author added without permission, "**the distributed copies shall immediately be recalled, corrected and re-released**… and where it has already been broadcast or screened, Party A is responsible for **eliminating the effect, publicly apologizing and compensating.**"

**Four unfavourable clauses to read carefully** (the *Commissioned Creation Contract for a Television Drama Script*, 2.1–2.2, p0318–0319): Party A "**permanently owns all rights in the script and in the entire copyright of the drama and its derivative products**" (including character names, character designs, dialogue and music); Party A may transfer it to others to shoot and "Party B may not interfere"; "where the script still fails to meet Party A's requirements after revision, Party A reserves the right to **engage other personnel** to revise and create… and the revisions other writers make to the script **shall not be regarded as infringing Party B's rights**"; and in a disagreement, "the script shall be completed on the basis of Party A's opinion as the final version, **and Party B acknowledges and accepts this and may not publicly express objection.**" Two clauses in the same contract favourable to the writer: the credit "**does not exclude Party B's right of attribution, with the order of credits determined by the proportion of the parts created**" (2.2.3); and "**regardless of whether Party A ultimately uses Party B's script for shooting, so long as the script Party B submits meets the contract's requirements, Party A shall still pay the fee as agreed**" (2.2.8).

**The copyright framework** (as of the 2010 amendment of the Copyright Law; ⚠️ it was amended again in 2020: article numbers changed and "cinematographic works and works created by methods analogous to cinematography" became "audiovisual works," **but the substantive framework is unchanged**):

- Article 15: copyright in the drama as a whole vests in the **producer** (the entity, not the natural person called "the producer"); "but the writer, director, cinematographer, lyricist, composer and other authors enjoy **the right of attribution** and are entitled to **remuneration** under their contract with the producer" (p0269).
- **The writer's protective clause** (p0270–0272): "the authors of works within a film or television work **that can be used independently** (such as the script, the music and the art design) **may exercise their copyright independently without the producer's consent**, and the producer may not interfere." → A script may be published independently or adapted into a novel or a stage play — **but this is stripped away the moment the contract says "all copyright vests in Party A."**
- Two written authorizations for an adaptation project (p0268): the **adaptation right** from the source's copyright holder; and if the source is itself an adaptation (an animation, a stage play, a film or drama), **a double authorization from the original writer plus the original rights holder.** Where documentary literature, real people or historical figures are involved, the producer must warrant in writing that consent has been obtained from **the persons concerned or their close relatives**, with personality-rights disputes borne by the producer (contract 5.2.7).
- The moral rights (publication, attribution, revision, integrity) **cannot be transferred or inherited**, and the protection of attribution, revision and integrity **is unlimited in duration** (p0272–0274).
- Product placement is a contractual obligation: "Party A shall coordinate with the drama's **writer**, director, leads and other unit members to ensure that the placement appears in the drama as agreed between Party B and the advertising client"; and the creative script for the placement "**shall be submitted ＿ days before the drama starts shooting**" (p0307–0311).

# Chinese Series Practice: the Creative System and the Production Chain

The writing system, document chain, adaptation craft and production/censorship chain of mainland-Chinese television drama. Five sources each do their own job:

- **Yao Kougen, *An Introduction to Television Drama Writing*** (Shanghai Theatre Academy's series on dramaturgy, Shanghai People's Publishing House, revised first edition January 2016) = **the academy system**: the three natures of the medium, the taxonomy, four script formats, structural laws with the episode as the unit, four tiers of suspense, the study of the scene, the 36 situations.
- **Zhang Wei et al., *A Course in Television Drama Adaptation*** (China Film Press, first edition September 2014) = **the craft of adaptation**: the five-source typology, sell-point migration, the thematic coordinate system, the passage-by-passage copy/delete/adapt pass, six episode- and scene-level case studies.
- **Zhang Mingzhi & Song Peiyi, *A Course for Television Drama Producers and Executive Producers*** (industry training material of the China Radio and Television Association's Television Production Committee, China Radio, Film and Television Press, first edition October 2016) = **the production chain**: the four forms of a script and the "eye of the drama," hard running-time data, eight administrative regimes, two stages of censorship, the scene-breakdown sheet, contracts, copyright.
- **Zhao Binbin, *A Course in Adapting Film and Television Fragments*** (China Film Press, January 2016) = **fragment adaptation**: the craft of **compressing** a finished film into an 8–20 minute stage fragment, the opposite direction from Zhang Wei's **expansion**.
- **The Chinese translations of Douglas's *Writing the TV Drama Series*** (trans. Yan Min, Shanghai Joint Publishing, April 2009 = the English 2nd edition) and **Blum's *Television and Screen Writing*** (Huaxia Publishing, 2003) = **terminology**: the official Chinese renderings against current usage.

American method itself is not here: episode act structure is in `sw-series-structure`, engine and bible in `sw-series-engine-bible`, the writers' room in `sw-writers-room`, the page-layout rules for the Chinese numbered-scene format in `sw-format-adaptation` §2.2, general dialogue method in `sw-dialogue`, and American case studies in `sw-series-case-studies`. This skill writes only "how Chinese series do it themselves" and "how that corresponds to and differs from the American system."

Format samples, sample planning documents, a single episode's scene table, the lists of 36 situations and 36 comic situations, and the terminology dictionary are in [reference.md](reference.md) (§1–12); the six adaptation case comparisons, the index of 26 fragments and the red-line classification are in [reference-cases.md](reference-cases.md) (§13–15).

⚠️ Policies, institution names, rates and the platform landscape are all labelled with the year of their source book (2014 / 2015 / 2016), and must be checked against current documents before being cited.

## 1. Correspondence and Difference Between the Chinese and American Systems

| Issue | Chinese series | American orthodoxy | Which to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structural unit | **Episode / movement / scene.** "Drama needs acts and scenes, film needs scenes and shots, **and television drama needs scenes, movements and episodes**" (Yao p0188) | Acts plus act outs | Use episode-movement-scene for documents going to a Chinese platform; make a separate act table when pitching to Americans |
| The lineage of closing each episode | **Li Yu's *Casual Expressions of Idle Feeling*, "the close"**: "hold the situation in suspense, quiet the drums a little, **and let people wonder what follows, not knowing how this will turn out**" (Yao p0260), plus the Song-dynasty storyteller's "to know what happens next, listen to the next instalment," plus Dickens's weekly serials, plus the radio serial's end-of-episode narration (Yao p0272, p0044) | The act out comes from the radio-and-television advertising system | Use the Chinese lineage to explain "why an episode must close on a hook"; use the American system to compute page anchors |
| Repetition | **In long-form, "repetition rises to the status of an essential technique"** (Yao p0313): repetition of pattern, of plot, of detail and of head-and-tail are all encouraged | The economy of information; repetition = wasted pages | Use the former for 30+ episode long-form with two episodes a night; use the latter for a 10–13 episode short series |
| Protagonist agency | **A passive protagonist is allowed**: "always passive, **but keeping a desire alive inside that passivity**" (Yao p0179, on *Oshin*); endurance, self-justification and panic all count as counter-actions | A passive protagonist = a structural fault | The suffering-and-growth female legend and family melodrama may use the former, but she must be given "one active action inside each episode's passivity" |
| Season shape | **Closed long-form**: "Chinese television dramas are essentially shot and completed in full and then broadcast on a television station in one complete run, **so the story is more complete and the structure therefore tighter**" (Zhang Wei p0043) | An open engine plus a finale hook, designed for indefinite renewal | Satellite-channel dramas are always closed; web series or an IP with a planned sequel may be exceptions (the series version of *Myth* left an open ending, Zhang Wei p0211) |
| The bottle episode | **Vetoed.** A rejection Zhang Wei experienced personally: one episode had "only two characters, and neither of them a lead… **anyone can see at a glance that it is unprofessional and irregular**" (p0035) | A legitimate means of being both cheap and profound | Chinese projects compress cost instead by "shooting several episodes on the same set" (see [reference.md](reference.md) §7) |
| An episode without the protagonist | **Forbidden**: "the protagonist must appear in every episode with sufficient screen time" (Zhang Wei p0035) | An ensemble rotation episode is legitimate | — |
| Dialogue density | **Moderate redundancy is an industrial requirement**: viewers "often have many accompanying everyday activities… **so that a viewer who occasionally leaves the set does not easily lose the main information**" (Zhang Mingzhi p0055); "**action cannot outweigh language but must support it, and to a degree be subordinate to it**" (Zhang Wei p0046) | The economy of dialogue; action outweighs language | Use the former for long-form television; use the latter for web shorts and film-leaning work. General method is in `sw-dialogue`. ⚠️ **Split this into two halves**: **the volume** (dialogue above 50%, 16,000–18,000 characters an episode) is a hard constraint, **but only for 45-minute satellite long-form**; and **"action subordinate to language" as a priority is a conclusion drawn in the 2014 context of two episodes a night with viewers leaving the set** — it need not be followed when the first run is on a video platform (which can be paused and rewound), or negotiation scenes turn into recitation. |
| The stability of theme | **"The highest standard by which film and television are judged is not the state apparatus but the audience"** (Zhang Wei p0026); a theme must come complete with an enlightener, a reward-and-punishment mechanism and a protagonist who does not stray (p0232–0233) | Theme may be ironic, suspended or morally ambiguous | Use the former for satellite dramas aimed at the broadest audience |
| "Enter late, leave early" | **A same-name opposite**: Yao's "enter late" means **lay in atmosphere first, let the audience get familiar with the setting and accumulate expectation, and only then light the fuse** (p0367–0368) | Enter late = skip the lay-in and go straight into the conflict | Use Yao's version for long-form at 25–35 scenes an episode; use the American version for a 45–60 page American episode |
| The opening | **Tracing origins is forbidden**: "you must abandon and avoid tracing complex factors back to their origins, **and generally open on the wind already blowing and the rain already falling: the event is happening**… every necessary explanation must be slipped in as the event develops" (Yao p0044–0045) | A pilot must establish the world and the characters | A Chinese episode 1 has to do both, accomplishing the establishment by "slipping it in inside the event" |
| Unresolved threads | **Legitimate**: in a network structure, "many threads appear and disappear, **with heads and no tails**," and still function (Yao p0286) | Every line must pay off | — |
| The last act / end of episode | **Every episode must end by selling one more big hook**; closing is opening a new one (Yao p0268–0272). ⚠️ **The half-hour comedy exception**: the disagreement table in `sw-sitcom-comedy` gives the test as "ad slots → an act out is mandatory; no ads plus emotional stakes → an earned emotional moment can replace it." A Chinese-language, ad-free, emotionally driven half-hour comedy falls under both this row and that one — **follow that one, but still book a debt in the episode outline for a specified later episode to pay** (see the ledger unit in `sw-series-engine-bible`), so that "not selling a hook" does not mean "not producing a reason for the next episode." | **The last act only pays off and introduces no new information** (`sw-series-structure` diagnostic 15) | **A direct conflict; choose by medium**: a Chinese episode always follows the Chinese rule, and the American rule is used only for a season finale. The corresponding item in `sw-series-structure` already carries this note. |
| The governing formula | **"Film is written for the classic; television drama is written for the ratings"** (Yao p0042) | The showrunner auteur theory | This is the basis of Yao's "maximize popular appeal, entertainment, fashionability and spatial coverage" |

Three additions: **the fake cliffhanger** — Yao frankly concedes that "sometimes a feint is **better than nothing**, because at least there is suspense" (p0272), which the American system regards as cheating the audience; **the technical boundary between serial and anthology** — Yao draws it by whether a scene has an obvious ending, because an obvious ending means it "cannot produce the next wave… **and so turns a serial into an anthology**" (p0252); and **ready-made material banks** — Yao gives Polti's 36 situations and Li Tianji's 36 comic situations outright and says plainly that "they may also be borrowed" (p0207), where the American system treats formula as taboo.

### Before 1.2: How a "Page" Converts in a Chinese-Language Script (the conversion principle has moved to `sw-format-adaptation` §2.2, since it is medium-independent; only the series-specific figures remain here)

Chinese scripts have no page standard, only a character-count standard — yet buyers, platforms and every writing guide speak in pages. This pack converts uniformly with the chain below, and must state that the figure is a converted page count:

1. An American page ≈ one minute (derived from the American page layout, not the Chinese one). So read "N pages" first as "**N minutes of screen time**."
2. Characters per minute in Chinese comes from two sources: a series episode of 40–45 minutes runs about 12,000–15,000 characters (**300–370 characters a minute**); dialogue-driven work runs about 18,000 characters an episode (**about 400 a minute**), while action-heavy work runs 12,000–15,000 an episode (**about 270–330 a minute**).
3. The values to use: **370–400 characters a minute for dialogue-driven long-form; 300–330 for action- and reaction-heavy material (single-camera comedy, thriller, action).**

**Convert word counts from duration, and do not apply long-form figures by episode count**: a 28-minute half-hour at 330 characters a minute is about **9,200 characters an episode**, not 16,000–18,000; a 12-minute short is about 4,000. When you write "about N pages," write out the basis with it (character count ÷ characters per minute = converted pages), or the same draft will be read as two different lengths by two different senses of "page."

### 1.2 "N Episodes per Case" Is a Spectrum, Not a Yes/No Question

Yao's boundary (a scene with an obvious ending turns a serial into an anthology, p0252) **warns of a cost; it does not forbid a practice.** Chinese long-form actually sits at any point on this spectrum, and the value is "how many episodes one case or one stretch of events occupies":

| Episodes per case | Form | What it buys | The cost | Suits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The anthology (series) | Enter at any episode, rerun- and second-window-friendly, easy to cut clips | Characters do not accumulate, and around episode 12 the audience starts to feel the repetition | Sitcoms, professional anthologies, daily strips |
| 2–3 | The unit serial (mainstream Chinese long-form) | A complete satisfaction every 2–3 episodes while still holding character change | 24 episodes fit only about ten cases, so the subject matter can look under-mined | Professional dramas, police procedurals, period units |
| 4–6 | Block form (one big event per movement) | Each block can be written to the end, with large emotional capacity | The first two episodes are slow, and the platform's great fear of "not holding people through the first three" lives exactly here | Family dramas, ensembles, adapted long novels |
| One case a season | The pure serial | Maximum tension | No single episode works alone, which hurts reruns and second windows | Mini-series and short series |

**Three tests for picking a point**: ① if the platform wants second windows and reruns, move toward 1–3; ② if the subject's supply of disputes or cases is limited (count how many kinds there are at most), move toward 4–6, or you will run out by episode 8; ③ how much space the protagonist's change requires — do not put a slow-changing character into a one-case-per-episode show.

**Two legitimate hybrids**:
- **The ledger method** (recommended for the 2–3 episode band): the unit still closes, but each unit leaves a debt for a specified later episode to pay, so that "having an obvious ending" no longer means "not producing the next wave" — which sidesteps exactly the cost Yao warns of. See rule 18 of `sw-series-engine-bible`.
- **The three-track method**: the A case closes this episode, the B case spans 3–6 episodes, and the C line runs through the whole season, all three running at once. The cost is having to serve three rhythms every episode, and the episode outline must mark where each of the three tracks stands in each episode.

**What not to do**: refuse to commit in the proposal. Pick no point on the spectrum and the 24 episodes you write will be half anthology and half serial, and the platform's first reader will ask "what kind of show is this exactly?"

## 2. Hard Constraints of Volume and Duration

- **Episode count**: Zhang Wei p0033–0034, "the total length is generally between **20 and 60 episodes**. Serials under 20 episodes are very rare at present," at about **40 minutes** an episode; Zhang Mingzhi p0055, "Chinese television dramas currently run commonly to **30–50 episodes.**" Prime time runs **two episodes a night** (a convention begun with *Debts of Sin*, Zhang Wei p0017).
- **Episode duration** (Zhang Mingzhi p0048): **a full 45 minutes, with the programme proper no shorter than 41 minutes 30 seconds; the title sequence is usually 60–90 seconds and the end credits 120–150 seconds.** → **Write an episode to 41.5 minutes, not 45**, and leave room for the "next episode" trailer (p0143).
- **Word count** (Zhang Wei p0076–0077): **a 30-episode script runs at least 400,000 characters, with dialogue mostly above 50% of the total volume**; popular novels run 150,000–300,000 characters, which is why "the great majority of adaptation work is not a matter of simply breaking a novel into scenes and shooting it, but an entirely fresh act of creation."
- **Episode count can change**: contracts state that "the episode count is subject to final approval by the National Radio and Television Administration," and adding episodes means adding investment proportionally (Zhang Mingzhi p0302, with the institution's 2016 name). A screenwriter's fee is paid per episode (table 7-1, p0146) — which is where the economic structure of "padding" lives.

The three natures of television drama (performance art plus screen art plus the television medium) and the taxonomy (six classification systems, with the full entries for the seven categories of single play / anthology / medium-length serial / long serial / docudrama / sitcom / studio drama) are in [reference.md](reference.md) §1.

## 3. The Document Chain

There is no single document chain in Chinese series; **three coexist**, and before writing you must ask which one the other party uses.

> **The client's format requirements outrank every convention in this skill.** The three chains below, the line format of an episode outline, and the per-episode word-count and scene-count ranges are all **defaults**, not hard constraints. If they give you page counts, word counts, columns or a template, follow theirs; this skill's conventions only fill the blanks they left unspecified. The typical conflict: they want "100 characters per episode in one paragraph," while this skill's episode-outline convention wants three things (the eye of the drama, the event, the closing state) — compress the three, do not exceed the word count, and keep the expanded version in a separate file for your own use (see "who is this page for" in the `sw-series-engine-bible` diagnostic checklist).

**A. Yao Kougen (the academic / development register, p0328–0348)**: three systems of script development = an adaptation / a work for hire / a spec script. Adaptation sources divide further into "**traditional classics**" (a diachronic vertical set: classic novels, plays and films) and "**crowd-selected classics**" (a synchronic horizontal set: web novels, comics, games, "selected by accumulating quantity through votes, clicks, purchases, reading and viewing"). A planning document = **a plot summary plus character design plus principal settings plus a statement of concept**, and then **a sample script**:

The extent and mandatory contents of all six documents (plot summary / character design / principal settings / statement of concept / plot flow / sample script) are tabulated in [reference.md](reference.md) §2.

⚠️ **Yao does not use "episode outline / scene outline"** (his chain is story synopsis → sample script → the full script); "character biography" appears at p0147 as "a character's event biography."

**B. Zhang Mingzhi (the producer's assessment register, p0067)**: **① the story outline** (time, place, principal characters, relationships, plot development; "it is entirely possible to judge from it whether the story has a distinctive spark and whether there is anything new") → **② the episode outline** ("it shows the whole show's chapter structure, how the conflicts rise, develop, turn and resolve, and **the 'eye of the drama' of each episode**"; where several people write together it "**carries still greater importance in holding the whole together**") → **③ the episode scripts** ("the main body is a small amount of plot exposition and **a great deal of actors' dialogue**"; "**a shooting budget can even be compiled from it**") → **④ the shot breakdown** (⚠️ "in current Chinese television production, because schedules are tight and there is a great deal of plot, **few directors make a formal shot breakdown**").
→ **The "eye of the drama" is the central term in Chinese episode-outline assessment: every episode must have an eye that can be identified in one sentence.** The budget is computed from the episode scripts, so changing the script moves the budget.

**C. The contract register** (the *Contract for Engaging a Film (Television Drama) Screenwriter*, 1.4, p0330): **the script concept → the master outline and character biographies → the episode outline → first drafts of episodes N to M (deliverable and accepted in batches) → the final locked script.** The *Commissioned Creation Contract for a Television Drama Script* (p0318) uses a different chain: **story outline → episode outline → first draft → second draft → the locked script before shooting**, corresponding to six payment stages (see [reference.md](reference.md) §7).

⚠️ **"Scene outline" appears in this body of sources only in Zhang Mingzhi's description of the American writers' room** (p0060, as the Chinese rendering of "outline"), and **is not a standard Chinese deliverable.** The Chinese equivalent is the **scene-breakdown sheet** — a production document made by the script supervisor after the script locks (see [reference.md](reference.md) §3).

## 5. Structural Laws With the Episode as the Unit

### 5.1 The Episode Skeleton: 3 Minutes Plus 15 Minutes Plus the Episode End (Yao p0259, excerpted as written)

> The first three minutes must grab the audience, then a small hook every 15 minutes, and at the end of each episode sell one more big hook… As for needing suspense every 15 minutes, **that used to be for the commercials… now it is about the sectioning inside an episode, taking 15 minutes as one plot point and building a so-called "fragmentary dramatic structure."** That is to say, **every section must have a "local moment of vividness," and you must periodically reel in the kite you have let fly**, raising one small climax after another… something is about to explode into a bloody catastrophe… and I am sorry, that will have to wait until tomorrow.

**The key: the 15-minute node survived into an ad-free era and was reinterpreted as an internal need of the "fragmentary dramatic structure."** The hard rule: "**each 15 minutes is one time block, and this segmentation and the 45-minute episode are essentially fixed**… with at most a minute's flexibility either way" (p0272). The production reality: "sometimes… depending on the director's taste, plot is arbitrarily expanded or reduced, so that a block's ending arrives early or late. **But once that is done, it destroys the section's suspense and the episode-end suspense and damages the ratings. A strict producer will generally not agree to it.**"

### 5.2 Four Tiers of Suspense (Yao p0270–0275)

1. **The whole show's overarching suspense**: "a number of small suspenses interlock to form the overarching suspense, and resolving the overarching suspense depends on solving one small suspense after another." Two ways to pose it: pose it directly and then extend into branches (*Yongzheng Dynasty*: the treasury is empty → raising funds in the south → recovering the silver); or **bring a main suspense that has not yet occurred to the front and then narrate backwards how this state of affairs came about** (*My Fair Princess*, part one's "the true and false princess," part two's "hunting the princess"). An anthology's overarching suspense = **"the endless attraction and repulsion between the sexes"** (Hunter, Hotel, *Stories from the Editorial Board*: "the two of them never get as far as talking about marriage, and **the author continually blocks the audience's expectation that they will end up together**," p0271).
2. **End-of-episode suspense** (⚠️ **a show released all at once needs a different reason**; **and a show airing simultaneously on a platform and a satellite channel — the commonest Chinese case — needs both reasons**, because the satellite side has an overnight gap and the platform side does not, so the episode end must be both a real hook, for the appointment audience, and a booked debt, for the binge audience. The test: **first ask which side the show premieres on**, and the premiere side decides which reason is primary. The mechanism of this rule is the "gap" created by broadcasting on alternate days, and when a streamer drops a whole season the viewer clicks the next episode three seconds later, so **that gap does not physically exist.** You should still leave an open ending, but the basis changes to "**leaving a debt to be paid**" — what this episode resolves must be paid for in a specified later episode; see the ledger unit in `sw-series-engine-bible`. **Do not count on "thinking about it overnight" to hold the tension for you**: in a binge, a weak ending propped up by a hook is exposed at once). The mechanism is the "**gap**" — "the time between episodes constitutes a gap between two scenes of the plot… breaking the narration cleverly creates a stretched tension… **handing the conflict to the audience in fact leaves them room to think and guess.**"
3. **Block suspense** (every 15 minutes): also an opportunity for a transition. The multi-line specification: "**maintain as many as five or six simultaneous plot lines**," while still keeping to "one question" — "**whichever line it comes from, one problem must be solved before a new one can be raised.**"
4. **Opening suspense**: its duties are the opposite of the five for stage and film (see section 1). Opening paradigms from four dramas (*April Rhapsody*, *Oshin*, *Palace of Desire*, *Red Carnation*) are in the reference. The image: "**rapidly divert the flow, pulling the audience's attention away from real life or from the middle of some other stream of television information and into the drama, exactly as a newspaper or magazine headline does.**"

**Four rules for setting suspense** (p0262–0267): ① **base it on character** — the time, place, events and relationships of the situation must be understood by the audience; "**the character must win the audience's feelings**… a stage play or a film need not have someone lovable or someone detestable, **but television drama attends to this greatly**." ② **Travel with the situation** — the overarching suspense plus a series of small ones; the diagnostic sentence: "**where a show fails to hold an audience, the reason is usually not that the author failed to pose an overall suspense but that a progressively developing series of small suspenses is lacking.**" ③ **Use parallel montage to create an "ultimate limit"** — every time one suspense is resolved, immediately add a new one with a hard time limit, and **make the limit physical as a visible meter** (*Red Carnation*: the file-copying progress bar against the rising floor indicator of a lift). ④ **The "one question" principle** — five requirements: pose it as early as possible / reduce the number of threads / make it concrete and specific / make it strong / raise a new one the moment one is finished. The counter-example is the 30-episode *Huangchenggenr* of 1993 (nine concurrent mysteries plus four romance subplots, "the bag was not shaken out until the last three or four episodes, and the audience was left howling at having been run round in circles").

### 5.3 Five Techniques of Delay (Yao p0267–0268)

The beginner's typical failure: "usually **like the bear picking corn, taking one and dropping one.**"

1. **Gather storm clouds over someone's head, with the disaster growing** — "the more the problem is solved the more tangled it gets," while the final deadline closes in.
2. **Break off suddenly at a tense moment and leave it hanging** — "**if the interval is too long, revive it at the right time. The narration may be interrupted, but the situation must not weaken.**"
3. **Break one complete large action into many small beats, each with its own small suspense** — "if the protagonist is certain to meet a great disaster on the way to an appointment, set small problems at his door, on the stairs and on the road, **so that only by solving these small problems can he meet the final big one.**"
4. **Add plot** — expand into social breadth and psychological depth; let the conflict pull in new characters, and let new characters bring new events.
5. **Modulate the rhythm, tightening and loosening** — set in contrasts of "**strong and weak (force), fast and slow (speed), large and small (amplitude), and solid and void.**"

The rhythm formula (Yao's summary of *24*, S2E1, p0205): "**When still, like a tree; when acting, like lightning and thunder; when acting on feeling, diffuse like flowing water; when narrating an event, concentrating superior force like a military assault.**"

### 5.4 The First Three Episodes (Zhang Wei p0035–0037)

"Be sure to **establish the show's situation as early as possible, create the show's central conflict as early as possible, throw out the overarching suspense, and bring the protagonist on as early as possible.**" "In traditional thinking, Chinese aesthetics tends to save the good thing for later. But that idea is now clearly out of date."

**The censorship-driven version** (same page): "the reviewer… has a great many programmes to watch every day… and will certainly put their main energies and their focus on **the first few episodes** of a show… so even considered purely from the angle of censorship, an adapter should put the story's most compelling part… at the opening, **especially in the first three episodes.**"

Alongside it: **a central character who only comes into force in the back half must show their face in episode 1** (*Ma Wen's War* moved Li Qin's entrance from "when Li Yi and Yang Xin marry" up to episode 1, p0124).

The table of quantified per-episode metrics (scene count / plot points / main events / spacing of climaxes / number of reversals / word count), the three duties of a scene, and the double-line formula of a career line plus a private-life line with its examples are in [reference.md](reference.md) §4.

### 5.5 The Two-Step Situation Method and the Compounding Principle (Yao ch. 9, the mechanism that replaces "acts")

The engine of long-form (p0193): "at the opening a television drama must **set a situation** that constitutes the whole show's driving force… making the characters' psychological activity condense into concrete motives that lead to concrete actions. From there, the principal characters' actions pull other characters' actions along, produce new conflicts, constitute new events, and lead to **changes in the local situation**… advancing layer by layer toward the climax."

- **The writer's two steps** (p0202): "**the first step is to contrive a situation by every means; the second is to let the characters tumble and scramble inside it, always keeping a state of action.**" — Yao's version of the series engine.
- **The action a situation prescribes must repeat**: a revenge situation → "the grudge cannot be resolved easily, and the act of revenge usually has to be attempted again and again"; a getting-along situation → "a long process of wanting both independence and fusion"; a misunderstanding situation → "once created, the more it is explained the less clear it becomes."
- **The compounding principle** (p0203–0204, for long-form only): **give a long-form protagonist 4–5 situations of different kinds at once** (*Grand Mansion Gate*'s Second Mistress: a blood feud / the family fortune / a wayward son / competition in the trade / the survival of the old firm), **and specify that at least one of them transforms partway through** ("'revenge' turns into 'pursuit,' 'getting along' becomes 'competition,' and 'courtship' sometimes becomes 'revenge'").
- **The reversal is the Chinese series' substitute for the act out** (p0210): "**because television drama emphasizes the alternating appearance of several action lines and small climaxes on each line in every episode, it makes greater use of the reversal technique**… **television's 'reversals' are scattered through every episode and every section.**" The reverse-engineering method: to turn to the negative, make the positive case fully first, and vice versa (p0218).

### 5.6 Structural Types (a disagreement that must be preserved)

- **Yao Kougen** (ch. 13): four coexisting types — **dramatic / essayistic / psychological / presenter-led**, of which the last three are not built on conflict. Two further classifications: by character configuration (one person one event / one person many events, "the gourd-string form" / many people one event / many people many events, "the ensemble, exhibition form"); and by how the plot unfolds (open / closed). Three types of thread: single / double (main-and-sub, or parallel) / networked ("**a net has a rope**… there is always a main line exercising control, though it is sometimes not obvious").
- **Zhang Wei** (p0032, p0038): "**In television drama, an essayistic structure is almost impossible, let alone a psychological structure or a poetic one. Every television drama in the market belongs almost uniformly to the dramatic structure.**" Two basic kinds = chronological in time and space ("applicable to almost every television drama") and interleaved in time and space (commonest in time-travel material).
- **Which to use when**: anniversary dramas, platform commissions and 30+ episode satellite dramas follow Zhang Wei (dramatic plus chronological); single plays, docudramas, authorial projects and web shorts may use Yao's essayistic / psychological / presenter-led forms, but must accept the risk of being "loose in structure and weak in pull" — which Yao writes himself (p0288).
- **Zhang Mingzhi's third set** (p0073, from the producer's angle): ① the changing fortunes of one or several families (the mansion drama); ② changes in one or several sets of relationships (the family emotional drama); ③ the issuing and completion of a mission (police and spy dramas); ④ a character's growth (wuxia, youth idol drama); and "**these structural types may be used in combination within a single drama.**"

## 10. Disagreements Between the Books

| Issue | One side | The other | Which to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theme first vs. character first | Zhang Wei's theme chapter: "**theme is a guiding principle of adaptation**… settling the theme settles the scope, the genre, and even the relationships" (p0023) | Zhang Wei's character chapter opposes "theme first": "**without vivid, deep, living characters full of vitality, there is nothing. Once the characters exist, feeling, emotion, temperament and motive follow naturally**" (p0044) | A disagreement inside one book. The resolution: **theme decides "whether it can be adapted and in which direction"; character decides "whether what comes out is alive"** |
| Structural types | Yao: four coexisting types, arguing for the last three from an ER episode, *A Blizzard Tonight* and *A Woman Reporter's Voice-Over* | Zhang Wei: "**we have never once seen a successful television drama narrated with these structures**" (p0038) | See 5.6 |
| Is a romance line compulsory | Zhang Wei's general rule: "no television drama aimed at a broad audience can dispense with an emotional line" (p0085) | *Drawing Sword* has "**only one female character in the first 19 episodes**," and admits "the script even commits one of the taboos of television writing" (p0292) | Mainstream military and war dramas may be exceptions, but must have an emotional axis substituting for romance (comradeship, brotherhood) |
| Settle character first or pick the conflict first | Zhang Wei argues: settle the relationships first and let the conflicts grow from them | Zhang Wei himself notes that "contemporary television, mostly American, frequently uses '**effect determinism**' — pick the most watchable and effective conflicts first, and then use characters and plot to make them plausible" (p0242) | Genre shows (spy, police) may use effect determinism; family melodrama and period drama put relationships first |
| Writing an ensemble | American shows run several equally weighted A/B/C lines (Zhang Wei concedes that "this ensemble method, with different characters each holding an independent action line, appears mostly in American shows") | Zhang Wei's localization: **one absolute main line plus two supporting lines carrying genre elements** (p0164, *Mei Lanfang*) | Use the latter for the Chinese mainstream audience; the supporting lines exist "to supply the show with other genre elements" |
| Whether to write shots | Zhao Binbin: "**you must describe the story in shots**" (p0014); Yao's format 3 also takes "a shot or a group of shots as the sentence unit" | `sw-format-adaptation`: never mention the camera | Do not write camera terminology in a draft for the market or a platform; read the Chinese textbook position as "think in shots when conceiving" |
| Character list / biography | Zhao's fragment format **requires them**; "the master outline and character biographies" is a mandatory deliverable in a Chinese contract | Anglo-American specs explicitly forbid them | Decide by audience |
| Closed vs. open | Zhang Wei: Chinese series are closed long-form broadcast in one run (p0043) | The series version of *Myth* left an open ending for a sequel (p0211); Yao: "television drama… **has no ending; the story is never finished**" (p0045) | Satellite dramas are closed; web series and IP shows with a planned sequel may leave an open ending |

## 11. Diagnostic Checklist

1. **Volume**: is an episode computed at 41.5 minutes? 25–35 scenes, of which 4–5 are major scenes? (**The unit**: when diagnosing, compare the **scene numbers on the breakdown sheet** directly against this range — one scene is usually one numbered scene, and an occasional long scene may span two; do not convert first, and do not go back to the theoretical distinction between "setting" and "scene," which is a concept for writing, not a unit for measuring. Hand me an outline that says "52 scenes" and it is 1.5 times over the range.) About 13 plot points an episode? 400,000 characters over 30 episodes, with dialogue reaching 50%?
2. **The episode skeleton**: is there something gripping in the first 3 minutes? Is there a "local moment of vividness" every 15 minutes? Is the big hook at the episode's end a real hook or a feint (feints are permitted, but not every episode)?
3. **The opening**: does the first line write "what is happening" or trace origins? Is the exposition slipped in inside the event?
4. **The first three episodes**: are all four things — the situation, the central conflict, the overarching suspense and the protagonist — delivered inside the first three? Does a central character who only comes into force later show their face in episode 1?
5. **Every episode**: is the protagonist present with enough screen time? (**The exception**: the protagonist may be absent for a whole episode, but only if both preconditions of `succession-series-writing` S7 are met — they are still exerting will while absent, and the episode is *about* their absence; and their presence must already have a stock of accumulated weight. **The question to ask is "did the absence generate action," not "are they present."**) Is there one main event? Is there any episode left with only two non-lead characters playing a scene?
6. **Suspense**: is the overarching suspense one question, or nine mysteries (the *Huangchenggenr* disease)? Is there a progressively developing series of small suspenses? Is a new suspense with a hard time limit added the instant one is resolved, and is the limit made physical as a visible meter?
7. **Delay**: which of the five techniques are used? After breaking off, is it revived when the interval grows long? Is there any "bear picking corn" — a good pivot used once and dropped?
8. **Situation**: how many situations does the protagonist carry at once? Does one of them transform partway (revenge → pursuit, getting along → competition)? Has the action a situation prescribes been repeated enough times?
9. **Lines**: do the career line and the private-life line each have a goal, an obstacle and a climax? Are they causes of each other? Does a subplot upstage the main line? Are there more than 5–6 parallel lines? Whichever line it is, does it keep to "solve one problem before raising a new one"?
10. **Repetition**: which are used — pattern repetition (a three-step pattern once an episode), detail repetition (one running prop), head-and-tail repetition? Does the **meaning** of each repetition change (or it is mechanical repetition)?
11. **Adaptation**: have you summed the source up in one sentence? Have you counted how many protagonists and how many lines it gives you, and whether that fills the target episode count? Have you made a passage-by-passage [copy / delete / adapt] table? Have the sell points been migrated (replace every sell point exclusive to the source's own audience, keep the keywords)? Is the expansion done with the four methods, or with padding? Have the new characters passed the "three questions"? Is there a purely platonic line between two new characters?
12. **Dual timelines**: are the switches stitched by the same object, place or scene? Is any single line too long, or the interleaving too dense?
13. **Theme**: is there an enlightener (and not the protagonist)? Is behaviour that violates the theme punished and behaviour that follows it rewarded? Has the protagonist's motive strayed from the theme?
14. **Format**: have you chosen one of the four formats and kept it consistent throughout? Do the scene headings state the location, interior/exterior, time and day/night? Do revisions use 19A/19B and "66 deleted"?
15. **Scheduling**: are set names consistent throughout? Are similar locations distinguished? Can day and night scenes at the same location be grouped? Have you written a batch of small scenes with "few actors, no special location, unaffected by weather" for flexible scheduling? Are there expensive props that must be destroyed, or avoidable crowd scenes and night shoots?
16. **Censorship**: have you gone item by item through article 5's ten classes in the *General Rules* (2015)? Is a negative character the main object of portrayal? Do you expose investigative methods or detection detail? Is the line between good and evil clear? How are extramarital affairs, one-night stands and irregular sexual relationships handled? Is there any "reversing the verdict" on a historical figure already judged, any playful treatment of revolutionary history, or any promotion of reincarnation or witchcraft?
17. **Expert reading**: does the script touch the military, armed police, state security, public security, the judiciary, doctors, lawyers, minority languages and scripts, revolutionary pioneers, specific insignia, or the costumes, hair and props of a specific historical period? Has time for expert reading and revision been left in the schedule?
18. **Delivery**: which document chain is the other party using (Yao's planning-plus-sample / the producer's four forms / the contract's delivery sequence)? Have you obtained **written approval signed by both the producer and the director** at each stage? Does the contract contain review deadlines and default clauses? Have you won limits on major changes (creative intent / principal characters / principal events / form), reversion of rights, remake permissions, the three royalty tiers, and a credit remedy?
19. **Start of shooting**: was the script locked before shooting began? Do you know whether the project has passed filing and approval, and whether the money is in place?
20. **Fragment adaptation (if working in the compression direction)**: has it passed the veto gate (not two people sitting and talking)? Has it been merged into one main setting plus one transitional area? Is the cut point at "the moment the motive has just been established"? Did you write the ending yourself?

## 12. Workflow: a Chinese Series From Planning to Submission

1. **Choosing the subject** (Yao p0329, four principles): it genuinely matters, you are genuinely interested, and it is **within your reach**; you can command **abundant first-hand material**; you have **made a new discovery** among a mass of phenomena; and "**you are not creating for the sake of creating.**" In parallel, do Zhang Mingzhi's three market-positioning factors (subject × audience × the creator's command of the subject) and objective setting (social benefit primary, or economic), and make a judgement about "audience demand a year or more from now" (a drama "takes at least a year from production to distribution and broadcast").
2. **If it is an adaptation**: assess the IP (Zhao Junkai's three foundations plus Zhang Mingzhi's two judgements) → confirm the adaptation rights and, where necessary, double authorization → sum the source up in one sentence and count its protagonists and lines → make the passage-by-passage [copy / delete / adapt] table → settle the thematic coordinate system → make the sell-point migration list.
3. **Settle genre and volume**: one main genre (it may be compounded, but only one goes in the filing document); the episode count (20/30/40/50); 41.5 minutes an episode; an estimate of the whole script's word count.
4. **Write the planning documents**: the plot summary (or 200–400 character per-episode summaries, each with a time anchor and each ending on an irreversible change) plus character design (three innovations per lead) plus principal settings (with location concentration already considered) plus the statement of concept.
5. **File / obtain project approval** (or do it in parallel): confirm whether it falls under the major revolutionary, major historical or major theoretical-documentary categories (the dual review channel); anything touching ethnicity or religion also goes through the ethnic affairs or religious affairs bureau; co-productions or foreign actors are reported to the foreign affairs department. (The 2016 picture; check current documents.)
6. **Story outline → episode outline**: write one **eye of the drama** per episode; lay out the first three episodes; lay out the four tiers of suspense; mark a climax every 3–5 episodes and at least two reversals across the whole show; give the protagonist 4–5 situations and arrange one transformation; lay in the career line plus the private-life line. **Get the producer's and the director's joint signature before writing on.**
7. **Character biographies and the master outline**: psychological and spiritual material goes in the biographies, not into the episode scripts (Zhang Wei p0046). Give anyone who needs to speak their heart a **confidant** (who may be a new character, or the enemy may take the role, Zhang Wei p0072–0073).
8. **The sample script (episode 1, or the first two)**: self-check against Yao's list of duties; pick one of the four formats and keep it consistent throughout; 25–35 scenes an episode.
9. **First drafts in batches → second draft → the locked script before shooting**: advance through the contract's gates at each stage; accept that "the script gets rewritten until the wrap" but write the additional payments into the contract; pass the script review meetings and seminars; and where specific professions, groups, costumes, hair and props are involved, pass expert reading and legal review.
10. **After the lock**: the script supervisor breaks the script into a **scene-breakdown sheet** and a scene sort sheet, and the schedule for shooting several episodes together begins; revisions use 19A/19B and "66 deleted"; creative scripts for product placement are locked before shooting and executed with the writer's cooperation.
11. **Submission**: the finished programme goes through content review plus technical review, and is revised per the notes "until the content review passes," after which the *Television Drama Distribution Licence* is issued and it may be distributed. Know that the story structure will be altered once more on the editing bench by the producers and the distributor.
12. **Going abroad (optional)** (Zhang Mingzhi p0201–0207): "international elements have to be built in from the production stage"; adapt actively to the buyer's requirements on programme format, episode length and content pace; and the standard method for taking long-form abroad is "**adapting the long serial into one or two television-film versions of about 100 minutes.**"

## 13. Exercises

1. **Copy the formats**: write the same three-page scene in each of Yao's four formats, and feel which one lets a director understand it fastest and which is easiest to publish.
2. **Calibrate to 41.5 minutes**: take an aired Chinese episode, time the title sequence, credits and programme proper, list its scene table (25–35 scenes), mark the 3-minute opening hook, the small hook every 15 minutes and the big hook at the end, and count whether the plot points come close to 13.
3. **The two-step situation method**: give one protagonist five situations of different kinds (revenge / getting along / misunderstanding / pursuit / competition), write out for each "the action the character must do repeatedly," and nominate one to transform in a specified episode.
4. **Sell-point migration**: take any bestselling novel, list three sell points and the audience profile for each, judge item by item which are useless for a television audience, and supply replacements (keeping the source's keywords as the shop front).
5. **The passage method**: take a 90–120 minute film, cut it into 8–12 passages by timecode and tag each, and from the table write a 30-episode expansion plan (where each of the four methods is used, how many characters you go from and to, how many relationship lines you go from and to).
6. **The four-tier suspense table**: for a 30-episode show, write the overarching suspense in one sentence; one sentence for each of the 30 end-of-episode suspenses; pick three episodes and write their three 15-minute nodes; and choose which of the four opening paradigms episode 1 uses.
7. **Red-line rewriting**: find a passage of a novel containing an extramarital affair, a morally grey setup or a superstitious element, rewrite it once per each of the six workaround techniques in [reference.md](reference.md) §7, and record what each version loses.
8. **The reverse exercise**: take the strongest scene in an episode of your own and rewrite it as a 10-minute, 4-person, single-main-setting stage fragment (writing the ending yourself, in Zhao Binbin's form), then compare: what makes this scene work in the series, and what makes it work on stage?
9. **The scene-breakdown sheet**: fill one of your episodes into the nine-field sheet by hand, sort by set name plus atmosphere, count how many sets you need, how many night scenes and how many scenes with extras, and then go back and revise the script so the set count drops by a third without losing the drama.
10. **The contract drill**: take the six screenwriter-contract clauses in [reference.md](reference.md) §7 and compare them item by item against a real contract, listing the clauses you would ask to change and why.

## 13. Zhang Wei's Six Adaptation Cases Compared

The six cases are examples one to six in Zhang Wei's *A Course in Television Drama Adaptation* (p0082–p0308), each demonstrating a different adaptation route. **Overview**:

| Example | The source (extent / genre) | The series (episodes) | The adaptation route | Written by (as credited in the book) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Go Lala Go*, a bestselling workplace novel, under 200,000 characters | 30-episode urban drama | **Sell-point migration**: a workplace handbook → a growth story | Teng Yang (Zhang Wei wrote the series) |
| 2 | Ye Zhaoyan's *Ma Wen's War*, a 30,000-character novella with 6 characters | 24-episode family melodrama | **Expansion by adding people**: 5 → 11 | Zhang Wei, Hou Ying, Peng Peijun |
| 3 | The life of Mei Lanfang (a real celebrity) | 40-episode period drama (**never produced**) | **Supplying a trait plus compounding genres** | Teng Yang |
| 4 | The film *The Myth*, 90 minutes | 50-episode time-travel drama | **Passage-by-passage copy/delete/adapt plus genre inversion** | Zhang Wei, Liu Yu |
| 5 | Yan Geling and Xiao Ma's *Tie Lihua*, a 160,000-character novel | 43-episode Republican-era female legend | **Thematic substitution**, "an almost complete break with the novel" | Zhang Wei, Chen Qiongqiong |
| 6 | Du Liang's *Drawing Sword*, a novel | 30-episode military drama | **Lift the whole pot plus cut the timeline** | Zhang Wei, Gao Boyang |

⚠️ **The warning on example 3 must travel with the case**: per entry 231 of the book's end-of-volume filmography (p0318), this 40-episode television version of *Mei Lanfang* "**was never put into production for various reasons**" (writers: Xiao ▢, Zhang Wei, Wang Qinan; director: Chen Guoxing). **What this chapter analyses is a script that was finished and never broadcast — every "ratings consideration" in it is an assumption made while writing, unverified by the market. It is the only one of the six with no broadcast data behind it.**

### 13.1 Example 1, *Go Lala Go*: a Novel → a 30-Episode Urban Drama (Zhang Wei p0082–0118)

- **The source's extent and genre**: a bestselling workplace novel published in September 2007 that sold over 100,000 copies that year; in February 2008 Shanghai Media Group bought the adaptation rights (p0082). **The hard capacity figure**: "**a plot capacity of under 200,000 characters is far from sufficient for 30 episodes of television**… so in the work of adaptation, **what matters more is not 'adapting' but 'composing'**" (p0084). The series' figures: a peak rating of 6.46 in Beijing; over 100 million views on a video site within 14 days (p0082).
- **Thematic coordinates**: switched from "a workplace handbook / practical office philosophy" to "**the growth story of the protagonist Du Lala — a girl who achieves personal growth through her career, growth that includes promotion but more importantly a fresh understanding and elevation of responsibility, family, friendship, love and even the self**" (p0086). The shop-front words are kept: "workplace," "white collar," "foreign firm," "metropolis" all retained, since "**they do not much subvert the original novel's theme or style**" (p0085). The coordinate-system image (figure 3, p0089): "**theme and style determine the script's direction on one plane (X = style, Y = theme), and the relationships are the 'Z coordinate' governing its volume.**"
- **Surgery on the principal characters**:
  - **Daisy: ex-girlfriend → ex-wife** (p0097–0104, the book's most brilliant single piece of character surgery). The diagnosis: "**in the novel Daisy is like a boss in a video game; the reader consumes the process of Du Lala's contest with her and does not consume Daisy as a character.**" The opposite mechanism in television: "**television is exactly the reverse: the audience cares about the characters first and therefore about their fates, and only by consuming characters can they consume the derivative product of a character's fate — the twisting plot.**" The effect of the surgery: "**it fixes a loose emotional relationship onto a solid social attribute**… and that change of status makes every mad act of Daisy's plausible. At the same time it adds obstacles to Du Lala and Wang Wei's path." The audience psychology behind it: "**everybody is right at their own starting point, and the greatest conflict arises only when one meets someone else's starting point that contradicts one's own.**"
  - **Rose: an ambiguous boss → an openly declared rival who is both enemy and friend** (p0104–0107). The diagnosis of the protagonist's passivity: "Du Lala does less taking the initiative than choosing a side… **and while that fits the early career of most viewers, for a protagonist this configuration lacks initiative**"; and another risk: "**at the show's opening Rose's material outweighs the protagonist's.**" After the change, Rose is "**a bad good person**" (against Daisy's "good bad person"): "**on one hand she suppresses Du Lala's promotion, and on the other she nominates her as an outstanding employee.**" The declaration of war (script 14-13): "As friends, this is all very well. But in the workplace, it does not work!"
  - **The new character Vivian (a workplace idol and a confidante)** (p0107–0110): not in the novel. The "**deferral–crossroads**" technique on expectation: "once this mentor-style conversation is done, the audience's expectation of Vivian has moved from deferral to standing at a crossroads: **if the next dramatic situation has Vivian making trouble for Du Lala, Vivian will be shaped as an out-and-out schemer; if Vivian then helps Du Lala, this emotional deferral will push the audience's goodwill toward her to its peak.**"
  - **New parents, plus binding in Li Hongming (the ex-boyfriend) and Xia Hong (the best friend)** (p0089–0097): one reason (p0089) — this show "must differ from the '**has a car, has a flat, both parents dead**' idol drama, and **family or friendship relationships are an important component connecting Du Lala to reality and to the audience.**" **Character deleted: Wang Qiang** (p0097).
- **Structural handling**: the novel has only the single through-line of the workplace, and "Du Lala's promotion process is mainly **upward**"; the series has three lines (workplace plus romance plus family), and stretches the workplace depth — "**changing Du Lala's promotion from the novel's administrative supervisor to HR manager into a rise from complete newcomer to middle manager**" (p0112). **The five new suspenses the series builds** (p0113, itemized as written): ① unemployed from a private firm and starting from nothing at DB, can she get promoted; ② betrayed jointly by her best friend and her ex-boyfriend, can she and Li Hongming resume, and what becomes of Li Hongming and Xia Hong; ③ having lied to her mother that she has bought a flat, can she buy one of her own; ④ her father has cancer — can she raise enough money, and can it be cured; ⑤ Wang Wei, wounded by his ex-wife Daisy, lacks confidence in women — can he truly come to love Du Lala. The premise judgement: "'**a girl who wants a promotion' cannot constitute suspense for an audience watching television.**" **The rhythm law** (p0113–0114): "**an emotional or event climax every 3 to 5 episodes; and sometimes the two coincide.**"
- **Transferable techniques**:
  1. **Sum the source up in one sentence first, and count how many protagonists and how many lines it gives you** (p0083).
  2. **A character's "entrance with material" = two linked scenes** (p0114–0117): the first establishes the trait and throws out a small suspense (Daisy in 1-20 buying a bag in the LV shop without looking at the price, paying with a supplementary card, while the sales staff mutter "she's probably somebody's mistress"); the second resolves the small suspense and throws out a bigger one, ending by hooking her to a main-line character (in 1-21, her American boyfriend breaks up with her, she hurls the LV bag at the television, and the television switches on to show Wang Wei accepting an award — **and Daisy freezes**). The governing principle: "**we cannot solve problems of visual presentation with adjectives and declarative sentences.**"
  3. **Land the theme with one scene that closes off two escape routes** (script 30-19, p0086–0087): the first question, "are you leaving me," closes off the escape of "there is a problem in the relationship"; the second, "can time change your decision," creates the career/love dilemma. "**If she gave up everything for Wang Wei, she would be no more than a woman desperate to marry who slaved away until she met a diamond bachelor. In the end, the Du Lala who chooses to leave quietly is the Du Lala who has been 'promoted.'**"
  4. **The contradictory demand IP audiences make** (p0117): "they want a drama that **still looks like the novel but must be better than the novel**, and they do not want the adapter making sweeping changes to the impression the novel has already formed in them."

### 13.2 Example 2, *Ma Wen's War*: a 30,000-Character Novella → a 24-Episode Family Melodrama (Zhang Wei p0119–0154)

- **The source's extent and genre**: Ye Zhaoyan's novella of the same name, **30,000 characters with 6 characters in all**, winner of the tenth *Xiaoshuo Yuebao* Baihua Award in 2003 (p0119). Why it could be adapted (p0120–0121, two reasons): ① "the story's distinctive 'one wife, two husbands' relationship is very rare, **with no precedent in Chinese television**"; ② "**constrained by length, the novel does not describe any character at length, which leaves ample room for a television adaptation.**" The series' figures: it premiered simultaneously on Beijing TV and in Shanghai in October 2008, averaged 2.46 in Shanghai and was the year's ratings champion; it was called "**a textbook of marriage**" (p0119–0120).
- **Thematic coordinates**: the source "confronts, through a story of adultery, the irresponsibility, selfishness and coldness with which some people face marriage and love… **but for television it is too sharp**"; the series "focuses on middle-aged marriage and love… **choosing a gentler angle on the theme, preserving the emotional relationships between the principal characters to the greatest possible extent while deleting sensitive elements such as the affair**" (p0122). The positive theme = "**cherish the one in front of you.**" **The dislocated sell-point formula** (p0124): "in past marriage dramas, most told of a couple going from harmony to rupture, and few told of a divorced couple reuniting… **so put the focus outside the besieged city, start from the emotional entanglements of a couple who have walked out through the wall, and show what life is like after divorce. From inside the city to outside it, examining marriage from an entirely new angle.**"
  - **The policy constraint (an industry item)**: "**in 2006 the broadcasting authority proposed to begin rectifying family melodrama, and dramas involving contested topics such as adultery would face certain restrictions on broadcast.**" (p0121) The three cuts made accordingly: everything involving adultery and "the third party" **deleted entirely**; Ma Wen and Yang Xin's divorce becomes the result of **young hot-headedness**; and Li Yi is reset as **an already divorced single man** (p0122).
- **Surgery on the principal characters**:
  - **A comparison of identity changes** (table 3, p0137, the original plus a "purpose of the change" column): Ma Wen, a temporary gatekeeper → **a software engineer** (raising the character's level); Yang Xin, laid off and unemployed → **a designer → PR → estate agency (salesperson → supervisor)** (each job change drives the plot and creates connections or conflicts with others); Li Yi, unspecified → **a designer** (creating an obstacle: colleagues cannot marry); Huang Xiaofen, running a small restaurant → **running a law firm** (lawsuits, prenups, gifts of property); Lin Zhifeng and Wang Dafei, **new → entrepreneurs** (enriching the class spread, creating conflict). Three reasons: ① "**characters and subjects that are too marginal rarely find resonance with the audience**"; ② the source was written around 2003 and the layoff wave "**would reduce the drama's contemporary relevance**"; ③ if the original setup were kept, the main setting might be concentrated in **a tenement**, whereas "**considered costume and settings make a drama more pleasing to watch.**" The functional definition of identity design (p0136): "**for family melodrama, the more complex the leads' identities and occupations, the more it can attract viewers of different levels and different tastes.**"
  - **The relationships go from 5 people to 11** (figure 9 → figure 10, p0137–0138): new are Ma Hu (the son), Sun Rong (Li Yi's ex-wife), Li Li (Sun Rong and Li Yi's daughter), Lin Zhifeng, Wang Dafei and Yang's mother; Huang Xiaofen's status changes from "Li Yi's friend" to "**Yang Xin's friend**"; and a "**primary-school classmate**" relation is added between Li Qin and Ma Wen. The verdict: "of this, 'adapting' is only a small part, **and 'composing' is much more.**"
  - **Material displacement** (p0122): "the source mentions that Li Qin once resisted marriage, and the drama gives that plot to another character, Ma Wen's blind date Huang Xiaofen. **This is a small but very important adjustment, making all three characters' personalities and emotional development more plausible.**"
  - **Three conclusions about character adaptation** (figure 12, p0151–0152): every character is "**extended out of Ma Wen and Yang Xin**"; every character must have "significance" for the plot ("**even next-generation roles like Ma Hu and Li Li affect the adults' emotional development**"); and "**the old have the obstinacy of the old and children the wilfulness of children, and every character should affect the others in their own distinctive way.**"
- **Structural handling**:
  - **Generating all the plot from one "obstacle" — the chain of "the flat"** (table 2, p0132–0133).
  - **The chain of scenes in episode 1** (figure 8, p0127–0130, 10 scenes): ① Yang Xin and Li Yi discuss marriage at the office and part unhappily; ② Ma Wen is at home on a date with a woman he met online, and she finds women's things in the flat; ③ Yang Xin comes home and walks in, introduces herself as the ex-wife and wrecks the date; their son Ma Hu comes home and says they both forgot the parents' meeting; ④ Li Yi discusses marriage with his sister Li Qin, who objects to Yang Xin moving in; ⑤ Li Yi agrees to marry, and Yang Xin wants to discuss with Ma Wen living together after the wedding; ⑥ Ma Wen goes to Yang's mother's to fix her computer, and she hints she hopes they will remarry; ⑦ Ma Wen goes home, and the two recall the divorce; Yang Xin tells him she is remarrying, and Ma Wen is shocked and objects to Li Yi moving in; ⑧ Ma Wen goes up to the roof to clear his head, Ma Hu thinks he is going to jump, Li Yi calls the police, and the crowd causes him to slip and fall; ⑨ Li Qin is unhappy and unwilling to let Li Yi marry Yang Xin; ⑩ Ma Wen is at home injured, and gives Yang Xin's mobile number to a reporter. **This episode accomplishes three things**: builds two sets of relationships; establishes the basic conflicts (the triangle, Li Qin's prejudice, Yang's mother's fondness for Ma Wen); and disposes of the unusable parts of the source (removing the peeping in the bath, removing every element of adultery).
  - **A central character who only comes into force later must show their face in episode 1** (p0130): Li Qin, "though of little use in the first half of the show, is crucial to the plot's development in the second half" — in the source she does not appear until Li Yi and Yang Xin marry.
  - **The logic of extent at the opening** (p0126–0127): "given that most stations air **two episodes** in prime time, a drama's broadcast lasts roughly **half a month or longer**, **and the audience usually decides after one or two episodes whether to keep watching.**" The model example, *Golden Marriage*: "though the first episode ends on a wedding, it leaves the audience a suspense: **how will two such different people hold hands through a whole life?**… **and in the event, *Golden Marriage* rated 8.78 on its opening day.**"
  - **The ending (open rather than a reunion)** (p0123): "both divorced couples come to regret their impulsive divorces, only to find that regret has 'come too late.' **Rather than simply reuniting the two couples, this slightly helpless open ending more easily provokes reflection in the audience.**"
- **Transferable techniques**: ① **when a novel's opening is unusable, say why** — the source opens with Ma Wen forcing his way in while Yang Xin bathes, and "this detail deftly sketches a warped set of relationships while establishing a darkly humorous tone for the whole novel," **but "it works against shaping 'Ma Wen,' the show's central character"** (p0127–0128); ② **add obstacles or misunderstandings to a character to give domestic trivia "significance"** (p0122); ③ an explicit warning against bandwagoning (p0124): "**dramas made by blindly following a trend rarely survive the test of time or of the audience: ratings open high and fall, or they sell without being liked.**"

### 13.3 Example 3, *Mei Lanfang*: a Real Celebrity's Life → a 40-Episode Period Drama (Zhang Wei p0155–0187) ⚠️ never produced

- **The source's (material's) extent and genre**: a real celebrity's life. The script was written by four writers, "**using the concept of a 'season,' with 10 episodes as one stage**," covering four stages: episodes 1–10, the young Mei Lanfang; 11–20, Mei Lanfang's association with the "Mei faction"; 21–30, "**the Mei–Meng romance**"; 31–40, Mei Lanfang during the war of resistance. **What this chapter analyses is the ten episodes on the Mei–Meng romance written by Zhang Wei** (the 1-x to 10-x references in the text are episode numbers inside those ten).
- ⚠️ **This version "was never put into production for various reasons" (entry 231 of the filmography, p0318).**
- **Thematic coordinates**: it takes the **third of three modes of adapting historical material** (p0157–0158) — "historical events and a character's fate tightly bound and driving each other, even to the point of the character acting on history," where "**though the outcome of the major historical events stays consistent with historical fact, the process is elaborated, enriched and made turbulent by the male lead's actions**"; the test: "**the balance point between fact and story is the key to whether the audience believes the plot and accepts the script.**" The producer's constraint (p0157): Huayi Brothers is a listed private company, "**which requires the adapter to consider ratings first**," and to "make the necessary choices and adaptations regarding plots that help the ratings but damage Mei Lanfang's image"; and the rights holders' constraint: "**not only the producers' and audience's preferences must be considered, but also the emotional needs of Mei Lanfang's descendants, and the legal disputes extreme disagreement might cause.**"
- **Surgery on the principal characters**:
  - **Technique one: merging and inventing a historical character — Yu Xiaolou** (p0158–0160).
  - **Technique two: supplying a historical figure with a new trait — "a love of talent"** (p0160, p0168–0170, the book's cleverest single piece of design). Three difficulties: ① these ten episodes cover his prime, and a stage of character is needed ("**finding the different stages of Mei Lanfang's character development is the measure by which to infer his behavioural logic in this period**"); ② **the question of gender register** — "his stage identity is feminized. **That mark of feminization very easily turns refinement and modesty into weakness in the audience's eyes**… so **a strong masculine trait must be found for him afresh**"; ③ the question of his marriage. The effect: "the writer distilled the quality of 'loving talent' and **set it above refinement and modesty**… by definition, it **flips wholesale** the part of Mei Lanfang's refined character that could be read as weakness **into the height of wisdom, of a wise man.**" **How the love of talent resolves the moral minefield of the Mei–Meng romance (the chain of inference, as written)**: "**because he loves talent, Mei Lanfang saves Meng Xiaodong when she is being harassed, and Meng Xiaodong becomes obsessed with him; because he loves talent, Mei Lanfang becomes her mentor on the stage and in life; and still more because he loves talent, when Meng Xiaodong is being forced into marriage Mei Lanfang tells the lie that he is already engaged to her, to save her.**… **This makes the audience overlook the elements of that relationship that violate their values.**"
  - **Technique five: no villain in the triangle — replacing good and evil with differences of position** (p0176–0181).
  - **Fu Zhifang goes from three words, "the virtuous wife," into a character with conflict** (p0161–0164): Zhu'er begs for help → Fu Zhifang takes it on with "I will pay" → she cannot withdraw the money → she takes off the betrothal jade bracelet Mei Lanfang gave her, intending to pawn it. The line: "**Tell me, how much did you take from them, to bury your daughter's whole life?**" The design note: "**as Mei Lanfang's wife, Fu Zhifang's characterization bears directly on Mei Lanfang's.**" "**The emotion is pushed layer by layer, until Fu Zhifang takes off the bracelet, and the suspense of whether Zhu'er can be saved is still unresolved.**" **Give the resolution to the protagonist**: Mei Lanfang comes home and withdraws the money to save her, "**making Mei Lanfang the one who resolves matters, and showing from the side his capacity as head of the household.**"
- **Structural handling**:
  - **Episode 1 opens on three lines advancing together** (figure 13, p0161–0165, as written): **the Fu Zhifang line** = Zhu'er begs her for help → she cannot get the money → she painfully pawns the bracelet → luckily her husband arrives in time and settles it; **the Mei Lanfang line** = doing everything to bring Yu Xiaolou back to the stage → celebrating Yu Xiaolou's return → going home → meeting Meng Xiaodong by chance and rescuing her from harassment; **the Meng Xiaodong line** = jeered on stage → publicly humiliated by Wang Renxi → asking her master for an advance to save a destitute mother and son. The point at which two lines converge: "**Fu and Meng help the same mother and son.**"
  - **Localizing the American ensemble method (the book's clearest single comparison of Chinese and foreign method, as written)**: "**this ensemble form, in which different characters hold independent action lines, appears mostly in American shows. In this drama the technique is localized and made more suitable for Chinese audiences: Mei Lanfang's action line remains the clear main line**, with Fu Zhifang and Meng Xiaodong's two supporting lines helping to unfold the characters quickly and convey the plot, and more importantly **supplying the show with other genre elements.**" → **A note for comparison: an American ensemble is several equally weighted A/B/C lines; this book advocates a Chinese treatment of one absolute main line plus two supporting lines whose function is "bringing genre elements in."**
  - **Technique three: compounding genres to solve the problem of a narrow audience** (p0164–0167). The narrow-audience diagnosis: "viewers who understand opera are few and far between… **this is less like making a television drama than making an opera programme.**" The compounding list: "besides family melodrama and the period legend, the part about Mei Lanfang growing a beard during the Japanese occupation adds the currently fashionable **spy-drama element.**" The general proposition: "**adaptation is not finished once you have found a genre similar to the source text; where necessary, compounding genres is the principal means by which an adaptation covers more of the audience.**"
- **Transferable techniques**:
  1. **A four-step method for adapting a celebrity** (p0182, usable directly as a checklist): ① collect real documents and material; ② **synthesize the material into a personal view** — "**collecting more material does not equal adapting better, and a mass of information easily misleads a writer. So in the early stages of reading, the adapter had better not choose television material while reading. Only after studying all the material and forming a clear personal judgement about the subject should you, on the basis of that judgement, establish the characters and then select the useful material.**"; ③ make reasonable inferences about missing plot — "**a character's truthfulness is built on the plausibility of their character, not on the truth of events.**"; ④ **select and discard material** — "**settled material need not all be used, and unofficial histories and rumours need not be discarded. Any material that can plausibly serve the character may be used in an adaptation.**"
  2. **The general rule: when a real person's existing traits cannot support the status of a male or female lead, distil a "higher-order virtue" from the historical record to set above them, and make that virtue the source of motive for every sensitive plot.**
  3. **Technique four: use the stage and the play-within-a-play to write a forbidden love** (p0172–0174). During a rehearsal, Mei Lanfang says to Meng Xiaodong: "Miss Meng, we are merely **a young couple chatting at home**; why play it at a pace that makes it sound like bickering?"
  4. **An invented plot must land on historical fact** (script 5-36/5-37, singing *Wang Baochuan* at home, p0182–0184): after Fu Zhifang finishes singing she laughs and says, "this Wang Baochuan is an unlucky woman… **she keeps watch in a cold cave for 18 years, and he comes back with a princess of Xiliang!**" "**All the women laugh; Mei Lanfang turns his wife's words over and is chilled to the bone.**" Three purposes: "**Mei Lanfang performed *Wang Baochuan* on his American tour, so using this piece lays in the later material; and the content of *Wang Baochuan* happens to fit Fu Zhifang's present situation exactly. Using the opera to allude to reality is therefore very deft.**"
  5. **A frank self-assessment** (p0182): adding "the love of talent" plus recombining three historical events is, "strictly speaking, a case of 'not respecting history.' **But this is exactly what constitutes the true screen image of Mei Lanfang in the audience's eyes, precisely because the writer held tight to Mei Lanfang's character traits, thinking through every event from Mei Lanfang's angle, doing what Mei Lanfang would do and thinking what Mei Lanfang would think.**"

### 13.4 Example 4, *The Myth*: a 90-Minute Film → a 50-Episode Time-Travel Drama (Zhang Wei p0188–0227)

- **The source's extent and genre**: the film *The Myth* (2005, Jackie Chan / Kim Hee-sun), which took **nearly 100 million yuan** — "in 2005 China's total box office was only 2 billion, and *The Myth* came second that year" (p0188). The series: financed by Shanghai Film and Emperor, premiering in CCTV-8's prime time in early 2010, "**a single episode broke 4 percentage points, and the show's average rating set a new record for CCTV's New Year flagship.**" **The commissioning judgement (a reusable "subject vacancy" logic)**: "by 2010… time travel should be a subject with considerable market potential and space, still awaiting cultivation"; plus the film's existing audience — "**adapting the film *The Myth* into a television drama meant gathering an audience numbering in the hundreds of millions before a word was written.**" And the industry preconception it broke (p0190): "there is an unwritten saying about television adaptation: **do not adapt works that have already succeeded.**"
- **Thematic coordinates**: one of the four general principles in the conclusion (p0226) — "**you cannot use a mechanical expansion; you must do 'deep processing' on the genres the television audience likes**, transplanting the drama's principal subject onto a genre within television's own range that the audience accepts better, while keeping the source's main framework from drifting." What is preserved at the character level (p0227): "**but its most essential thought, of loyalty to love and to country, is carried through**, and the principal character Yi Xiaochuan, having passed through a long stretch of time, gradually changes and finally grows into General Meng Yi of the film *The Myth*."
- **Surgery on the principal characters**:
  - **The three things that cannot be lost in adaptation** (p0192–0193): ① **the source's essence** ("the deep look at the cliff's edge," "Yushu's dance," "the antigravity heavenly soldiers in the palace" — "**the eyes, the actions and the shots are all replicated**"); ② **the main structure** (the ancient and modern lines converging in the heavenly palace at the end; "**change it and you injure the bones, and the whole sense of time travel is lost**"); ③ **important relationships and identities** ("characters are the vessels carrying emotion… **which is why the 'character biography' is generally born before the script**"). The balancing warning: "interpret the source too little and transcend it too little, and you will be accused of being a poor imitation; set the source aside and innovate too much, and it becomes subjective invention — going too far is as bad as not going far enough."
  - **Gao Yao → Zhao Gao, a reversal of temperament** (p0190–0191, from the synopsis): "**the originally timid Gao Yao is castrated into a eunuch and his temperament reverses**"; "if I am to be a eunuch, I will be the highest and greatest eunuch!"; he searches for the historically all-powerful "Zhao Gao" to attach himself to, "**only to discover that the real 'Zhao Gao' is himself.**"
  - **The character and relationship counts** (table 7, p0213); **two ways of complicating the relationships** (p0214–0215); **extending the time over which fates develop** (p0215–0216), with the formula: "the television drama's length ÷ the film's length = the drama's average character-time ÷ the film's average character-time."
- **Structural handling**:
  - **The passage-by-passage "copy / delete / adapt" comparison table** (table 4, p0191–0192) — cut into passages by the film's timecode and tagged. Excerpts: `0–10'10"` [ancient] Meng Yi receives the princess, the runaway horse rescue at the cliff → **[copy]**; `10'10"–26'30"` [modern] Jack and William visit the antigravity temple of Dasa → **[delete]** (the series keeps no Indian story, and all 4 Indian passages are deleted); `39'15"–48'25"` [ancient] Yushu saves Meng Yi at her own peril, dances and falls in love, and reluctantly enters the palace → **[adapt]** (adding **Meng Tian's secret love**; **Yushu's flying dance is replicated after she enters the palace**); `93'06"–98'08"` [ancient] Meng Yi dies in battle → **[copy]** (adding "**miraculously revived with the help of a divine physician and an elixir**"). **The methodological hint: cutting the source into passages by timecode and tagging each [copy] / [delete] / [adapt] is the single most directly applicable adaptation worksheet the book supplies.**
  - **The inversion of the costume/modern proportion** (figures 15 and 16, p0193–0194, the hardest set of data in the book): **costume material is only 40% of the film** (modern 60%); **costume material is 81.32% of the series** (modern about 19%). The characterization: "**the film *The Myth* is an action-fantasy comedy interspersed with costume material, and the television drama is a costume drama interspersed with modern shots. Fundamentally, the genres of the two stories' principal material are exactly reversed.**" Two reasons: ① **production cost** — "you cannot use too many special effects… **or, for want of suspense, it becomes an urban melodrama or family drama about modern people with nothing to do causing chaos**"; ② **viewing habit** — "**Chinese audiences have not yet formed the habit of watching modern fantasy or science fiction, which is why there is still nothing like *Battlestar Galactica*… while there are many costume genres, with many successful and mature types to draw on.**" → **A marked divergence from American methodology: American "high-concept" series often make the modern or science-fictional the main arena (*Lost*, *BSG*), and this book holds that a Chinese series must set its main arena in a time and space with mature existing genres.** And the industry effect is recorded (p0226): "**most time-travel dramas produced afterwards made costume material the principal subject. In that sense, the television version of *The Myth* contributed a great deal.**"
  - **The discipline of switching between timelines** (p0194–0198): in the film "neither line develops alone for more than 20 minutes" and "each passage is roughly as long as those adjacent to it"; in the series "there is no fixed rule for switching, and many consecutive episodes present only the costume material, **so a viewer who has not seen the earlier episodes could easily mistake it for a pure costume drama rather than a time-travel one.**" The stitching device must be the same object or the same place (the film uses the splash of water; the series uses the treasure box).
  - **The four-section table** (table 10, p0216–0219, the book's most directly applicable template for sectioning long-form):

    | Episodes | Main genre | Conflict |
    |---|---|---|
    | 2–8 | **Personal growth** | **The difficulty of getting home**: after time-travelling he meets Xiang Yu, survives a brush with death, and through a contest sets out to find his way home; he saves the Lü family and wins the two Lü daughters' affection, and introduces Liu Bang to Lü Zhi; he catches plague and Lü Su saves him at the cost of her own life; and finally he and Gao Yao learn it will take 60 years to return to the present |
    | 9–16 | **Romance** | **The difficulty of love**: sold out by Liu Bang; meeting Yushu at Tu'an and falling in love through a contest; Yushu is married off as an imperial consort, and Xiaochuan seizes her; meeting Meng Tian, who takes him for his lost brother Meng Yi; Yushu enters the palace and the two bury their feelings |
    | 17–41 | **Palace intrigue** | **The difficulty of court life**: Gao Yao realizes the ambition born of his humiliation, and uses Xiaoyue, who looks like Gao Lan, to draw Xiaochuan in; the factions of the two princes Fusu and Huhai form; Xiaochuan and Gao Yao each serve their own master and become mortal enemies |
    | 42–48 | **Historical war** | **The difficulty of winning an empire**: Xiang Yu's uprising; Xiaochuan returns from the dead and rallies Liu Bang; Chu and Han contend; Xiang Yu cannot match Liu Bang; the 60-year limit arrives |

    The conclusion (as written): "**the film's single three-act costume romance has been adapted into four relatively independent, genre-compounded television sections.**"
  - **The ending: the same outcome, a different close — closed vs. open** (table 6, p0206–0211); the theoretical distinction between suspense in film and in television (p0202–0204); and the expansion of dialogue (table 5, p0204–0206, **the only cross-medium dialogue comparison in the book**).
- **Transferable techniques**: ① **a defence of "vertical development"** (quoting Pamela Douglas's *Writing the TV Drama Series*, p0218): "**when a character is not developing horizontally toward their goal, you have to develop them vertically. That is, reveal their inner conflict, which likewise creates tension.**" The application: "**these singular horizontal motive forces cannot fill the plot, so the writers deliberately developed the characters vertically, and the palace intrigue is undoubtedly the genre most extended in this show.**" ② **The market data on compounded genres** (p0219): "**of the top three rated period dramas and family melodramas across twelve major regions in 2010, the great majority added light-comedy elements**… **the great majority of traditional-genre dramas have already become compounded genres, and the motive force of that compounding comes from the conflict and antagonism in the characters' lines of fate.**" ③ The general principle of selection (p0226): "if the source has no value, or no core worth adapting, **then adapting is superfluous and you may as well write an original.**"

### 13.5 Example 5, *Tie Lihua*: a 160,000-Character Novel → a 43-Episode Republican-Era Female Legend (Zhang Wei p0228–0272)

- **The source's extent and genre**: *Tie Lihua*, co-written by Yan Geling and her father Yan Dunxun (pen name Xiao Ma), **160,000 characters**, "**originally a screenplay her father wrote for his wife, which became a novel only after father and daughter revised it.**" The series aired in 2010 with an investment of over 30 million yuan; "from its April premiere, by November when it was halfway through, **a single episode had peaked above 12% and a single day reached 11.7%**" (p0228). ⚠️ The book's OCR is slightly inconsistent on episode count (the background says "34 episodes" while the sectioning gives "episodes 42 and 43"); this table follows the episode numbers in the sectioning.
- **Thematic coordinates**: **substitution of theme** (p0229–0240). The novel's theme (as written): "how a woman painfully struggles to build an identity… **when she sees the precious righteousness inside the dishonourable identity of a tomb robber, she can at last face herself.**" The series' theme (as written): "**so long as one holds fast to love, to loyalty to one's brothers and to filial devotion, then however many flaws one has, however much injustice one suffers, however many setbacks one endures, one can certainly save oneself, save others, and even save the whole nation.**" In summary: "if the novel exalts the **honour** shared with the tomb-robbing trade, the drama exalts the traditional **clan benevolence.**" The main line: "how Tie Lihua, who does not love her husband, takes **25 years** to discover, while fleeing him, her feelings for her husband Zhao Yuangeng, and then, having admitted that love in her heart, once again sets out on the road away."
  - **The second reason for the substitution (a technical rather than a censorship reason, and extremely important, as written)**: "**it does not serve the extremity of conflict**… **the original novel is more like Tie Lihua's one-woman show, a struggle between her and a 'general environment.' But once the theme concerns love, loyalty and filial devotion, a relational quality of mutual force is immediately established between characters — love is mutual and requires a concrete object, and that object is plainly more concrete and perceptible than a 'general environment,' which lays in the conflict's extremity very well.**"
  - **The overall judgement of the scale of adaptation** (p0228, as written): "from thematic setting, character setting, relationships, structural pace and plot direction, the drama makes **an almost complete break with the novel**, avoiding the tomb-robbing plot that would cause problems with censorship and replacing it with the genre styles of the mansion drama, bandit suppression, the war of resistance and espionage… **one story expressed through several styles, yet that mode of expression does not degenerate into showing off narrative technique and audiovisual spectacle: it holds tightly to the relationships and runs closely along their internal logic of family melodrama.**"
- **Surgery on the principal characters**:
  - **Four ways of "removing what the audience would dislike" about the protagonist** (p0243–0254): ① **remove the "psychic" element and turn the tomb-robber identity into a deception**; ② **add "the Liu family breaking off the engagement," turning her identity from a stain into a victimization**; ③ **two moves to defuse "killing her own son with her own hands"** (in the source Tie Lihua buries her son alive in his grandmother's grave); ④ **"make every object of the protagonist's affections unreliable"** (p0253, a very distinctive technique for gaining sympathy). Accompanied by the author's **self-criticism** (p0253–0254, a rare piece of self-diagnosis).
  - **Zhao Yuangeng: a four-stage appreciation curve of "hiding and showing"** (p0254–0261, the chapter's most copyable structural design). Functional position: in the environment of thematic expression he is **the enlightener**, and in the relationships he is **the judge**. **The difficulty of combining them (a theoretical warning, as written)**: "then this character has little room to grow and change, because if everything he does is right he has no shortcoming needing to be ground away through change and growth… **and once Tie Lihua's opponent becomes a flat character, the difficulty of her struggle with him drops… and the material between the two of them will not be watchable.**" **The solution (as written)**: "**if the character's own transformation cannot form a progressive growth, then use a progressive presentation externally… deploying the relation of 'hiding' and 'showing' with technique.**"
    - **Episodes 1–15 (× Tie Lihua, the mansion material)**: "**as the principal character setting off Tie Lihua, Zhao Yuangeng cannot be beyond redemption from the start. That would make his later transformation difficult, and once he has too many stains it inevitably affects Tie Lihua, who shares his values.**" The formula (the chapter's core technique, as written): "**every scene in which he must oppose Tie Lihua shows his negative side; and against every character who runs counter to the thematic values he also imposes extremely harsh punishment, with extreme obstinacy. And every other part shows Zhao Yuangeng's virtues — for instance his filial devotion to his mother, and his attitude of confining Qiu Xiang without harming her.**"
    - **Episodes 16–26 (× Liang Feihu, the bandit-suppression material)**: "**let him be obstinate on the thematic values and blacken him as far as possible on everything unrelated to the theme**" — "Zhao Yuangeng not only killed Liang Feihu's father but dug up his ancestral graves, **and his motive is plainly filial devotion, only carried to excess**"; calling in the artillery unit for private revenge under public pretext; and, after his eight-year-old son is poisoned, a spell of indiscriminate killing.
    - **Episodes 27–42 (× Pang Ya and Jing San, the war-of-resistance material)**: "**the brilliance of this character, suppressed and hidden until now, is at last fully released**" — rescuing Liang Feihu at the risk of his life without holding the past against him; the revenge on Jing San and the courage of his fighting; and the kindness and tenderness toward Qiu Xiang, who once betrayed him, so that "**Zhao Yuangeng suddenly turns from a selfish, obstinate warlord into a magnanimous, unconstrained and forthright national hero**," with his position undergoing "**a hundred-and-eighty-degree turn.**"
    - **Episodes 42–43 (the civil-war material)**: the closing section.
  - **Qiu Xiang: the "highlight character" who breaks through the genre's constraints** (p0261–0267); **Niu Dan, Zhang Ji'an, and "the necessity of flat characters"** (p0267–0270).
  - **Ensemble treatment and "small person makes good" motives** (p0242): "the novel puts its attention on Tie Lihua alone to foreground the theme, **while the drama presents it through an ensemble.**" Every motive stays inside the sphere of family ethics: "**rather, it is guided by ordinary clan-ethical emotional motives and demands of love and hate**" — Tie Lihua joins the resistance to avenge Liang Feihu and settle accounts with Zhao Yuangeng; Niu Dan joins the army to escape his "sister-in-law" Liu Feng'er, whose advances he rejected. The genre positioning: "**they are not 'heroes with stains' but 'ordinary people with a hero's spirit'**," which "very cleverly chimes with the protagonists of several war dramas that have been hits on screen in recent years, such as Li Yunlong in *Drawing Sword*, Chang Fa in *Wolfsbane* and Shunliu in *My Brother Is Called Shunliu*."
- **Structural handling**:
  - **The main line's four stages** (p0271, as written): ① episodes 1–15, Zhao Yuangeng and Tie Lihua establish a connection and become a bickering married couple; ② 16–26, Tie Lihua evades Zhao Yuangeng's pursuit and continues to flee her identity as the fifth wife; ③ 27–41, amid national and family enmity Tie Lihua draws closer to Zhao Yuangeng and takes up her family responsibility; ④ 42–43, Tie Lihua admits her love for Zhao Yuangeng and walks away. The symmetry of the ending (a very fine structural comment): "**structurally, it corresponds exactly to the first stage's 'together in appearance, apart in spirit': the ending is apart in appearance, together in spirit.**"
  - **The main-line / supporting-line section table** (p0230–0231, transcribed as written):

    | Part | Episodes | Style/genre | The main conflict | The supporting conflict |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | 1 | 1–15 | **Mansion drama** | Tie Lihua, whose heart is with Liu Tianci, seeking every way to escape the Zhao mansion | Inside the extended family headed by Zhao Yuangeng's mother, how the five wives compete for favour and for domestic power |
    | 2 | 16–26 | **Bandit suppression** | Xu Fengzhi (renamed "Tie Lihua" in this part), having escaped the Zhao mansion, surviving on Tiger Mountain while searching for Liu Tianci | How Zhao Yuangeng and his hereditary enemy Liang Feihu struggle against each other |
    | 3 | 27–42 | **The war of resistance** | How Tie Lihua opposes the camp represented by Pang Ya over the mandarin-duck pillow ("every character's dramatic task turns uniformly into resisting Japan") | Zhao Yuangeng's campaign against Japan, and how his relations with Liang Feihu, Niu Dan and others change |
    | 4 | 42–43 | **The civil war** | Through Tie Lihua as courier, the smokeless battle of wits between the PLA's Niu Dan and his warlord father Zhao Yuangeng | The deranged Zhang Ji'an is generously forgiven by his daughter and son-in-law |

    The structural relation (as written): "**the drama breaks the novel's narration with Tie Lihua as protagonist and viewpoint, and makes the changing two-person relationship between Tie Lihua and Zhao Yuangeng the main line, with the different conflicts of four different periods as supporting lines. The supporting lines frequently affect the main line's development, and the two mesh very tightly.**"
  - **Three layers of "packaging" a family melodrama in multiple genres** (p0270–0272, as written): "**in the internal logic of the storytelling it strictly follows the family-melodrama style that traditional mainstream audiences are used to; in structural form it uses several genres to package a family story; and in pace it uses dense plot and dramatic ups and downs to chime with the viewing habits of a new generation.**"
  - **A self-diagnosis of the cost (as written)**: "**call it a mansion drama and it plainly cannot match *Grand Mansion Gate*; call it a bandit-suppression drama and it plainly cannot match *Tracks in the Snowy Forest*; call it a war drama and it plainly cannot match *Drawing Sword*. For exactly that reason, which classic category this excellent drama belongs to has become an open question.**"
- **Transferable techniques** (this chapter supplies three general tools found nowhere else in the book):
  1. **The three elements of "the environment of thematic expression"** (p0232–0233): **the enlightener** ("**usually must not be the protagonist**… the protagonist often cannot accept or understand it at once, and must go through a series of events, grow, and only then realize / accept / transcend / reject the value the enlightener told them years before") / **the reward-and-punishment mechanism** ("the theme is right and just — but saying so proves nothing! There is only one way to make people believe it: **when we are punished for violating the values the theme sets and rewarded for following them**") / **a protagonist who does not stray** ("**for instance, if the discussion here is whether women can be independent while over there the protagonist's motive becomes restoring the nation, the audience will necessarily be confused**"). With a contextual rule attached: "in a war drama, delaying the fighting out of filial devotion will be taken as selfishness… yet the same thing in a family melodrama… **only by ordering the family can one govern the state and pacify the world.**"
  2. **The design method of relationships = interest relations × emotional relations in opposition** (p0240–0241): "**in conventional narration, if the relations of interest can be opposed to the relations of feeling, conflict is relatively easy to build.**" And it raises the problem of "**characters running parallel**" — "**if two people are alike in character from start to finish and react similarly to shared experiences, you get the phenomenon of 'characters running parallel,' the characters are poorly differentiated, and the audience finds it hard to tell them apart on a first viewing.**"
  3. **Three standards for arranging conflicts** (p0241–0242): **plausibility** ("**television connects events by causal relation**… **and generally the development of relationships matters far more than objective sequence**"; **figure 32, the nested-event model** — "**this whole event then becomes a 'cause,' becomes the beginning of a still larger event, and that larger event's development, turn and resolution continue**") / **emotional fit** ("which conflicts to emphasize and exaggerate depends on which aspect of a character you want them to express"; "**so the crucial conflict material is placed in Tie Lihua's dealings with Zhang Ji'an, because strong conflict must be placed on the development of the principal relationships**"; and it notes the opposite American practice — "**contemporary television, mostly American, frequently uses 'effect determinism' — pick the most watchable and most effective conflicts first, and then use characters and plot to make them plausible and affecting**") / **pace** (a string of self-questions: "**when the audience has been given positive emotion all along, when do you need to bring negative emotion, to keep their emotions varied and avoid emotional fatigue? At the tightest point in the narration, how do you slip lyricism in? How far can the lyricism go before it takes the audience out of the show?**").
  4. **A market judgement about the split between old and new audiences** (p0271–0272, the book's most forward-looking passage): "**audiences are no longer satisfied with a conventional, fixed-formula genre work; they have no sense of freshness about things they could recite backwards**"; "**and that leisurely narrative style and standard, settled since *Yearning*, continues to attract them**" (meaning older audiences); "**the different viewing habits of old and new audiences have produced the present gap in television's development, and how to balance the relation between two audiences with different tastes has become a problem every creator must face.**"

### 13.6 Example 6, *Drawing Sword*: a Novel → a 30-Episode Military Drama (Zhang Wei p0273–0308)

- **The source's extent and genre**: Du Liang's debut novel *Drawing Sword*, which "**pioneered an entirely new conception of the war novel — 'the market-style war story'**"; it spans from Li Yunlong commanding the Eighth Route Army's independent regiment against the Japanese in north-west Shanxi "through to his persecution during the Cultural Revolution, his tragic suicide, and his rehabilitation in 1980" (p0274). The series aired on CCTV-1 in 2005 (writers: Du Liang and Jiang Qitao; directors: Zhang Qian, Chen Jian): "**it became that year's ratings champion… in its first week *Drawing Sword* averaged 11.42, peaked at 13.7, and took a 28.71 share.**"
- **Thematic coordinates**: a passage of declamation from the novel is distilled into the show's theme — "**the drawing-sword spirit**" (p0276–0278). The declamation as written (spoken in the show at Li Yunlong's graduation-thesis defence, quoted twice in full in the book): "faced with a stronger opponent, knowing you cannot match him, you must still resolutely draw your sword; even if you fall, you must become a mountain, a ridge!… Even outnumbered, even surrounded, we dare to draw our sword, and we dare to fight to the last man. In a word, **when the narrow road meets, the brave one wins.** The drawing-sword spirit is the soul of our nation's army. Where the sword's edge points, nothing can stand." Three thematic operations: ① "raise and elevate the more positive side" of the novel's theme; ② **demote patriotism to second place** (a counter-intuitive move for a mainstream-message drama, as written) — "**demoting the patriotic theme of a mainstream-message subject to second place, and putting personal boldness and the 'drawing-sword spirit' first**"; ③ **avoid becoming a biography** — "**weakening the character's specific experiences and selecting only the crucial or pivotal and compelling events.**" The definition guarding against misreading: "'**drawing the sword**' refers to a spiritual realm, like a strategic guiding thought, and not to concrete tactical content. As Chairman Mao's famous formulation had it: despise the enemy strategically, take the enemy seriously tactically." Where the theme is cashed: the graduation thesis at the very end (p0278).
  - **The third strength inherited from the novel: writing Nationalist–Communist cooperation positively** (p0277, as written): "**but political divergence cannot efface their mutual admiration for each other's character, boldness and spiritual strength. Rather than negating a character wholesale on political grounds, it acknowledges a light of humanity that transcends politics.**"
- **Surgery on the principal characters**:
  - **The "lift the whole pot" strategy** (p0278, in contrast with the previous five cases, as written): "**apart from a few additions and subtractions, the leads — Li Yunlong, Chu Yunfei, Zhao Gang, Ding Wei and the rest — have no change at all in traits, background, fate or relationships. This 'lift the whole pot' strategy of character selection is like translating the roles sideways.**"
  - **A sociological explanation of the rough-hewn hero** (p0279, a valuable passage): "on the surface, **Li Yunlong's starting point of character lowers the threshold of social recognition and acceptance.** But in fact… **Li Yunlong's growth is the process by which the Chinese peasant understood the red revolution and the war**… they are not 'gentlemanly Pattons' but '**Chinese-peasant Pattons.**'" And it explicitly opposes the reverse approach: "**had it, out of concern for some 'overall image of senior cadres,' taken the opposite treatment, this drama would have lost at least half its appeal.**"
  - **An honest diagnosis of the absent character arc** (p0280): "but Li Yunlong is not like Jiang Bida in *Sky of History*, whose character is full of change; **his character is already fixed from his first appearance**… **which is connected to the show's omission of the stage in which his character grew.**"
  - **A trident of relationships** (p0284–0285, the chapter's most reusable structural diagram): Li Yunlong at the centre, with Zhao Gang and Chu Yunfei both as **counter-forces** — "this counter-force is not 'opposition' or 'antagonism' in the narrow sense, nor a matter of putting obstacles in Li Yunlong's way, but of interacting with him organically." **Four connection modes (as written)**: ① Zhao Gang — (**a working relationship**) — Li Yunlong — (**an enemy-friend relationship**) — Chu Yunfei; ② Zhao Gang — (**a conflict of temperament**) — Li Yunlong — (**a conflict of camps**) — Chu Yunfei; ③ Zhao Gang — (**mutual accommodation**) — Li Yunlong — (**growing familiarity**) — Chu Yunfei; ④ Zhao Gang — (**combat cooperation**) — Li Yunlong — (**military cooperation**) — Chu Yunfei. The use of the "imbalance": "Li Yunlong is supreme within his own unit… **so Li Yunlong's 'dynamism' naturally needs a character to provide a 'check.'**" Zhao Gang's break with genre: "**overturning the stereotype of the weak, scholarly political officer in our army.**" Chu Yunfei's position: "on Chinese television, **this is the first appearance of a positive Nationalist officer like Chu Yunfei**… and though their politics differ, the audience can accept the character," and he is characterized as **a witness.**
  - **Two added relationship scenes**: **the rewriting of Chu Yunfei's entrance** (episode 1, 1-7) uses "concern for an allied force" to close the distance first — Chu Yunfei: "**we ought to find a way to support them, even if only with some artillery cover.**" "**In the end it is China's national defence that suffers the loss.**" The commentary: "**the adaptation seems deliberately to blur the boundary between the two camps, so that from the very beginning the audience is emotionally closer to this Nationalist officer.**" **The pistol-gift scene** (episode 4, 4-9 and 4-16, not in the novel): Li Yunlong's "**a real man carrying a lady's pistol? If word got out it would cost me face.**"; Zhao Gang's "**I do not ask for a priceless jade, only a sword that kills.**" The commentary: "**this plot is woven in as a witness to their relationship, laying in their later cooperation and their later clash**… **and dispensing with the superficial cordiality and exaggeration of the teahouse meeting in the novel.**"
- **Structural handling**:
  - **What was cut and the complete reasoning** (p0291–0292, the frankest industry record in the book): "the drama actually adapted only the earlier content of the novel, **a capacity of about a third of the source, stopping abruptly at the 1955 conferral of ranks and the reform of military doctrine.**" The censorship reason (as written): "**it involves the Cultural Revolution and cross-strait relations**, and if so the broadcasting bureau's review would probably not pass it, and the drama would be stillborn. **Du Liang reportedly disagreed at first, hoping for a complete interpretation of the source.**… **This was cut not because it was untrue but because it was too true.** It gave a real sense of history, but it was too bitter, too raw, past the line. **For a production group that must think first about economic interest, that risk cannot be taken.**" The audience-emotion reason: "**the audience's mood would drop from a height… and if the marked contrast in style between the two halves were badly handled, some of the audience would be unwilling to accept it.**" But the author admits: "**the novel's second half really is better than the first; it is the essence.**"
  - **Two hard data points / counter-convention decisions** (p0292): ① "**the script arranges 18 battles, large and small, each one different**… and especially the cavalry engagement (not in the source), which **in script terms is Li Yunlong's only defeat and the most tragic battle of all.** To bring that out, the script uses the cavalry commander's three charging cries to express the drawing-sword spirit to the full"; ② "**to reinforce that feeling, the script even commits one of the taboos of television writing, minimizing the appearance of women: in the war years, only one female character appears in the first 19 episodes, and even she soon gives her life to the cruelty of the war.**" — **This is the only exception the book admits to the general rule that a romance line is mandatory, and it calls it "committing a taboo."**
  - **The four sections (using the 30-episode version, p0290–0297, as written)**: "**episodes 1–18 — the war of resistance; 19–21 — the civil war; 22–25 — Li Yunlong's marriage and romance; 26–30 — the military academy.**"
    - **One, the war of resistance (episodes 1–18).** Episode 1 "**does not convey Li Yunlong's backstory by narration but places him directly on the battlefield fighting the Sakada regiment, presenting his character through action in that dramatic scene**"; "**the strength of his opponent invisibly raises Li Yunlong's value**"; and he is demoted to running a uniform factory, "**a pattern of treatment that runs through the rest of Li Yunlong's military career.**" In episodes 2–12 "**Li Yunlong's Japanese opponents escalate in strength, from the Sakada regiment to the Yamazaki battalion, which pushes deep alone and inflicts heavy losses on our forces, and then to Yamamoto's elusive special unit**," with Yamamoto "appearing in episode 2 to form a suspense, **and not meeting Li Yunlong face to face until episode 6.**" **The double-climax structure** (episodes 13–14): "great joy meets great grief, but this is only the first section's small climax, and it builds energy for the great climax of the decisive battle at Ping'an in episode 14… **Li Yunlong chooses to sacrifice his own wife Xiuqin, and yet resolutely fights the battle that avenges his humiliation**… **and the drawing-sword spirit is released in concentrated form at the moment Li Yunlong shells the Ping'an gate tower.**"
    - **Two, the civil war (episodes 19–21).** The transition: "**but episode 15 already raises the curtain on the civil war**… closing the resistance section with Xiuqin's burial, and opening the prelude to civil war with Chu Yunfei's occupation of Dagu Town." The logic of the shifting relational centre of gravity (as written): "**once victory in the resistance is near and Nationalist–Communist conflict rises to become the principal conflict, the two men turn from friends into enemies and set aside their mutual regard for the sake of their political positions — and this shift in the centre of gravity of their relationship follows naturally.**" **The escalating sequence of "a needle in silk" (directly usable as a beat sheet for a friend-to-enemy turn)**: "**Chu Yunfei occupies Dagu Town — Li Yunlong drills his troops at Dagu Town and intercepts Chu Yunfei's supplies — Chu Yunfei seizes the district squad's weapons and lays on a Hongmen banquet — Li Yunlong attends the banquet with explosives strapped to his body.**" The control of extent: "the direct confrontation between Li Yunlong and Chu Yunfei **in fact occupies only about two and a half episodes**… **the structure carefully avoids dragging: each exchange is clean and effective, and Li Yunlong strikes to kill from the first move, every move lethal.**" And "Tian Yu gives blood to save Li Yunlong's life" launches the next section.
    - **Three, marriage and romance (episodes 22–25).** The functional position: "**Li Yunlong being transferred to a field hospital is not only the show's shift of setting from the front to the rear, but also a turning point in his life stage.**" Episode by episode: episode 22 "carries over and leads on… **closing out the show's real combat material**"; episode 23 is **the first obstacle** (the unit's leaders ask the hospital's Director Luo to act as matchmaker), with three functions — "**this state of affairs is in fact somewhat anti-heroic in conception**" / it gives Xiao Tian an opportunity to display her character / "**Li Yunlong becomes the listener to Xiao Tian's troubles**… **that is, not writing their emotional development head-on but writing it from the side, in reverse**"; episode 24 has the proposal accepted, with **the second obstacle** being Tian Moxuan's objection, and the principle (as written): "**you absolutely must not write it smoothly**; there must be no calm here, and the twists of the characters' emotional fate must be arranged like ranges of mountains"; episode 25 has the marriage permitted and serves as a transition, "**using Li Yunlong's request for a command being refused, so that he can only fight on paper, to convey his sense of loss after victory**," and the episode's question (as written): "**when a man born for war is absorbed into a system of daily trivia, how does he find his position, and can he draw his sword again? That is the question this episode raises, and the place where the audience finds resonance.**"
    - **Four, the military academy (episodes 26–30).** Two lines (as written): "**the main line is Li Yunlong gradually integrating into study at the military academy; the supporting line is his extramarital affair. Both lines have a complete structure and their own introduction, development, turn and resolution.**" The main line's beats: reporting late and being punished with sweeping the road (the introduction) → disrupting lessons and defying instructors (development) → correcting his attitude after the president's criticism (the turn) → the reading of the graduation thesis driving it to a climax → **joining the generals' formation in the National Day parade as a "glorious" resolution.** The supporting line's beats (as written): "the **beginning** is Li Yunlong visiting Zhang Bailu at Tian Yu's request; the **development** is Zhang Bailu falling in love with Li Yunlong and mounting wave after wave of advances until he cannot cope; the **reversal** is Tian Yu learning of it and telephoning Feng Nan; the **climax** is the frank conversation among Li Yunlong, Tian Yu and Zhang Bailu; and the **resolution** is Li Yunlong finally choosing Tian Yu and settling his family." Both lines point at "**return**" and "**finding the way back.**"
  - **The choice of the motive for the change (an explicit principle against "external coercion," p0306, as written)**: "**the writers did not design the motive for Li Yunlong's change and return as the authority of his superiors, or the constraint of public opinion, or correction by law and regulation — none of these superstructural hard frames can be the main motive force for Li Yunlong's finding his way back… the real cause and main motive force lies in the character's internal motive.**"
  - **The thematic scene at the end: the graduation-thesis defence** (p0307–0308). The divergence from the novel: "in chapter 24 of the novel, the description of the graduation theses **gives only a small part to Li Yunlong, and most of the text describes the uproar Ding Wei's thesis caused at the academy**… Li Yunlong is placed second and dispatched in a line." The series changed the thesis title to "On a Soldier's Will to Fight — the Drawing-Sword Spirit," and supplies the book's one example of a "thematic monologue" (script 30-12): "**a force with a fine tradition usually has the soil in which to grow heroes, and the appearance of heroes or outstanding soldiers usually takes a collective rather than an individual form.**"
- **Transferable techniques**: ① **the producer's "three foundations" = an IP assessment sheet** (Zhao Junkai, p0274): "**one is the theme, the untainted banner of the soldier; second, distinct characters; third, a brilliant story.**" ② **Reading a character's attributes out of one line of physical description in the novel** (p0283): the novel says Li Yunlong's head is large because he trained head-butting on stone tablets, and the book's analysis is that "**humour, self-mockery, banter, a fondness for exaggeration, and a refusal to follow the rules all become indispensable features of Li Yunlong in the novel and in the television adaptation. That inheritance of a single trait often implies future malleability and capacity for action, and is in fact the essence of a character's attributes.**" ③ **The right way to handle a policy cut**: not a head-on collision but **distilling a spiritual slogan that can run through the whole show** and moving the narrative's centre of gravity to the writable period, "not only preserving the essence of the characters but shortening the length and concentrating the narration" (p0275). ④ **A key turn carried by "one added display of capability"** (p0298–0302, the chapter's most technical passage).
